## Brief-derived frame

The system has one commercial kitchen, 20 businesses, and three coordinators. The required production schedule must account for the full period a tenant needs access, including setup and cleanup, and reserve the room and shared equipment so overlapping bookings cannot be confirmed. The $21,000 is the stated system budget, not evidence of total cost of ownership. A six-tenant pilot should preserve paper cleaning signoffs during transition and produce an export containing each tenant’s own annual records.

The stored data must not expose recipes, customers, or production volumes to other tenants. Some tenants handle major allergens, but the kitchen does not want the system to define or certify their food-safety programs. The record should therefore be a tenant-authored attestation about their equipment use and cleaning signoff, with enough context to identify the equipment, booking and signer. It should not quietly become a centrally prescribed allergen program, a finding that food is safe, or a conclusive record that cleaning was physically effective. The board’s two unresolved policy decisions—whether missing signoff blocks later bookings and whether cleaning steps are standardized across equipment—must remain decisions for the board, not product defaults.

The entrance tablet and coordinator’s screen-reader-compatible administrative view are acceptance conditions. The setup must work with the actual tablet under wet-hand conditions; a browser claim or minimum target-size criterion is not a wet-hand test. During the transition, the system and paper process need an explicit operating rule so neither is mistakenly treated as the complete record. No runtime, account, trial or sandbox was available or used for these product observations.

## Candidate approaches and primary-source findings

### A. Purpose-built shared-kitchen operations platform: The Food Corridor

This is an unfamiliar vertical product directly aimed at shared-use kitchens. It combines room calendars, equipment calendars, client-specific booking approval, billing, onboarding/document management, digital sign-in/out, reporting, and client communications. The product can reserve equipment for all or only part of a room booking. Its help center describes food-business users seeing approved bookings for a room and approved bookings for the whole kitchen in a Daily View; a tenant can request time and equipment, while a coordinator approves or declines unless that client is set to auto-approve. These are useful discovery points for equipment-specific conflicts, tenant self-service, a six-tenant pilot, and trusted-client rules. They are vendor documentation claims, not observed runtime behavior. [S06–S10](sources/index.md#food-corridor)

There are material unresolved fit checks. The public booking guide implies that tenants can see the kitchen’s approved schedule, but does not establish that tenant names, event labels, or other details can be hidden while showing occupied resources. The reports page describes kitchen-side CSV reports filterable by client and date, but that does not prove every tenant can download its own complete year-end archive; it also does not settle former/inactive tenant exports. Digital sign-in/out documents presence and duration, not a tenant-defined cleaning signoff. Public feature material mentions compliance documents, but does not document an equipment-linked, tenant-owned cleaning record that preserves each business’s procedure without representing the kitchen as a safety authority. Obtain a six-tenant demo using separate tenant accounts, inspect exact visibility, attach a sample signoff, then export a current and former tenant’s data. [S07, S10](sources/index.md#s07-food-corridor-tenant-booking)

The current public U.S. pricing page lists $129/month Starter for up to five active clients, $206/month Annual with unlimited clients, and $229/month Professional with unlimited clients. For 20 businesses the entry plan is ruled out by its stated client cap. The page lists a 4% platform fee on paid tiers, reduced to 2% when clients use bank transfer or manual payments; additional small-transaction charges may apply. The page says onboarding/account activation have no fee and describes a first month free for annual/professional. Those figures and conditions are mutable, and volume, payment mix, subscription period, taxes, fee payer and any needed add-on must be included in a written quote before judging the $21,000 cap. A low subscription headline alone is not the budget. [S06](sources/index.md#s06-food-corridor-pricing)

### B. Another vertical kitchen/community product: Spacebring

Spacebring’s current product page advertises room and equipment booking, member self-service, real-time availability, room-specific preparation time, scheduling and rental rules, and membership approval. Its broader approach bundles customer/member management, billing, access-control integrations, mobile apps, community and analytics. This is materially different from a scheduler-only system and may fit a kitchen that wants an integrated tenant portal. The public page does not establish whether “preparation time” is asymmetric, includes after-use cleanup, or blocks both the room and associated equipment. It also gives no precise tenant visibility matrix, cleaning-signoff data model, per-tenant annual export behavior, accessibility statement or price. Treat it as a shortlist/demo candidate, not a proven fit. Require it to demonstrate the specified six-tenant pilot and quote implementation plus ongoing fees within the budget. [S11](sources/index.md#s11-spacebring)

### C. General-purpose scheduler: Skedda

Skedda is a useful lower-domain-complexity alternative because its current support documentation exposes unusually specific mechanisms for resource buffers, approval rules, access visibility and data export. Its buffer rule can differ by space; the minimum unit is 15 minutes if platform time granularity is set accordingly. A buffer applies only to bookings created after the rule is set; conflicting rules on a space use the longest duration; a buffer appears both before and after a booking; adjacent buffers may overlap. Its example calls for a one-hour buffer where an operator wants 30 minutes to clean up and 30 minutes to set up. Regular users cannot book inside the buffer, but system users can override it. Thus a coordinator can create an exception that bypasses the safeguard, and existing future bookings need explicit migration/review. The feature fits a symmetric gap, but may be a poor fit if each resource needs different before/after durations or an interval of actual tenant access. [S01](sources/index.md#s01-skedda-buffer-time)

Booking approvals can be configured by space, tenant/user tag and weekday. A request remains pending rather than a confirmed calendar booking until a venue admin decides. The consequential limitation is that conflicting pending requests do not reserve against each other and multiple conflicts may be submitted; approval is a queue, not itself a concurrency lock. Admin role/space exceptions can also make approval rules inapplicable. Approval is plan-gated according to the help page. [S02](sources/index.md#s02-skedda-approvals)

Privacy can be configured rather than assumed: a venue can be private and require login, and booking access can be tag-scoped. With no visibility rules, regular users see date, time and space for other bookings but no user information. Rules can expose more details, and the most generous matching rule wins. System users always see holder, title and custom fields. An availability view can therefore reveal occupancy to support conflict avoidance without showing tenant identity/title, subject to testing every role/tag combination. But if exact occupancy itself reveals a tenant’s production schedule, the business decision must balance privacy against safe shared-resource allocation. [S03](sources/index.md#s03-skedda-access-and-visibility)

The export facility is a notable limitation for this brief. The vendor documents XLSX/CSV export from List view for System users, with filter fields and records that may include contact details, titles, prices, holder tags and custom fields. Regular users lack the described export interface and see their own bookings plus bookings made visible by the venue. The help page says export/print controls are absent on smaller devices, including tablets. This supports coordinator-generated extracts but does not establish tenant self-service annual export and makes the entrance tablet inappropriate as the export path. A sensitive export policy and a live tenant-specific export test are needed. [S04](sources/index.md#s04-skedda-export)

Skedda’s public pricing separates products and regions and charges by space count; plan features differ. Resource modeling (including whether each piece of equipment counts as a space), approval entitlement, history retention and total price must be checked against an actual venue quote. The published page is not a quote for this kitchen. [S05](sources/index.md#s05-skedda-pricing)

### D. Build the booking invariant separately from the calendar UI

A small custom booking core is a materially different approach if product demos cannot satisfy record ownership, privacy/export, or setup/cleanup rules. PostgreSQL 18 provides a way to store time ranges, including `tstzrange` for time-zone-aware timestamps, and a two-argument constructor uses half-open `[start,end)` intervals. An exclusion constraint combining `resource_id WITH =` and `access_range WITH &&` using GiST (with `btree_gist` for the scalar resource key) rejects overlapping intervals on the same resource. This would make adjacent boundaries legal while preventing overlap, and allow one room-access interval plus separate equipment-access intervals. The enforced range must cover setup and cleanup access, not merely production time. The constraint is a database mechanism, not an already-tested application; required extensions, status transitions, recurring expansion, cancellation/rescheduling, daylight-saving changes, and simultaneous requests still need design and tests. [S12](sources/index.md#s12-postgresql-range-types)

For privacy, Postgres 18 row-level security can restrict which rows tenant queries read and write, but RLS is not on by default. When enabled, no policy means deny; table owners, superusers and `BYPASSRLS` roles bypass ordinary policies unless appropriately configured. A tenant-scoped product would need explicit SELECT and write checks, a non-bypass application role, protected export paths, and negative tests proving tenant A cannot read or alter tenant B’s bookings/signoffs. RLS is defense in depth, not a turnkey tenant-isolation guarantee. [S13](sources/index.md#s13-postgresql-row-security)

A calendar component helps users see availability but should not own booking correctness. FullCalendar v7.1.1 documents `eventOverlap` as a drag/resize rule whose default is `true`. It can reject an interactive move, but it does not constrain API writes or two simultaneous server requests. A UI check should be followed by an atomic server/database write and a clear “this time was just taken” response. [S14](sources/index.md#s14-fullcalendar-event-overlap)

### E. Separate booking, cleaning attestation and food-safety judgment

A practical middle ground is to make the shared-kitchen system record only: resource, tenant, booking/access interval, tenant-selected equipment-use completion, signer identity, timestamp, and optional tenant-owned procedure/revision reference. If a status/checklist is included, the tenant supplies its wording and owns updates. Do not collect recipes, customers, batch volumes or ingredient/allergen details in cross-tenant calendar fields. The schedule can show only occupied/open state and any information the board authorizes. Provide an export scoped to one tenant and a data dictionary. Keep the paper signoff parallel during pilot; reconcile sample records rather than assuming internet availability makes paper obsolete.

The FDA’s 2026 Food Code page describes a model offered for adoption by governmental jurisdictions. The brief supplies no location or adopted local code; this research does not infer legal duties or decide which program governs. That uncertainty reinforces a narrow system role: it can retain a tenant’s own contemporaneous signoff without calling itself a food-safety program, grading cleaning against a universal standard, or asserting that a record proves effective allergen removal. The system may report “tenant submitted/not submitted,” not “safe/unsafe,” unless the board later defines and validates a narrower operational policy with appropriate advice. [S17](sources/index.md#s17-fda-food-code-2026)

## Issue/fix/release chain

A FullCalendar 5.8.0 user reported that several DayGridDay bookings stacked visually when `eventOrderStrict:true` (#6393, June 23, 2021). GitHub marked the rendering issue confirmed and assigned milestone v5.9.0; the tagged v5.9.0 release (commit `620efb5e9c023f229a22af31e10b8b810fa5b2ba`, July 28, 2021) includes a fix for that exact symptom. The chain is useful as a reminder that a calendar can misrepresent events and needs versioned UI acceptance tests. It is specifically a rendering bug, not evidence that the underlying reservation system admitted double bookings. Current v7.1.1 docs still make `eventOverlap` a client drag/resize setting with default `true`; therefore the correct mechanism for conflict prevention is authoritative server/database validation, not visual arrangement. [S14–S16, S19](sources/index.md#s14-fullcalendar-event-overlap)

## Initial synthesis and discriminating checks (all proposed, none run)

No candidate is yet selected. The best initial procurement comparison is The Food Corridor or Spacebring for domain coverage against Skedda for a rules/visibility/export-focused general scheduler. A small custom core is an alternative only if the board can fund implementation, maintenance, hosting, accessibility, and ongoing support within the $21,000 ceiling. All vendor feature claims need live verification. A possible scope boundary for the pilot is: calendar booking plus resource conflict prevention and tenant-authored records, while recipes, customers, production quantity, allergen-control procedures and safety verdicts stay out of the shared kitchen system.

Proposed acceptance checks, not executed:

1. **Resource conflict and access windows:** With six separate tenant accounts, submit the same room/equipment interval at once; exactly one confirmed reservation should persist. Repeat for same start/end, partially overlapping times, exact `[start,end)` adjacency, equipment used for only part of a room booking, recurring edits, coordinator override, cancellation, setup/cleanup boundaries, and a time-zone/DST transition. Verify that a pending approval cannot be mistaken for a confirmed slot, and that concurrent requests cannot both win.
2. **Setup and cleanup:** For representative rooms/equipment, specify before/after access and test earlier and later bookings, including a booking that existed before a rule change. Determine whether product buffer rules are symmetric and whether changes affect the calendar as users understand it. Confirm coordinators cannot silently defeat conflict checks without a visible audit/exception record.
3. **Tenant privacy:** Run as tenant A, tenant B and coordinator. Attempt schedule search, event detail, notification, API and export access. Tenant A must not see B’s recipes, customers or production volumes; verify whether names/titles can be hidden while keeping resource availability understandable. Check export fields and unexpected broad visibility rules. The requested policy must be decided before data fields are configured.
4. **Year-end records:** Export each pilot tenant’s data over a complete year-equivalent range, then test an inactive/former tenant. Confirm completeness, stable IDs, timestamps/time zones, resource names, signoff data, procedure-version references, status history, and a format that can be read without vendor software. Confirm another tenant’s rows do not appear. Establish who generates/receives each export and how it is handed over.
5. **Tenant-owned cleaning signoff:** Have each tenant author a distinct equipment signoff during the paper/digital overlap. Record only signer, time, equipment, booking and tenant-supplied procedure/version as agreed. Compare digital record to paper; document correction/late-entry behavior and the meaning of blank/missing. Until the board decides otherwise, missing signoff should be measured and surfaced without converting it into an automatic booking ban.
6. **Tablet and accessible admin:** On the actual entrance tablet, test navigation and booking lookup with wet hands, large touch targets, no mandatory drag gesture, legibility and session reset/privacy. W3C WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 uses a 24×24 CSS-pixel pointer target baseline subject to exceptions; use that for UI review but add the direct wet-hand test. For the coordinator, complete the full admin flow with the chosen screen reader and keyboard, including labels, errors, status changes, tables and export. Vendor documentation alone cannot prove accessibility. [S18](sources/index.md#s18-wcag-22)
7. **Transition and budget:** Run six tenants for a planned pilot period while paper remains authoritative or while a clear cutover/reconciliation rule is used. Simulate internet interruption and recovery. Track confirmed double-bookings, setup/cleanup collisions, unresolved booking requests, signoff completion, privacy defects, inaccessible tasks, export failures and coordinator time. Obtain a quote that totals licenses by resources/users, transaction/platform fees, payment processor costs, onboarding, integrations, hardware, implementation, accessibility remediation, support, tax and year-end export/storage. Do not infer costs from marketing averages.

**Executed:** public-page searches/opens and source reading described above. No product trial, code execution, benchmark, privacy penetration test, accessibility user test, budget quote, tenant interview or database witness was run. All validation items above are proposals only.
