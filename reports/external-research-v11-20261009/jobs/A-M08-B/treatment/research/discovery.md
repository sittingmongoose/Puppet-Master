## Decision frame

The brief describes about 2,000 hours of community oral histories, with volunteer transcripts, multilingual speakers, consent restrictions, staff corrections, retained source audio, time-linked excerpts, whole-recording and restricted-segment withdrawal, search, and a low-cost public site. The product is not a speech-recognition benchmark. It needs an archive workflow that keeps original recordings, searchable human-reviewed descriptions, speaker and word corrections, governed access, and public playback synchronized.

A promising first-pass architecture is separable rather than one-format-only:

1. Keep each received source recording unchanged in a controlled preservation store. Record a stable interview ID, original filename, source and technical metadata, consent authority, retention disposition, checksums, and preservation events. Generate a separately identified access derivative for streaming; do not overwrite a source file to create a redacted copy.
2. Make the searchable public layer from only text and media approved for that audience: item metadata, time-coded transcript segments, topic index segments, language/translation variants, and links into an approved playback object. Store speaker labels as editable person identifiers, not model guesses treated as identity.
3. Keep versioned, attributed transcript and index edits. A volunteer edit is a proposal/revision with reviewer and timestamp; the original transcription and prior corrections remain recoverable. Public corrections propagate to the synchronized version only after review.
4. Model access at the smallest unit the consent warrants. A record can be public while a file, transcript, index, or interval is restricted. A restriction must remove the affected interval from search results and exports as well as from playable audio; hiding a player control is not an access boundary.
5. Treat withdrawal as a controlled workflow: validate authority and scope, suppress the public item/search documents, invalidate direct access and derivative URLs, rebuild allowed transcripts/indexes/access audio, clear application/CDN caches, and record who decided what happened to the preservation copy. Preserve a sealed source copy only when the agreement and governing policy permit it. If consent requires deletion, deletion wins; retaining it as an “immutable” master would violate that decision. Copies already downloaded by the public cannot be recalled, so the project must not promise that they can.

This is an architectural recommendation to evaluate, not a claim that a product was deployed or tested. Exact legal authority, retention, community governance, expected costs, staff capacity, and supported languages are not supplied.

## Independently found product and workflow paths (O1)

### 1. OHMS: oral-history-specific authoring and synchronized discovery

The University of Kentucky's Nunn Center describes OHMS as connecting word-level searches to precise interview moments; its page points to an OHMS tool, a free account request, and the login. The OHMS Viewer is a separate viewer that can sit with a chosen media host or site. Its repository documents adapting the player with PHP and JavaScript and testing that transcript and index searches resolve to the same playback time. This is a direct fit for synchronized transcript/index access, but it adds hosting, integration, and deployment ownership. The repository's older README setup requirement is not safe to treat as the current system requirement without checking the chosen release and current PHP/runtime support. [S02, S03, S05]

OHMS is useful even where a full verbatim transcript is too costly or unreliable: a human-created topic index can expose major themes and time-linked excerpts without asserting a word-for-word record. OHMS's own release trail shows that the distinction between transcript, index, formats, and navigation matters operationally. [S02–S05]

### 2. OHMS inside Aviary: hosted, more integrated alternative

Aviary documents a hosted OHMS application and an editor that can import or create transcripts, revise text, replace recurring terms, edit speaker labels, and set timecodes. Its current transcript standard uses time-coded segments and can export common formats such as TXT and WebVTT; legacy transcripts have more limited editing until converted. Aviary also supports multiple transcript languages, timed indexes, role-based workflow, per-file privacy/status and access requests. This could reduce local operations and custom integration compared with self-hosting OHMS, especially if staff need editing and a viewer in one place. It is a commercial subscription path; no current quote or total storage, transfer, migration, or exit price was collected. [S06–S08]

The documentation exposes privacy/status for resources, media, transcripts, and indexes; it describes index segments within an index but documents visibility at the index level. It does not establish that arbitrary individual intervals within one transcript/index have independent access-control lists. Treat segment-level withdrawal as unproven until demonstrated against the vendor's exact tier and exports. A workable workaround might be distinct public and restricted transcript/index objects or generated redacted access media, but that can complicate searching, display, and synchronization. Validate rather than assume. [S07–S08]

### 3. Mukurtu CMS: community protocols and shared governance

Mukurtu v4 is a distinct fit where the represented community needs to define culturally appropriate access. Its user manual requires a cultural protocol for every content/media object, distinguishes public “open” from member-only “strict” protocols, and supports sharing across community protocols. An open descriptive item can carry a media asset under a stricter protocol, allowing discovery without public playback. Protocols represent community-defined rules and stewardship, not merely generic staff roles. This is a meaningful alternative when community governance is a first-class requirement. [S09–S10]

The documented control unit is the content/media asset. I found no official documentation establishing arbitrary transcript-word or time-range ACLs. A strict whole-file protocol cannot alone implement “hide this 47-second interval, allow the rest.” If Mukurtu is selected, segment restrictions require a tested composition/redaction design or a separate access layer; do not describe its media protocols as segment-level consent enforcement. Multiple-protocol matching also matters: the documented default “All” behavior is stricter than “Any.” [S09–S10]

### 4. Omeka S: general collection/exhibit catalog with object-level media visibility

Omeka S supplies a flexible web-publication system, shared item pool, metadata, sites/themes, and media. Its manual documents independent public/private status for item and attached media: an item may be public while attached media are private. Its six global user roles plus separate site roles can support staff and public access, but the manual describes object-level private-resource rules, not timecoded transcript editing or segment ACLs. It is a plausible low-cost/self-hosted catalog and public-site base if the institution can build or integrate the oral-history synchronization layer; it is not by itself the transcript/index workflow. [S11]

### 5. Speech recognition as an assistive draft, not the record

OpenAI Whisper is a public code/model path that supports transcription in the spoken language or speech translation to English. The model card describes 680,000 training hours across 98 non-English languages, but only 17% of the listed training hours are non-English speech paired with non-English transcripts, and explicitly says accuracy varies by language and accent/dialect and that hallucinated words can appear. The paper also finds multilingual performance correlated with training-data volume and records uneven results among evaluation sets. Neither overall headline accuracy nor an English result predicts this collection's WER. Do not suppress meaningful dialect, disfluency, code-switching, or community terms via aggressive normalization. [S12–S13]

Whisper's current release exposes word timestamps and prompting options in code, but timestamps do not make recognition correct or diarization reliable. The official model card says speaker diarization capabilities are not robustly evaluated and calls for evaluation in the intended context. Use an opt-in pilot with consent-cleared audio and native-language listeners; an ASR transcript is a marked draft, never an approved transcript. A strong operational alternative is to prioritize a short topic index for less-resourced language recordings while recruiting reviewers or language contributors for transcript repair. [S12–S14]

## Consequential defaults, formats, units, and applicability (O2)

- **OHMS navigation and integration:** the Viewer supports transcript/index time links, but substituting an external or hosted player requires player-specific adaptation and verification that searches and time links still align. The v3.10.15 release provides sample Apache/Nginx rules to block direct web access to cache files while allowing the application process to access them. This is evidence of a practical deployment boundary: cache folders and transcript/index XML must not be assumed private merely because the application hides a link. [S03–S05]
- **Aviary editing semantics:** in its newer OHMS Aviary transcript standard, each segment/paragraph has a timestamp; the built-in editor can edit text, speaker, and start time, and legacy imports need conversion for full edit/sync capability. The documented manual time unit is HH:MM:SS. Its WebVTT import format encodes hours, minutes, seconds, and milliseconds; make one canonical internal time representation and test conversions, rather than mixing integer seconds, milliseconds, and formatted strings. [S06]
- **Privacy objects:** Aviary describes distinct statuses for resource, media, transcript, and index; Omeka S for item and media; Mukurtu v4 for content and media assigned protocols. None of these reviewed pages prove interval-level access controls across byte-range media playback, transcript text, topic index, search results, API/export, caches, and derivatives. The safe assumption for design is that interval-level withdrawal needs deliberate redacted derivatives or application logic with authorization checks at every access path. [S07–S11]
- **Preservation audio:** the Library of Congress identifies WAVE/BWF with LPCM as preferred for media-independent digital audio and says BWF is its archival master for reformatting mono/stereo analog audio. For already-digital originals, retain their received bits; do not “upgrade” by resampling. Make web access copies separately. The appropriate master bit depth/sample rate is a source-quality and local policy decision. [S16]
- **BagIt and PREMIS:** BagIt RFC 8493 v1.0 describes a package with a data payload and checksum manifest(s); each payload file is listed once per manifest, and validation means the checksums verify. That supports controlled intake/transfer and fixity, not an access-control or backup service. PREMIS 3.0 separates Object, Event, Rights, and Agent entities, which is a useful design vocabulary for linking source and access files, transformation/fixity/deletion events, governing rights, and people/software. PREMIS is metadata, not a storage system. [S17–S18]
- **Storage scale estimate (calculation, not observed inventory):** 2,000 hours of 96 kHz, 24-bit, stereo PCM is 4,608,000 bits/s × 3,600 s ÷ 8 = 2,073,600,000 bytes per hour, or about 4.15 decimal TB for one copy. At 48 kHz/24-bit/stereo the estimate is about 2.07 TB for one copy. A 64 kbit/s access copy is about 28.8 MB/hour or 57.6 decimal GB total. These figures exclude container overhead, preservation metadata, other formats, filesystem overhead, replication, backup history, and future growth; retain the original source if it is already compressed rather than expanding it. One-hour interviews imply 2,000 interview records and 83.3 days of audio, but no ingestion or search benchmark was run.
- **Rights and care:** OHA's 2025 principles say access planning starts before an interview and advise documenting informed consent and giving narrators a chance to review/approve before public release where possible. OHA's archive manual calls for explicit access and restriction policies; its glossary distinguishes ongoing/rolling consent from copyright. Build an auditable consent/rights record before making an interview searchable, and do not make blanket promises about control after public release. [S15]

## Product comparison and preliminary recommendation

| Path | Strongest fit | Constraint / condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-hosted OHMS Application + Viewer with local preservation store and a separate search/catalog layer | Oral-history indexing and searchable time-linked transcripts, source-audio flexibility, open integration | Local hosting, hardening, media-player compatibility, transcript editing workflow and security maintenance must be staffed; confirm current runtime requirements and license obligations. |
| OHMS within hosted Aviary | Integrated transcript/index authoring, synchronization, reviewer roles, exports and managed playback | Commercial quote/TCO and exit/portability review required; demonstrate interval-level restriction and deletion across text, media, caches and exports. |
| Mukurtu v4 plus an oral-history layer | Community-owned access protocols, community roles, shared protocol governance | Object/media protocols are not documented as arbitrary segment policies; hosting and language/transcript tools still need a fit check. |
| Omeka S plus OHMS or a custom synchronized component | Flexible public catalog/exhibits and self-managed item/media visibility | General-purpose framework; synchronized oral-history editing and segment-level consent are separate integration work. |
| Human topic indexes plus selective human-checked ASR drafts | Makes 2,000 hours discoverable when full transcription is unaffordable or ASR is weak in a language | Less complete than verbatim text search; indexing is interpretive and must reflect community terminology and context. |

For the small brief, I would pilot two routes on the same consent-cleared interview: (A) self-hosted/open OHMS plus a public catalog and separate preservation store, and (B) Aviary OHMS as managed authoring/hosting. If community protocols govern access, add a Mukurtu fit trial rather than treating it as an interchangeable generic CMS. Keep a schema/export boundary (OHMS XML where applicable, transcript WebVTT/TXT, index CSV/WebVTT, stable IDs and rights metadata) so pilot learnings do not trap the archive in one vendor. Select only after comparing staff hours, hosting/storage, media bandwidth, annual subscription, security updates, accessibility, and export round-trip.

## Proposed discriminating validations (not executed)

1. On a consent-cleared 30–60 minute sample, with permission, run manual human transcript, an ASR draft, and human topic index in one selected language; compare reviewer time, deletion/repair count, named-entity/dialect/code-switch error types, WER/CER with a documented normalization rule, and index coverage. Repeat with at least one low-resource language and one noisy/accented sample before accepting ASR.
2. Invite community-approved staff/volunteers to correct words, rename speakers, and adjust times in each candidate; verify reviewer attribution, revision history, and round-trip export/import preserves timestamps, speaker identity IDs, language, punctuation, and annotations.
3. Apply a test restriction to one known interval and search for its exact word, topic, speaker, transcript, index and excerpt. Verify public search/API/export does not reveal it; direct audio URL, range requests, media player seek, captions, cached copies, feed/embeds and derivative downloads do not expose it. Then withdraw the interval, invalidate cached paths, and verify unaffected allowed content still plays at correct offsets.
4. Withdraw one whole-recording test item. Verify public page, search index, OAI/API/RSS/search exports, direct media URLs, transcripts, indexes, embeddings/previews and CDN links disappear; verify the repository records approval, requested disposition, purge/embargo events, and any permitted retained master. Confirm the exact deletion/retention promise against the consent agreement.
5. Package a test intake with BagIt; validate required payload manifest entries, detect one-bit alteration, restore a test backup, and record checksum/format-identification/normalization events with PREMIS or a minimal equivalent. Test storage and egress estimates with actual audio formats and chosen replication policy.
6. For Aviary, request a written tier-specific quote and an export/exit sample; demonstrate how one interval's transcript/index/stream is restricted and withdrawn, how exports and caches behave, and whether the public website can use the existing domain/theme. For self-hosted OHMS, test current supported PHP/web-server dependencies, direct cache/XML denial, viewer integration and accessibility of keyboard/audio controls.
