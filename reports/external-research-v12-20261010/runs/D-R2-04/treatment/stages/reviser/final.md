## Proposal

Before any notice revision or deployment, establish the actual fee and enrollment rules, recover the current notice and its print/mobile paths, and learn whether people understand those rules from the notice itself. The reported questions could arise from missing or crowded information, translation or source-text changes, channel presentation, different reading needs, staff explanations, or uncertainty in the underlying service process. The supplied packet does not identify a cause.

Once the owner approves one process map, compare the current authorized notice with (B) an information-structure or wording alternative that preserves exactly the same rules and (C) the same words in an alternate presentation/channel. Keep (A) the current notice as the anchor when it can be recovered. Offer (D) a consistent, approved staff explanation only after recording each participant’s independent first answer. These alternatives answer different questions; do not combine them into one “improved” version.

A later formative study should separately measure fee comprehension, reservation-status comprehension, task completion, and reader confidence. It should include people who may actually use the courses, with language preference, reading comfort, mobile context, and access needs considered within each language group. Neither fewer staff questions nor a readability score establishes comprehension or accessibility. Policy details, the language pair, audience distribution, current materials, access routes, recruitment budget, and the organization’s priority remain owner decisions.

## Plausible mechanisms to investigate, not findings

1. **Compression and information order.** Shortening may have removed a qualifier, example, materials cost, payment condition, or step in a process. Fee inclusion and reservation status are separate questions; placing price, materials, and payment timing far apart could make a reader miss a relationship. A form-submission step without its actual conditions could be read as a confirmed reservation. These are hypotheses only. Test them against the actual notice and owner-approved rules.

2. **Translation and source-text changes.** Shortening and translation revision happened together. This confounds possible changes in source content, wording, clause order, terminology, length, and visual balance. The language pair and texts are unknown. Compare each actual version against the same owner-approved facts; use qualified language review and later participant testing rather than assuming equivalence or deficit.

3. **Presentation and channel.** A mobile preview may omit a qualifier or hide it behind a link. A linked page may wrap, reorder, or separate the two languages. Print and mobile can expose different text or reading sequences. Inspect the actual journey before attributing a problem to channel. If a web destination is in scope, WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.4, 1.4.5, 1.4.10, 3.1.1, and 3.1.2 suggest bounded technical desk checks for text resizing, text versus image-of-text use, reflow, and page/part language identification. They do not measure comprehension and do not establish conformance or legal duties here (E05).

4. **Variation among readers.** “Bilingual” does not describe how each person reads or speaks either language. People may vary in preferred reading language, literacy, confidence, course-registration familiarity, mobile use, vision, and assistive-technology use. Ask about task-relevant needs and preferred mode; do not substitute ethnicity, nationality, or a broad language label for them. Analyze findings by language/version and context where the actual audience supports it.

5. **Staff support and process consistency.** Staff may be clarifying a real policy gap, translating informally, or repeating a stable process. Record an unassisted answer first, then offer a consistent, owner-approved explanation and note whether the response changes. Map scripts, training, and contact routes. Fewer questions alone are not proof of understanding.

These mechanisms can coexist. A later study should distinguish the notice’s effect from policy uncertainty and assistance, rather than presuming a translation or literacy problem.

## Alternatives worth preserving for a later comparison

Hold the owner-approved policy facts constant in every condition.

- **A — Current notice, unchanged.** Recover and use the actual authorized notice and its actual route. This is the anchor. If no current notice or route can be recovered, mark A unavailable; do not fabricate a baseline.
- **B — Content or information-structure alternative.** In the same medium, test an owner-approved arrangement that groups the exact course price, materials inclusion or separate cost, and payment timing, then states what submission does and does not guarantee. Preserve every rule and exception. It is a research prototype, not publishable copy.
- **C — Presentation or channel alternative.** Keep A’s words exactly the same while testing a realistic alternate layout or route. If the route is a short message and destination page, test preview and destination as one journey. A text-based, reflowing web page is one possible digital condition. Do not change wording in this condition.
- **D — Assisted route.** After collecting the participant’s unassisted answer, offer a standardized, approved explanation in the participant’s chosen language, with qualified language support where needed. Record what changes. This tests support; it does not replace the notice.
- **Policy clarification before comparison.** If the rules themselves are absent or inconsistent, return the question to the owner. This is a prerequisite and owner decision, not a participant-led policy change.

Possible bilingual digital arrangements include a selected-language page, a language-navigation pattern, or another system-supported layout. GOV.UK explicitly advises its own publishers not to put translated text on the same page as English because of assistive-technology problems; that is a GOV.UK system instruction, not evidence that every website, mobile message, or printed bilingual notice must use separate pages (E04). Keep compatible options open and test the actual implementation. No universal layout or translation method is selected.

If the budget cannot support all conditions or all language/access cells, name what is untested. Do not merge wording, translation, layout, channel, and assistance changes into a single winner condition.

## Proposed later study

### Gate and sequence

**Gate 0 — Recover and approve facts.** Obtain the current and prior notices if available, all language/channel variants, source text and review history, change dates, exact fee/materials rules, enrollment status sequence, exceptions, mobile preview/destination, staff scripts/training, and any lawful aggregate question categories. Ask the accountable owner to approve the process map. Identify the actual language pair and variants, likely user routes, access needs, local ethics/privacy requirements, recruitment route, and budget. If a fact is unknown or inconsistent, pause the comparison and return it to the owner.

**Phase 1 — Prepare frozen conditions.** Freeze source wording and policy facts. For each supported language and actual delivery route, prepare A, B, and C only where source materials and qualified language review make a fair comparison possible. B may change wording or order but not rules. C changes presentation/channel while retaining A’s wording. Do not silently translate a missing version. If a language/route cell cannot be prepared, call it unavailable. Create a D explanation only from the approved process map and train the staff member or interpreter to deliver it consistently.

**Phase 2 — Run formative sessions.** Use neutral, moderated, one-to-one sessions in an accessible setting or remote route selected for participant access and privacy. Use fictional course scenarios based on approved facts; do not submit a real form or collect a participant’s account, enrollment details, or financial information. Give each participant one primary first-exposure condition to reduce learning effects. Where feasible, balance or randomize condition within language and relevant access profile. Capture independent answers first; then offer D and record any change. If later sessions compare multiple conditions within one participant, use parallel scenarios, counterbalance the order, and disclose the learning limitation.

### Participants, sample, and access

Once language and service-area evidence is available, recruit adults who are actual or plausible course users through voluntary, owner-approved outreach and relevant community partners. If youth are actual users, add safeguarding and consent arrangements before including them. Do not treat a language group as uniform. Within each materially used written language, seek variation in self-described reading and speaking comfort, preferred language, literacy/reading confidence, prior enrollment familiarity, mobile access, visual/access needs, and use of zoom or screen readers where those routes are in scope. Ask participants which language and mode they prefer; do not infer needs from nationality or ethnicity.

As an initial planning heuristic only, the draft proposes 12–18 people per materially used written language across two short waves, aiming for 4–6 independent first exposures per A/B/C condition in each language before making a comparative judgment. This is not statistically justified, powered, representative, or evidence-derived. Confirm or revise it after learning the number of languages, recruitment budget, access needs, condition availability, and intended decision. If several important languages cannot all be covered, prioritize using service-area evidence and state explicitly which cells remain untested. Treat small purposive samples as issue-finding, not population estimates.

Use realistic phone sizes, actual message previews, the full linked journey, and realistic connectivity states if mobile delivery is part of the service. Let participants use their own assistive technology where feasible. Offer remote, in-person, or supported options according to participant preference, privacy, and access.

### Recruitment, consent, and privacy

Before outreach, obtain local privacy/ethics review and owner authorization. Prepare invitation, information sheet, consent, task, and follow-up materials in the identified languages and accessible formats, with qualified translation and independent review. Offer oral explanation, interpretation or other language support, time for questions, and extra time as needed. Explain the study purpose, procedures, data collected and use, observers and recording, retention, reporting, voluntariness, withdrawal, and a contact/complaint route. Make a non-recorded option available if structured notes are sufficient.

Collect only information needed to interpret language, device, access, and task outcomes. Prefer coded notes; do not ask about actual fee hardship, account data, or registration history that is not needed for the task. Keep participation separate from eligibility or course access. Do not publish identifiable quotations or participant-level results. Consent and research safeguards are proposals, not approvals already obtained. Local law, privacy advice, and ethics requirements remain to be determined.

### Tasks and separate outcomes

Use neutral prompts that do not name the answer or point to a location. For example, ask someone considering a fictional course to explain what they would expect after submitting the described form and what they would do next. Record the initial answer and the participant’s cited evidence before asking a neutral follow-up such as “What in the notice led you to that?” Score against the owner-approved fact map.

1. **Fee comprehension:** course charge; whether materials are included or separately charged; any amount and timing stated by policy. Accept “not stated” or “not sure” when the approved information does not support a determinate answer.
2. **Reservation-status comprehension:** status after the described submission; whether a place is held, reserved, or confirmed at that step; and any actual next action, response window, or condition. Do not infer that receipt means reservation.
3. **Task completion:** whether the person can locate/report the necessary facts and take the stated next step without moderator help. Record completion, time, lookup location, errors, and requested assistance separately from the comprehension responses.
4. **Reader confidence:** ask for a confidence rating and optional reason after each answer. Analyze confidence separately from correctness; confidence in an incorrect answer warrants investigation. Confidence or fewer staff questions is not a proxy for correct understanding.

Predefine error categories such as omitted materials fee, wrong amount or timing, form submission mistaken for reservation/confirmation, missed condition, preview/link not followed, and answer unsupported because the policy does not state it. Record condition, language/version, device/viewport, task-relevant reading/access needs, and help given. Report denominators and missing values; compare outcomes by condition and language where numbers permit rather than relying on a pooled average.

### Revision decision

Before seeing results, the owner and research lead should define the required policy facts and what counts as a critical error. Compare each separate outcome by condition, language, and relevant access context. Consider a prototype only if it preserves approved policy, supports correct independent answers and task completion, and does not introduce a critical error or conceal an access barrier. Use confidence to find certainty/correctness mismatches. Keep an assisted route if it helps and the service can sustain it.

A lower staff-question count is a separate organizational outcome. Consider tracking it later only through lawful aggregate records and if the owner chooses it as a priority. Do not treat fewer questions as proof of better understanding. If a participant cannot answer because policy is absent or contradictory, return the matter to the owner and pause notice revisions. Any later selected version still needs language review, a new participant check after material changes, media-appropriate accessibility review, and separate publication/deployment approval.

## Critique and SCW-6 adjudication

Critic labels and its diagnostic C* statements were treated as hypotheses. Each selected record was checked against the governing primary source; the evidence establishes only the stated scope.

### SCW-1 — RMIT preference results and transfer

**Disposition: accept intended claim C; reject the broader diagnostic C*.** C is that the report’s result varied by document. The RMIT results table and discussion (report pp. 15–18; web reader pages 16–18) support Methods 3 and 4 being preferred for Documents A, B, and C, while Document D had a different pattern: Method 1 led, Method 2 was second, and Method 3 received the most confusion votes. Respondents cited font, format, and wording for the confusing Method 3 version. The project page identifies the Commonwealth Department of Health and Aged Care commission and the health-communication study frame. C* would extend Methods 3 and 4 as preferred methods to multilingual notices generally, including course enrollment; the report does not support that extension. Its perceived ease/preference/confusion outcomes do not measure this center’s fee/status comprehension or task completion. No translation method is selected.

### SCW-2 — GOV.UK translated-page guidance

**Disposition: accept intended claim C as a scoped option; reject diagnostic C* as a universal rule.** The current GOV.UK publishing page says to decide on translation using policy and user-need evidence and instructs GOV.UK publishers not to put translated text on the same page as English because this creates problems for assistive-technology users (lines 29–39). This supports retaining separate-language pages as an option to evaluate in a compatible system. C* would require separate pages for every web system; GOV.UK guidance does not establish that default or prove other layouts should be used. Its directions concern GOV.UK content types and publishing responsibilities, not every mobile message or paper notice. The actual system and users must decide.

### SCW-3 — RMIT recruitment route

**Disposition: accept intended claim C for the inspected methods; retain uncertainty about diagnostic C* for the whole report.** The report’s methods section (pp. 10–13; web reader pages 11–13) says recruitment aimed for diversity across each language group and preferably readers unable to speak English, and reports three readers per language group recruited from different states. Those pages do not name the recruitment channel. I searched the full PDF text for recruitment/recruited and likely channel terms; this did not yield a documented route, but text search cannot prove global absence. C* would assert that the whole report never names the route; that broader negative claim remains uncertain. The final wording therefore remains limited to the inspected methods.
