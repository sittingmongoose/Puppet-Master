# Independent discovery — D-R2-04-treatment investigator

## Scope and evidence boundary

This discovery is based on the assigned brief alone plus the public primary-source records in the adjacent source map. The brief describes a fictional community-center notice. No actual notice, service policy, old version, translation, enrollment form, response log, staff script, participant interview, language pair, audience distribution, assistive-technology profile, or recruitment budget was supplied. I therefore do not identify a cause, assign an error rate, state which translation is equivalent, or propose final wording.

No participant contact or recruitment, notice publication, form deployment, center contact, translation, or user validation was performed. Public examples below are comparators, not evidence about the center.

## Facts and unresolved service questions

The only reported history is that staff hear questions about whether materials fees are included and whether submitting the form reserves a place; the notice was shortened at the same time its translation was revised; and some distribution moved to mobile messages. There are no logs, interviews, or stable earlier notices. The temporal coincidence does not show whether shortening, translation, presentation, channel, policy, or their interaction caused the questions. We cannot tell whether the current notice says the same thing in both language versions or across print and mobile channels.

Before content changes, the service owner must document and approve: which costs are part of the course price; whether materials are included or separately charged; amounts and due dates; what happens immediately after a form is submitted; whether capacity is checked before or after submission; what a receipt/confirmation means; when a place becomes reserved or confirmed; what can prevent confirmation; and what action, response window, or contact route follows. Fee inclusion and enrollment status are distinct policy questions. Evaluate participant answers against that approved process map; do not use them to decide policy.

## Plausible mechanisms to investigate, not findings

1. **Compression and information structure.** Shortening may have removed a qualifier, example, fee component, or process step, or placed two independent questions close together. A reader may find the fee but miss whether it includes supplies, or see a submission action without the conditions that distinguish received, complete, offered, reserved, and confirmed. The CDC checklist suggests testing a main message, information order, audience-familiar terms, headings/chunks, and numbers; these are desk-review prompts, not predicted effects here (E01).

2. **Translation and content changes are confounded.** Shortening happened alongside translation revision, so any later difference cannot be attributed to language alone. Translation may alter sentence length, clause order, conventional terms, or visual balance, but the language pair and text are unknown. Assess each actual version against the same owner-approved facts, with language-specific review and participant testing. CDC advises separate assessment when translated versions address different audiences; DfE notes literacy varies within first-language groups (E01, E06).

3. **Channel and viewing context.** A mobile message may show only a preview or move conditions behind a link; a mobile page may wrap, resize, reorder, or separate the two languages. These are hypotheses to inspect on the real distribution path. If a web destination is used, WCAG 2.2 supplies proposed checks for reflow, text rather than image-only text, and programmatic language identification (E05). GOV.UK’s separate-language-page advice is specific to its publishing system and screen-reader concerns, so compare it as one digital option rather than a universal bilingual-layout rule (E04).

4. **Audience differences.** “Bilingual” is not a proficiency profile. People may differ in reading and speaking skill in each language, literacy in their preferred language, familiarity with fees or registration, comfort with mobile messages, visual needs, and assistive-technology use. Ask participants how they prefer to read the material and about task-relevant reading/device needs; do not substitute nationality or broad language labels for this variation. Analyze language-specific and overall findings separately where audiences differ (E01, E06).

5. **Oral support and service explanation.** Staff may be clarifying incomplete policy, translating ad hoc, or repeating a stable process; the brief does not say. A standardized staff-assisted option could help people who prefer oral support, while variable explanations could obscure whether the notice itself works. First record an unassisted answer, then offer a consistent, approved explanation and record any change. Later history should map staff scripts, training, contact routes, and question categories without treating fewer questions alone as proof of understanding.

These pathways may coexist. Do not infer a translation deficit or literacy problem from questions alone.

## Comparable implementations and transfer limits

- **Toronto Parks and Recreation (E09):** the live page distinguishes registering, joining a waitlist, receiving an offer, responding within 36 hours, and enrollment; it explicitly says waitlist entry does not guarantee enrollment. Its payment rules have conditions, including an installment option for programs over $100. This is useful as a process-state model and shows that service conditions can be stated. Toronto’s eligibility, costs, channels, and status transitions are not evidence of the fictional center’s policy.
- **Austin Parks and Recreation (E10):** the FAQ says spaces are not held across online, walk-in, and phone registration; it describes a no-fee waitlist and a payment deadline for an offered space, with partial payment limited to eligible classes. These different policies make the transfer limit concrete. Ask what “submit” means for this center and whether status differs by channel; do not copy Austin’s policy.
- **Digital translation/accessibility (E04–E05):** separate language destinations, semantic text, language tags, and narrow-viewport reflow are options to evaluate for a digital route. They do not settle the layout of a printed bilingual notice or prove which option this center’s readers prefer.
- **Communication and research guidance (E01–E08):** official guidance emphasizes audience/objective definition, neutral tasks, accessible consent and language support, and user pretesting. CDC and UK public-service guidance are method prompts from health/government contexts, not case-specific outcome evidence. The DfE manual warns that it is under review.

## Comparison set for the later study

Keep policy facts fixed across conditions and use an owner-approved process map.

- **A — Unchanged notice:** obtain the actual current authorized notice and use its actual print/mobile route. It is the anchor. If the current version or route cannot be recovered, do not invent a baseline; report that comparison as unavailable.
- **B — Content/information-structure alternative:** in the same medium, test an owner-approved version that groups the exact total cost, materials inclusion/separate charge, and payment timing together, and states what form submission does and does not guarantee. Preserve all policy conditions. This is a study prototype, not publishable copy.
- **C — Presentation/channel alternative:** keep A’s approved words unchanged but compare the actual mobile rendering with a text-based, reflowing presentation that exposes fee and status information without image-only text or hidden continuation. If a short message links to a full page, test preview and destination as one journey. Do not fold a wording change into this condition.
- **D — Supported route:** where staff help is offered, first collect an independent answer, then provide an approved, consistent oral explanation in the participant’s chosen language or interpreter-supported route. Record what changes. Keep this as support, not a substitute for an understandable notice.

A/B/C separate unchanged content, content/structure, and presentation/channel hypotheses; D tests a support path. They depend on actual source materials and owner-verified policy. If budget cannot support all comparisons, state what remains untested rather than combining conditions.

## Proposed participant study

### Gate before recruitment

Recover current/prior notice versions where possible, actual channel variants and mobile-message/landing sequence, fee and enrollment policy, submission and confirmation states, change dates, translation/review process, staff explanations, and any lawful aggregate question/support records. Ask the owner to validate the process map. Establish the actual language pair(s), writing systems/variants, common access routes, budget, and local ethics/privacy review. This brief authorizes a proposal only; no outreach was done.

### Participants and accessibility

Recruit adults who are actual or plausible users of these courses after the relevant languages and audience are established. Use voluntary outreach through center-approved channels and appropriate community partners only after the owner authorizes a study. Ask how participants prefer to read the material; self-described reading/speaking ability in each offered language; reading comfort; prior enrollment familiarity; device/connectivity needs; and requested accessibility support. Purposively include variation in literacy/reading confidence and mobile use within each language, without treating a language group as uniform or using ethnicity as a proxy. Include screen-reader/zoom users where those routes are in scope. Offer remote, in-person, or assisted formats according to participant preference and feasible access.

Prepare invitation, information sheet, consent, tasks, and follow-up materials in identified languages and usable formats, with qualified translation and independent review. Let participants ask questions through appropriate language support and allow extra time for interpretation. Explain purpose, procedures, data use/retention, observers/recording, voluntary participation, withdrawal, and a contact/complaint route. Make non-recorded participation possible if structured notes suffice. Collect only task-relevant data, use coded notes and fictional course scenarios, do not ask for actual fee hardship or enrollment-account data, and do not link participation to course access. Obtain local privacy/ethics approval before recruitment. These are proposed safeguards, not approvals already obtained (E06–E08).

### Sample and session design

A practical first formative round is 12–18 participants per materially used written language across two short iterations, balanced across A/B/C first exposures as recruitment allows. Aim for 4–6 independent first exposures to each condition per language before making a comparative judgment; expand or narrow claims after the actual number of languages, budget, and access needs are known. This is an issue-finding target, not a statistically powered or representative sample. If the service has several important languages and cannot fund all cells, prioritize with service-area evidence and report untested cells explicitly.

Use a 30–45 minute moderated session with a neutral script, one primary first-exposure condition per participant to reduce learning effects, and parallel course scenarios if later cross-condition comparison is needed. Where feasible, randomize condition within each language and profile. Ask realistic tasks without pointing to the answer, then ask what in the notice led to the answer. The moderator should stay neutral and note whether staff/interpreter assistance was needed. Test actual phone sizes, message previews, and connectivity states if mobile delivery is in scope; let assistive-technology users use their own devices where feasible (E05, E08).

### Separate outcomes

Score against the owner-approved process map. Do not collapse:

1. **Fee comprehension:** identify course price, whether materials are included or separately charged, and any amount/timing the policy specifies. Preserve “not stated / not sure”; do not force an invented answer.
2. **Reservation-status comprehension:** after the described form submission, identify current status, whether a place is held/confirmed, and the expected response or next action and timing. Do not assume receipt means reservation.
3. **Task completion:** locate/report the required cost and status and take the stated next step without moderator help. Record completion, time, retrieval location, errors, and requested assistance.
4. **Reader confidence:** after each answer, ask for a separate confidence rating and optional reason. Analyze confidence apart from correctness; high confidence in an incorrect interpretation is a serious signal. Do not use confidence or a literacy formula as a proxy for comprehension.

Also record language/version and device context only as needed to interpret results. Predefine error categories (materials-cost omission, amount/timing confusion, submission mistaken for confirmation, condition missed, link/preview not followed). Do not publish participant-level data or turn a small purposive sample into population estimates.

### Revision decision

Before seeing outcomes, the owner and research lead should define required policy facts and how to resolve a wrong, missing, or uncertain fact. Choose a prototype only if it preserves approved fee/enrollment rules and supports correct independent answers across relevant language and access routes. Review each outcome by condition and language, not an average alone. If participants cannot know whether materials are included or what submission does because policy is unstated or contradictory, escalate an owner decision; do not rewrite around it or change fees/reservation policy. If a content test is promising, conduct another revision, independent bilingual review, mobile/accessibility desk check, and participant round before any separate publication/deployment approval.
