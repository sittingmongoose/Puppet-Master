# Investigator discovery — fine particles in ceramics rinse water

Run: `C-R1-02-control` · case: `ER12-C-R1-02-FRESH` · role: investigator

## Scope and finding

This research develops a proposal only. The workshop is synthetic; no wastewater sample, glaze inventory, throughput record, discharge point, local rule, or disposal acceptance is known. I did not visit or sample a workshop, build or operate equipment, perform a wet test, or discharge water. Clear appearance is not a measure of suspended-solids concentration or dissolved constituents, and removal of particles cannot establish potability, discharge permission, or harmless captured solids.

The first comparison should be between (1) batch settling with controlled inflow/rest and separate solids capture, (2) settling followed by a replaceable, rated filter selected only after feed characterization, and (3) source segregation / reduced rinse-water generation with all captured material held for a qualified local partner. A high-pressure ceramic membrane train is a literature comparator, not a presumptive small-workshop choice. Do not add coagulants or other chemicals: the brief excludes dosing.

## Mechanisms and practical comparison

| Approach | What it can do | Main failure modes and costs to test |
|---|---|---|
| Improved settling / flow management | Capture the fraction that settles under the actual particle sizes, dispersion, and rest conditions. A covered, contained batch or equalized inlet avoids a new rush of rinse water disturbing an earlier batch; transfer only a sampled, measured supernatant to a retained vessel. | Fine or colloidal particles can remain suspended; solids can be resuspended during additions or decanting; a bucket can fill, short-circuit, overflow, or be cleaned too rarely. Settling transfers mass into wet sludge; it does not resolve dissolved constituents or sludge status. Measure rest time and settleable fraction rather than assuming a tank or a visual endpoint works. |
| Filtration after coarse capture/settling | A screen plus a locally selected replaceable depth/cartridge medium may retain additional suspended fines. Test the actual feed; rate alone does not establish removal. | Solids loading can blind media, reduce flow, cause bypass/breakthrough, and add replacement and cleaning labor. Used media and any wash water become retained waste requiring characterization and a locally accepted route. A simple particle filter is not a dissolved-metals or salt treatment. Granular backwash systems have larger space, maintenance, and reject-water burdens than this small-shop concept. |
| Source segregation / managed collection | Dry wipe or reclaim solids before rinsing; first-rinse glaze/tool residues can be kept separate from lower-residue clay rinse, with sealed, labeled, compatible containers and partner-approved off-site handling. This prevents some solids entering the water stream and avoids choosing an unsupported discharge endpoint. | It creates an explicit labor, training, container-space, storage, transport, and receiving-facility problem. Unknown or mixed residues complicate characterization. Do not infer that evaporation, ordinary trash, sewer, or any other destination is acceptable. |
| Ceramic MF/NF/RO/ED (industrial comparator) | Published ceramic-manufacturing studies test staged membrane separation; NF/RO/electrodialysis can address some dissolved salts in addition to suspended particles. | High pressure/cross-flow, pretreatment, fouling, cleaning, power, monitoring, and concentrated retentate management are material design burdens. This is not a modest treatment recommendation absent a characterized need and capable partner. |

Settling and ordinary mechanical filtration are physical partitions. Their success criterion must include paired water measurements and the captured solids/media balance; apparent clarity or a single turbidity number cannot establish that dissolved components are absent. If glaze/material inventory supports it, a qualified lab should analyze selected total and operationally dissolved constituents in paired samples. A 0.45 µm filtered “dissolved” metal fraction is an operational laboratory definition, not proof that all smaller material is molecularly dissolved or safe.

## Prior implementation evidence and transfer limits

* In a 2018 ceramic-tile factory study, composite samples reflected production shifts. A 1 L laboratory sedimentation series (10–120 min) reached about 100 minutes; before any coagulant, TSS fell from 15,750 to 9,400 mg/L (41.97%) and turbidity fell 39.9%. Later jar tests used PAC and polymers, so their higher removal is out of scope here and must not be turned into a dosing recommendation. This is evidence that settling alone can remove a substantial but incomplete fraction in that factory matrix, not a design result for a community studio.
* A 2025 Egyptian ceramic-factory pilot compared sedimentation-plus-filtration, flotation-plus-filtration, and cyclone-plus-filtration. The feed had roughly 20,700–23,600 mg/L TSS in the reported first stage. A 250 L feed tank plus constant-head tank stabilized flow; each of three lines was reported at 25 L/day for 6 h/day. Stages ran sequentially for two weeks each: first without added chemicals, then with ferric chloride, then with anthracite substituted for sand. The reported untreated-chemical stage’s sedimentation line is a treatment *train* result (about 102 mg/L TSS), not a settling-only measurement. The paper reports physical treatment did not meet its own reuse pH target and identifies sludge disposal/reuse and cost as further work. It is one factory, pilot-scale, and its sewer/reuse claims are not transferable or permission for this synthetic case.
* A 2021 German ceramic-manufacturing study characterized variable production wastewater and compared single- and multi-stage ceramic MF/UF/NF. It reports an industrial particle distribution spanning submicron material through coarse silt, and notes that feed variation, fouling, reduced flux/rejection, and cleaning affect stable operation. Its 20-bar-capable cross-flow membrane pilot and factory wastewater are not small-shop analogues. It is useful evidence that particle size and feed history must be measured before choosing a filter and that a short clear-water run is weak transfer evidence.
* EPA’s 1984 municipal tertiary-filter review records clogging/head-loss, hydraulic surges, poor effluent, downtime, and maintenance/training failures. It recommends stable/equalized flow and upstream solids reduction, among other plant-scale fixes. This is a dated municipal granular-filter failure catalog, not a specification for a bucket or disposable cartridge.
* Practical studio precedents are workflow examples, not treatment-performance data: the University of Florida studio directs users to rinse tools in glaze-reclaim buckets first and keep clay slurry out of the sink; Western Carolina University’s 2020 laboratory plan describes a settling tank for glaze preparation/rinsing and frequent emptying, with its own hazardous-waste handling requirement. That disposal rule belongs to that institution and cannot classify this workshop’s waste.

## What an earlier implementation must report before transfer

Require source identity and site context; material/glaze inventory and SDS; process step generating each stream; influent TSS, turbidity and particle-size distribution; relevant total and dissolved chemistry; pH/conductivity; per-event and daily volume; peak/quiet and intermittent flow pattern; tank geometry, inflow/decant path, rest time and mixing; filter type, media/pore rating, loading, pressure or head loss, flow, breakthrough and replacement/cleaning interval; paired influent/effluent sampling and analytical QA; repeated runs across different work and glaze mixes; solids/sludge/filter-media mass, moisture, characterization and documented receiving route; staff time, space, consumables, energy, downtime, overflow/bypass and maintenance; and the exact performance endpoint, intended reuse/discharge destination, permit/authority, and limits. Include adverse runs, fixes, unplanned maintenance, changes between versions, and remaining issues. A headline percent removal without feed definition, methods, operating history, and residuals disposition does not transfer.

## Proposed baseline and later controlled pilot

**Gate before sampling or piloting:** identify where every current overflow/rinse stream goes; map the actual plumbing and containment; inventory all glaze/clay/cleaning inputs and task types; measure representative event and daily volumes plus peaks; establish available footprint and a real operator time cap; and ask the qualified local wastewater/waste partner to set the sampling plan, analytical panel, safe temporary containment, and lawful receiving route. Confirm whether the supervisor will accept zero discharge during a pilot. No one should pick analytes, disposal, or release limits from guessed composition or volume.

**Baseline:** over multiple representative work periods, include both busy and quiet activity and different documented glaze/tool tasks. Log activity, materials, rinse-water volume, additions, settling/rest/decant time, overflow, bucket cleanout and labor. Take paired, well-mixed influent and current-overflow/supernatant samples where safe and authorized. For variable episodic flow, use an event- or flow-representative composite for average load where the lab/authority approves it; collect separate immediate grabs for pH and short-lived/extreme conditions. A composite must not hide peaks. Ask the partner lab for TSS, turbidity, particle size, pH, conductivity and a material-inventory-informed total/filtered analyte panel; characterize separate sludge/media samples for management decisions. Preserve custody, blanks/duplicates and method/holding-time records as the lab requires.

**Contained comparison:** after baseline and acceptance gates, the partner may split a homogenized, documented batch into matched aliquots for controlled settling-time/decant and candidate-filter screening. If promising, compare the current capture practice, improved equalized batch settling, settling plus selected replaceable filter, and source segregation/collection over matched or alternated work blocks. Keep all liquid and solids contained; nothing is released while results or route approval are pending. Do not add chemicals. Keep production volume and task/glaze mix in the record so a quieter week is not mistaken for a treatment effect.

**Pre-agree endpoints:** captured particulate mass or paired TSS/particle measurements; turbidity as a secondary indicator; total and operationally dissolved analytes selected by inventory; retained water/solids volumes; overflow/bypass; filter flow/head loss and service interval; cleanout/replacement labor; storage footprint; consumable/energy use; and a complete residuals route. Set any numeric thresholds with the partner from actual analytical methods and the intended destination; none can be inferred here. Do not treat reuse targets as discharge standards.

**Stop and escalate:** immediately pause if an unrecorded material or glaze enters the stream, a container is near capacity, a leak/overflow/bypass occurs, solids/media cannot be safely contained, or the operator time cap is exceeded. Pause a filter trial on blockage, sharp flow decline, breakthrough, or failed partner-defined effluent criteria. Do not resume until the cause and waste route are reviewed. Escalate to source segregation and authorized off-site management or a broader qualified assessment if dissolved constituents remain, the captured solids have no accepted route, no permitted/accepted discharge endpoint is documented, or a physical step cannot meet a partner-set particle objective. No final disposal or discharge route is recommended before characterization and local review.

## Questions for the owner / local partner

1. Is preventing discharge entirely acceptable, including while characterization is pending?
2. What is the actual receiving point (sanitary sewer, septic, stormwater, surface water, or retained/off-site), and who can give written acceptance?
3. Which glazes, stains, clay bodies, cleaners, and outside-user materials enter the rinse stream, and can unknown/high-residue work be kept separate?
4. What are actual batch volumes, peaks, cleaning frequency, footprint, and staff-minutes available?
5. Can collected liquid, sludge, and used media be characterized and accepted by a specific local facility, and what records does that facility require?
6. What locally valid analysis and success thresholds would justify a later controlled pilot?
