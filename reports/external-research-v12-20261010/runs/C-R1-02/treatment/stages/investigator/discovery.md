## Research finding

The reported “clear overflow” and the apparent benefit from an extra bucket do not establish that the bucket caused improvement: the extra bucket coincided with a quieter week. The first research task for a later, locally approved pilot is to describe the streams and flow events, then compare like activity with like activity. No particle-size, glaze-residue, throughput, sink, sewer, or waste data are available. This proposal therefore does not pick a permanent device or assert a discharge or solids route.

A settling step and a filter primarily separate suspended material. The historical EPA contractor survey describes clays and glaze ingredients as waterborne suspended solids, notes that pottery waste quantity varies greatly and that flows were intermittent, small, and infrequently measured, and warns that fine clays can resist efficient settling. The same survey identifies that glaze formulations can introduce metal compounds and that treatment practice varied with formulation. It is explicitly a 1975 draft contractor report, not an official EPA publication or reviewed agency finding; it supplies historical mechanism and implementation context, not current requirements or a performance guarantee. [S01]

Treat the water as at least two analytically different fractions: (1) suspended/settleable particles and (2) dissolved constituents. Settling can transfer settleable solids to a sludge; a particulate filter can retain material above its effective retention range. Neither mechanism alone demonstrates removal of dissolved salts or metals. Metals may be associated with particles, dissolved, or mixed; the split is unknown here. Clear-looking water is therefore not an acceptance test. EPA Method 200.8 distinguishes an operationally filtered (0.45 µm) dissolved fraction from an unfiltered total-recoverable fraction; a qualified lab should choose suitable methods, analytes, preservation, reporting limits, and quality controls for this actual matrix. TSS is also measurable rather than visual; the cited EPA-hosted QAPP includes a versioned Method 160.2 procedure, but its lab and site context are not this workshop’s specification. [S05, S06]

## Feasible concepts to compare

| Concept | Mechanism and potential fit | Limits, labor, and residuals |
|---|---|---|
| Source control and stream segregation | Dry capture/scraping before rinse; keep glaze-contact cleanup separate from clay-only water; use closed, labelled collection while composition and route are unresolved. This reduces mixing at the source and makes later sampling and waste characterization interpretable. | It does not treat water. It needs reliable worker habits, covered/compatible containers, space, inventory, and a supervisor decision about whether avoiding discharge entirely is acceptable. It moves, rather than resolves, liquid and solids management. Do not mix, fire, reuse, evaporate, or dispose of residues based on appearance. [S01, S03, S04] |
| Flow-managed gravity settling | A quiet, staged or baffled collection volume can provide settling time and dampen short inflow pulses; solids accumulate for periodic removal. This may fit low-volume intermittent flow if the actual particles settle and the volume/cleanout workload fit the shop. A small art-pottery case in the old EPA survey recycled all process water after settling; the report does not establish that this workshop can do so. [S01] | Fine clays can remain suspended; high or sudden inflows can shorten effective residence, resuspend settled solids, or overflow. A current three-chamber pottery trap, ROHDE TA 85, is one purchasable example: 85 L internal / 70 L fill, one sink only, with a warning against suddenly draining a full 20 L sink; its manual says most suspended particles settle, requires regular cleanout, and describes the outlet as connected to sewer. That is an equipment use description, not permission or effluent validation. It does not characterize dissolved constituents. Settled glaze-bearing sludge remains an unknown waste. [S07, S08] |
| Filtration / recirculation | A staged bag/string-filter arrangement can capture particles that remain after settling and return water for tool cleanup, reducing reliance on a sewer connection. DiamondCore’s Cink 2 product page claims multi-stage 1 µm filtration and lists a powered recirculating station; the manufacturer's March 2025 manual describes a settling bucket, bag filter, and string cartridge. Treat these as product descriptions, not verified treatment data. [S09, S10] | The Cink 2 manual explicitly excludes glazes, chemicals, and hazardous materials; prohibits sewer/septic connection; and says disposal of collected water/material is subject to local rules. It therefore is not a candidate for mixed glaze rinse water; at most it is a conditional clay-only comparator after dependable segregation and confirmation of intended use. Its manual warns that heavy continuous use can cloud water, calls for settling time and at least weekly refresh, and documents frequent bucket cleanout, bag-filter cleaning, periodic string-filter cleaning/replacement (at least every 3 months), and reduced nozzle flow as pores clog. Solids and refresh water still need an approved route. Vendor page prices observed at access were USD $3,850 (Cink 2), $698 (Mini Cink), and $498 (Mini Cink Lite); these are time-sensitive listing prices, not project estimates, and the Cink 2 exclusion governs its use. [S09, S10] |
| No-treatment containment / qualified off-site handling | If the owner accepts preventing discharge as an option, retain glaze-contact and unknown rinse streams in suitable closed collection and ask the local authority/qualified waste partner about characterization, transport, and acceptance. This is a real comparison arm, not an assumed disposal method. | No treatment-performance claim; containers, handling, storage limits, fees, transport, and acceptance could make it impractical. The supervisor has not decided whether no discharge is acceptable, and no composition or local route is known. A US example, 40 CFR 262.11, calls for a generator’s accurate determination at generation before mixing/dilution and considers process knowledge and testing; it is not assumed to govern this synthetic location. [S03, S04] |

A possible later comparison is a source-segregation baseline plus (a) flow-managed settling on an approved clay-only stream, (b) a qualified filtration/recirculation trial only if the manufacturer’s intended-use restriction is met, and (c) closed collection/off-site handling if the supervisor chooses that option. Do not add flocculants, chemical dosing, pH adjustment, or any wet bench experiment: they are outside this brief’s boundaries. A 1975 industrial pottery report describes settling plus flocculation at plants, but that is historical evidence, not a recommendation here. [S01]

## What earlier implementations must disclose to transfer

For a settling tank, filter, or reuse claim to transfer to this shop, request: the stream/source and glaze/clay inventory; product/formulation and operating range; particle-size/TSS and total-versus-dissolved constituent data before and after treatment; flow per event and per day, peak/pulse pattern, batch size, tank geometry, effective volume, residence/settling time, and bypass/overflow conditions; whether measurements cover quiet and busy periods and each materially different glaze; sampling methods, detection/reporting limits, QA/QC, and representative sample handling; solids quantity and characterization plus its actual destination; water-reuse/discharge destination and the authority/permit condition; cleaning/filter-change frequency, staff minutes, clogging, leaks, odors, downtime, power/water needs, and consumables; and the study date, scale, operator, and independent verification. “Clear water,” a generic removal percentage, an uncharacterized sludge route, or a product claim without the above is insufficient.

The 1975 EPA contractor survey is a useful but weakly transferable implementation record: it reported 14 pottery plants, with one small earthenware art pottery recirculating all water after settling, five untreated discharges, five settling-basin discharges, and two settling-plus-flocculant discharges. Another plant used a complex settling/flocculation/clarification train and filtered underflows, recycled filtrate, and landfilled filter cake. The survey says many existing effluent records lacked heavy-metal analysis. Its plant scale, dated formulations, draft status, mixed process streams, incomplete characterization, and unavailable contemporary permission/fate records mean these cases cannot set this workshop’s design, performance threshold, or disposal route. [S01]

## Proposed later pilot and sampling plan (not run)

1. **Resolve inputs with the owner and qualified local partner.** Map each cleaning task and destination; inventory every clay/glaze product and its current supplier safety/technical information; record sink/bucket geometry, floor drains, daily/event water volumes, space, staffing, cleaning tolerance, and existing practices. Ask the supervisor to choose whether zero discharge is an acceptable pilot objective. Ask the wastewater authority/control authority whether a proposed contained pilot or any discharge requires approval, and ask a qualified waste partner how to characterize and manage the liquid and captured solids. Applicable location and authority are unknown.
2. **Establish a comparable baseline before changing flow.** Over representative quiet and busy operation and different glaze-use events, log event times, activity/recipe class, rinse volume, whether clay-only or glaze-contact, overflow/visible fines, and staff/cleanout time. Collect matched, representative influent and existing overflow samples only under an approved sampling plan. If event pulses are short, use event samples or a lab-approved flow/event composite rather than a single convenient clear sample. Record volume so concentrations can be interpreted as loads.
3. **Compare only pre-approved options under matched work.** The later partner should predefine the test arms and endpoints: current practice/baseline; a flow-managed settling configuration; and, only for a demonstrably segregated clay-only stream, a suitable filter/recirculation option. Keep a contained no-discharge/off-site arm available if the owner selects it. Compare under similar tool cleaning, water volumes, clay/glaze product mix, and high/low use. No treatment system was built, operated, or tested during this research stage.
4. **Measure both phases and operability.** Have a qualified lab choose analytes from the material inventory and assess TSS/settleable solids, supplementary turbidity, pH/conductivity as useful screening variables, and selected metals or other constituents in paired total-recoverable and dissolved fractions where indicated. The 0.45 µm filtered fraction is an operational definition, not complete chemical speciation. Track influent/effluent volume, dry collected solids mass (or a lab-agreed solids metric), sludge identity, filter changes, settling/holding time, labor minutes, water refresh/reuse, clogging, leaks, bypasses, odors, and overflow. Use lab-approved representative sampling, blanks/duplicates, chain of custody, method detection/reporting limits, and QA/QC. Do not handle strong-acid preservation or characterization outside the qualified lab’s protocol. No samples or analyses were performed here.
5. **Predefine stop and escalation rules with the authority/partner.** No pilot discharge occurs until the receiving authority confirms an acceptable route and conditions. Immediately stop inflow and contain/secure the stream after leak, overflow, bypass, unknown glaze entering a clay-only option, filter failure, or unsafe handling. Stop or redesign if measured values fail partner-approved limits/endpoints, if dissolved constituents remain unaddressed, or if the labor/solids burden is not sustainable. Escalate unknown glaze/solids classification to the local waste authority or qualified waste professional; do not infer harmlessness from captured solids or clarity. If no approved route exists, use the owner’s chosen containment/off-site option rather than discharge.
