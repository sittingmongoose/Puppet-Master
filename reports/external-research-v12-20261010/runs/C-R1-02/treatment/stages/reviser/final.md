## Decision statement

The clear-looking overflow and the apparent improvement after adding a bucket are observations, not evidence of treatment performance. The extra bucket coincided with a quieter week; lower inflow, different cleanup tasks, longer settling time, changed materials, or other differences could explain it. Recover the dates, configuration, cleanout practices, operators, and workload if records exist. Do not call this a trial or infer causation.

The useful next step is a staged, partner-led inquiry: map and segregate streams; establish a workload-matched baseline; characterize both particles and dissolved constituents; and compare only pre-approved options with prespecified sampling, operating measures, and stop rules. Compare flow-managed settling, a filtration option only on a reliably segregated clay-only stream and within the product’s intended use, source segregation, and closed collection/off-site handling if the owner accepts that objective. No permanent device or combination is selected.

A particle separator or particulate filter can transfer some suspended matter to collected solids. Neither mechanism by itself shows that dissolved constituents were removed. Glaze-derived metals may be particle-associated, dissolved, or divided between phases; the actual composition is unknown. Visual clarity, turbidity alone, a “recycling” label, a micron rating, or a settled supernatant is not a sufficient acceptance criterion and establishes no potability, safe discharge, regulatory compliance, or harmless residuals. Each separation step also creates collected sludge, media, or refresh water and recurring cleanout work; labor and residual fate are feasibility endpoints alongside particle capture.

## Mechanisms and feasibility

| Approach | Mechanism and potential fit | Conditions, failure, labor, and residuals |
|---|---|---|
| **Source segregation and dry capture** | Scrape or capture dry residues before rinsing; separate glaze-contact cleanup from clay-only water; label distinct streams. This reduces mixing and makes later sampling and residual decisions more interpretable. | It is source control, not water treatment. It depends on repeatable worker practices, suitable covered containers, space, inventory, and preventing cross-contamination. It does not address dissolved constituents already in collected water. Staff time shifts to separation and handling. Liquid, dry residue, sludge, and filters still need characterization and an accepted route. The owner has not decided whether preventing discharge entirely is acceptable. [S01, S03, S04] |
| **Flow-managed gravity settling** | A quiet batch, staged or baffled compartments, or flow buffering may provide settling time and reduce disturbance from short pulses. The idea is most plausible for an actually settleable stream with an operating volume and cleanout schedule the shop can manage. | Fine clay may stay suspended. Fast or sudden inflow may shorten effective settling time, resuspend settled solids, or overflow. Performance depends on particles, concentration, actual flow, usable volume, geometry, and dwell time; none is known here. Periodic sludge removal and vessel cleaning take labor, and the captured glaze-bearing sludge remains uncharacterized. No sewer connection shown by a vendor is local approval. [S01, S07, S08, S12] |
| **Particulate filtration / recirculation** | A bag or string filter may capture particles remaining after settling and return water for tool cleanup. This is a conditional comparison for a stream whose material is within a candidate product’s intended use. | The March 2025 Cink 2 manual limits use to clean water and water-soluble clay materials; it excludes glazes, chemicals, paints, and hazardous/biohazardous materials and prohibits sewer or septic connection. It is unsuitable for mixed or unknown glaze water. Even for a reliably segregated clay-only stream, intended use and local handling need confirmation. The manual warns that heavy continuous use can cloud water, calls for sedimentation time and at least weekly refresh, and describes frequent drain-bucket emptying, bag-filter cleaning, string-filter cleaning at least every three months, reduced flow as pores clog, and leakage during maintenance. Captured clay, filters, and refresh water still need an accepted route. These are manufacturer instructions, not independent treatment results. [S09, S10, S11] |
| **Closed collection / off-site handling without treatment** | Keep unknown or glaze-contact water contained in compatible, labeled containers while a qualified local partner and the owner determine characterization, transport, and acceptance. It is a useful no-treatment comparator or interim approach if the owner chooses a no-discharge objective. | It does not treat water. Space, storage limits, handling, fees, pickup, and acceptance could make it infeasible. No route is selected or assumed. Ask the actual local authority or qualified handler before choosing one. [S03, S04] |

Combinations may be considered after stream characterization, but are not presumed better. No flocculant, chemical dosing, pH adjustment, or wet experiment is proposed.

### What the earlier examples do and do not show

The April 1975 EPA contractor draft surveyed 14 pottery plants. It describes variable clay/glaze wastes and intermittent flows. Of 14 plants, one had no process wastewater; one small earthenware art pottery recycled all water after settling; the other 12 discharged: five untreated, five after settling basins, and two after settling with flocculants. One larger train filtered underflows, recycled filtrate, and landfilled filter cake. Some available plant data lacked heavy-metals analysis. The report itself says it is a draft contractor report, not an official EPA publication or agency-reviewed finding. Its age, industrial settings, formulations, mixed streams, incomplete characterization, and missing current permission/residual records prevent it from setting a small-shop design, performance threshold, or waste route. Flocculation is historical context only, not a recommendation here. [S01]

The ROHDE TA 85 is a product example, not validated workshop performance. The current product page lists 85 L internal volume and 70 L fill volume. Its March 2026 manual describes three compartments and regular cleanout, limits connection to one sink, and warns that a sudden gushing inflow such as draining a full 20 L sink can overflow it. The July 2025 manual lists 85 L, 587 × 387 × 523 mm, and 12 kg; the March 2026 manual lists 85 L internal/70 L fill, 375 × 575 × 525 mm, and 11 kg. Both carry the same pulse warning, and neither explains whether the specification changes reflect a physical revision or documentation. Confirm the exact model/serial and usable volume before any future sizing. The manual’s sewer installation and residue language do not authorize local discharge or disposal. [S07, S08, S12]

DiamondCore describes Cink 2 as a multi-stage 1 µm clay-water filtration/recirculation product. Its March 2025 manual’s use exclusions govern any comparison: it is not for glaze-bearing or unknown water, and it cannot connect to sewer/septic. The detailed current page describes updates (including nozzle, foot switch, bucket/dolly and filter changes), lists a 190 lb unit and 220 lb shipping weight, and cautions against glaze capture/reuse and mixed-clay reuse. No formal recall or dated problem-to-fix history was located in the bounded records, which is not a comprehensive recall/service search. One hosted customer review reports motor noise affecting classroom conversation; this is one unverified experience, not evidence of a general defect. The comparison page’s access-time displayed prices were $3,850 for Cink 2, $698 for Mini Cink, and $498 for Mini Cink Lite; they are dynamic listing prices, not quotes or project costs, and were not independently re-audited in this reviser stage. Neither page nor manual supplies independent treatment-performance data. [S09, S10, S11]

## Proposed later investigation and pilot — not performed

### Gate 1: map the process and resolve prerequisites

With the supervisor, define the objective: fewer visible fines, less plumbing blockage, lower rinse-water generation, zero discharge, or another goal. These are not interchangeable. Recover any extra-bucket chronology and note configuration, workload, task mix, and cleaning habits. Map each cleaning task and current drain path, including floor/mop water. Inventory clay and glaze products and obtain current supplier information. Record actual bucket/sink geometry, event and daily volumes, space, staffing, and tolerable cleanout labor. Ask the supervisor whether no discharge is acceptable. Ask the actual wastewater control authority whether any proposed pilot or discharge requires approval; ask a qualified laboratory and waste partner about representative sampling, characterization, and accepted handling. Location, authority, drain route, chemistry, and approvals are unknown.

The U.S. POTW provisions in 40 CFR §403.5 and EPA’s local-limits explanation are jurisdiction-specific examples; they support consulting the actual authority, not a conclusion about this synthetic site. U.S. hazardous-waste determination rules in 40 CFR §262.11 illustrate why process knowledge and testing may matter before mixing or assigning a route; they do not classify this water or sludge. Applicability, generator status, and any local requirements remain unknown. [S02, S03, S04]

### Gate 2: establish a comparable baseline

Before a process change, log representative quiet and busy activity and materially different glaze-use events. For each event record date/time, task and product/recipe class, clay-only versus glaze-contact versus floor/mop/mixed/unknown, rinse volume and duration/flow pattern, bucket configuration, overflow or visible fines, and staff and cleanout time. Include available records from the extra-bucket period. Compare like work with like work; if workload or material mix differs, do not attribute clearer overflow to the bucket. Record volumes so concentrations can later be interpreted as loads.

### Gate 3: compare only pre-approved conditions

After characterization, lab/authority review, and owner choice, a qualified partner may compare (A) current practice as baseline; (B) source segregation plus flow-managed settling on an accepted stream; (C) filtration/recirculation only on a demonstrably segregated clay-only stream and a model whose intended-use restrictions are met; and (D) closed collection/off-site handling without treatment if the owner selects a no-discharge objective. Define arms, endpoints, schedule, sample locations, operating limits, and stop rules in advance. Match work, rinse volumes, and material mix as far as practical, avoid changing multiple factors at once where possible, and document unavoidable differences. Keep all pilot water contained unless and until the authority and partner confirm an acceptable route. No treatment system was built, connected, operated, or compared during this research work.

### Sampling, measurements, and quality

Have a qualified laboratory select current matrix-appropriate methods, analytes, preservation, reporting limits, and QA/QC after reviewing inventory and the question. For short pulses, use event samples or a lab-approved flow/event composite; do not select a convenient clear sample. Collect representative paired inlet/outlet samples for each approved condition across relevant workload and stream classes. Measure TSS or settleable solids; turbidity may supplement but does not replace solids or chemistry results. Where product inventory or partner review indicates, assess selected constituents in both total-recoverable and operationally dissolved fractions. EPA Method 200.8 defines dissolved trace elements operationally using a 0.45 µm filtered fraction and separately prepared unfiltered total-recoverable aqueous samples; this is an operational fraction, not complete chemical speciation, and the method does not identify what is present here or select acceptance criteria. The EPA-hosted Method 160.2 QAPP/SOP is a dated measurement example, not this shop’s method specification. The qualified lab should choose the method and handle any required preservation; do not conduct strong-acid preservation outside its protocol. pH and conductivity can be useful screening variables, not stand-alone safety tests. [S05, S06]

Log event flow and volume; settling/holding time; solids quantity using a lab-agreed metric; reuse/refresh volume; sludge and filter identity; staff minutes; cleanout and filter changes; fill level; flow reduction; breakthrough, carryover, resuspension, clogging, leaks, bypass, overflow, odor, and downtime. Use representative sampling, suitable blanks/duplicates, chain of custody, holding/preservation, and lab QA/QC. Choose sample count, duration, endpoints, and thresholds with the partner from observed variability and detection needs; the brief supplies no data from which to invent any of them.

### Stop and escalation conditions

No pilot discharge until the receiving authority confirms an acceptable route and conditions. Stop inflow and contain/secure the stream after a leak, overflow, bypass, unknown glaze entering a clay-only branch, filter/settler failure, or unsafe handling. Pause or redesign if partner-agreed endpoints are missed, dissolved constituents remain unaddressed, or labor/residual burden is unsustainable. Escalate unknown glaze or solids classification to the local authority or qualified waste professional. Do not infer harmlessness from clarity or captured particles. If no accepted route exists, continue only the owner-approved contained option; do not discharge.
