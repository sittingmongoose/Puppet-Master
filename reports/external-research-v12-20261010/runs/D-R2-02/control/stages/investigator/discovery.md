## Scope and evidence status

The brief describes a synthetic produce cooperative, not an observed operation. It supplies no raw logger trace, product-temperature reading, route record, product identity, verified quality result, or cost ceiling. The only reported facts are that some logger traces reportedly show short warm events around the route midpoint, a logger sometimes rides loose on top of the load, delivery order changed recently, and customer batches became smaller. These reports do not establish that product warmed, that quality fell, or that food safety was affected. No operating instruction for current deliveries is proposed.

The study question is whether the reported events are (a) a real change in the air at the logger, (b) a meaningful change in produce temperature, and/or (c) a change associated with delivered quality. Those are separate observations. Food safety, compliance, product suitability, and shelf life require separate product- and process-specific assessment; this brief supports none of those conclusions. No safety temperature or quality threshold is inferred here.

## Mechanisms and live hypotheses

Heat reaches the load by conduction through the crate and vehicle walls, convection with moving air and door exchange, and radiant loading from sun or a hot road surface. Produce also generates respiratory heat. Product temperature changes according to its initial condition, mass and shape, thermal properties, packaging, contact and air velocity. A small logger exposed at the top can respond to local cargo air or an opening much faster than produce pulp. Its record is still a real measurement at that sensor location, but it is not automatically a product-temperature trace. Logger model, calibration, response time, sample interval, attachment, clock and exact position are all unknown [S01, S02, S05].

A brief spike could therefore be a local air pulse near a door, a top-of-load microenvironment, a stop or loading event, a sensor/clock/sampling issue, or a product-relevant exposure. The supplied facts do not choose among them. A small batch could change the crate fill, overall load geometry, thermal mass, exposed voids, airflow path, delivery time or number/duration of openings. Its effect has no known direction without the vehicle, load and crate geometry. The route-order and batch-size changes occurred together, so a before/after comparison that does not record both would be confounded.

Initial product temperature is a first-order variable. USDA’s 2016 handbook describes precooling as a separate operation for removing field heat; it says highway trailers should not be relied on for initial cooling, while noting crop-specific methods and exceptions. Forced-air cooling is broadly adaptable, but time depends on airflow, produce size and package resistance. Hydrocooling requires a product that tolerates water contact and water-resistant packaging; room cooling is slow and has commodity-specific exceptions [S01]. This supports measuring and investigating the cooperative’s initial conditions, not prescribing a crop-independent cooling setpoint.

Packaging creates another plausible mechanism. Vent placement, blocked or misaligned vents, liners, stack patterns and load spaces can change airflow and heat removal. Full-scale citrus work found that room precooling did not bring every fruit to the study’s target after 24 hours; some fruit reheated in the trailer middle where pallet ventilation was insufficient. It used 108 sensors in one Greece–Switzerland shipment and measurements at the trailer rear across 30 additional shipments. This makes airflow and spatial mapping relevant, but its citrus, large trailer, long route and stated target do not transfer to this unknown produce route [S03]. Reusable-crate history also matters for material, shape, condition, cleaning, pooling and reuse; the FAO guide is a supply-chain management reference, not evidence about this cooperative’s crates [S07].

## Alternatives worth evaluating

1. **Measurement that separates air from produce.** Keep one fast-response air logger intentionally at an exposed/diagnostic position if the goal is to see door or top-of-load events; pair it with a logger inside a marked, representative crate position; and use a commodity-matched artificial fruit or a permitted, instrumented representative fruit to estimate pulp response. Mark every position, synchronize clocks, record device/model, calibration, response, interval and retrieval, and record loading and stop/door events alongside temperatures. UF/IFAS guidance distinguishes air, carton and pulp readings and recommends recording logger location and using multiple cargo positions; its three-point placement is guidance for larger transport equipment, not a universal layout for this route [S02]. An artificial-apple prototype’s cooling time was within 5% of ten real apples and closer than water-filled simulators, which differed by up to 16%; it is a candidate measurement approach only after matching and validating the target produce, not a ready-made model for unspecified vegetables [S05].

2. **Passive packaging/buffering.** A reusable phase-change-material (PCM) insert or alveolus in an insulated crate is a promising short-route comparator. It can buffer temperature change only while its thermal store is in the suitable preconditioned state; phase-change temperature, amount, placement, route duration, product compatibility, heat load, crate airflow, condensation and reuse all need testing. A 2023 local-distribution study compared orange crates with and without PCM. Its van was insulated and the reefer was off during winter measurement; study crates were stored around 5 °C overnight, placed near the rear door/right wall, and sensors were in/near the crates. It reported less temperature variation in PCM crates and spikes during unloads. The paper’s abstract calls the field test 8 h while its methods list 08:00–14:05; it also measured sensor air around produce rather than pulp or delivered-quality outcomes. It therefore shows a plausible packaging mechanism and a monitoring design, not a predicted benefit for this cooperative [S04]. The paper itself identifies PCM re-use, quantity/autonomy and route/climate/product matching as unresolved.

3. **Preconditioning and airflow/packaging correction.** If records show hot starting produce or a slow, uneven cool-down, first test a crop-compatible use of existing shaded/cool staging or available precooling, then verify whether crate vents, liners, filling and stack alignment let air reach the load. Do not assume a new powered unit is needed, that the current insulated crate is inadequate, or that additional insulation alone is beneficial; insulation can slow both gain and removal of heat. Compare candidate packaging with the current crate in matched loads. Any structural crate change requires the crate owner’s approval and checks for strength, product damage, moisture loss, and airflow.

4. **Route/handling controls for a later, approved pilot.** First reconstruct stop order, departure/arrival times, door-open durations, staging, loading sequence, number of drops, unloading order and ambient/sun conditions. A later comparison could change one approved handling variable at a time (for example, load staging to reduce open exposure) while holding crop, initial temperature, load geometry and package as stable as practical. Test any route-order change only after baseline observation and cooperative agreement; do not build route software or alter today’s deliveries. A field study of multi-drop dill/parsley transport found fast changes in truck-body air/moisture with door opening, but its empty-body heat calculation and herb/truck conditions do not quantify product response in this case [S06].

5. **Low-cost path if powered cooling is not justified.** Do not buy powered cooling on the basis of a loose top logger alone. The useful no-purchase package is: correct measurement placement; preserve and map the current crate/load; use existing shade and crop-appropriate staging; improve documented loading/door handling only if the baseline supports it; and trial reusable PCM only if a matched test shows worthwhile product-temperature and quality improvement. An evaporative cloth/crate is another possible passive approach, but only where crop and ambient humidity/wet-bulb conditions, water access, moisture/cleanliness handling and the short route make evaporation useful. A published single-crate study used a duck-cloth-wrapped plastic crate with lettuce, rambutan, bitter gourd and eggplant; its repository has only an abstract, so it is a screening lead, not a recommendation without the full conditions and crop match [S08].

## Pilot proposal and decision gate

**Preparation gate:** Identify the produce types and varieties, crate/vehicle and air-delivery arrangement, initial-condition range, customer/owner quality specification, acceptable commercial quality loss, available existing cooling/staging, operator agreement, and practical spend. Select non-safety quality endpoints with the produce owner (for example, blinded visual grade/defects, firmness or texture where appropriate, mass loss and saleable fraction at delivery and an agreed follow-up point). Set the minimum worthwhile improvement and decision rule before seeing pilot results. A food-safety professional separately determines any food-safety assessment; this study will not use an air trace to make that decision.

**Baseline:** Observe enough representative future routes to include ordinary differences in crop/lot, initial conditions, load size, weather and stop pattern. Preserve a traceable shipment/lot ID. Log air at an intentionally selected exposed location and inside a marked crate; pair those with product-core readings or a crop-matched, checked artificial product sensor at the suspected warm zone and a comparison position. Take documented initial and arrival produce readings. Record temperature logger specification/calibration/interval/clock, exact crate/vehicle location, crate and load map, produce and lot, mass/count, loading and departure/stop/door/open/arrival times, outdoor conditions/sun exposure, vehicle and reefer status/setpoint if present, initial condition, and handling. Keep the existing logger’s top position as a diagnostic channel during this phase rather than silently moving it. Do not treat mixed loads as a single crop.

**Comparisons:** Based on baseline evidence, compare current practice with one package/preconditioning candidate (including PCM only if plausible) in randomized or position-swapped matched crates across repeated routes. Separately compare one approved route/handling change in a crossover or matched-run design; avoid changing route and package together. Determine the sample count from baseline variability and the cooperative’s minimum worthwhile effect rather than inventing it now. Track labor, materials, crate/inserts and reuse, staging/conditioning, instrumentation, energy/fuel, product loss and operational burden; quote actual local costs before a purchase decision.

**Decision:** If measured produce response and quality meet the owner’s pre-set target with no economically meaningful loss, retain the current cooling setup and adopt the least-cost proven monitoring/handling practice. If not, pilot passive packaging or preconditioning and route/handling separately. Purchase powered cooling only if representative produce-temperature/quality evidence repeatedly misses its agreed target under the best feasible passive and handling alternative, powered cooling is shown to improve the pre-set endpoint by a worthwhile amount, and its total cost per saleable crate is acceptable against the cooperative’s own ceiling. No numeric threshold, safety limit, or investment figure can be set from this brief.
