# Independent discovery — ER12-D-R2-02-FRESH

## Question and evidentiary boundary
The reported brief spikes describe logger exposure at an unverified location. A loose logger atop a load measures the air at its own location and is especially responsive to airflow, sun, door events, and brief ambient changes. Product surface/core temperature has thermal inertia and may follow a different time course; neither is equivalent to delivered quality. Quality must be assessed with a predeclared, produce-specific endpoint at delivery (and, if agreed, after a defined holding period). Food safety is a separate assessment and cannot be inferred from these traces. There are no case data to analyze.

## Independent discovery and implementation history
The 1995 Codex transport code (amended 2004) is still a useful mechanism checklist: pre-cool produce when needed, check ventilation and load arrangement, keep air paths through/around loads, document the logger location, and place a small air recorder in a suspected warm zone (its illustrative location is near the top, side wall and rear door, away from discharge air). That recommendation captures a warm-air environment; it does not turn an air logger into a product-temperature measurement. A later FAO postharvest account likewise distinguishes commodity temperature from room/vehicle air and notes that package design changes airflow. These sources support investigation, not a generic limit or a claim that this cooperative's crates or vehicle meet a standard.

Two short-range field studies show why route conditions and package results must be interpreted in context. A 2022 Portugal delivery-route study instrumented diverse produce crates along a refrigerated van route with multiple stops and compared within-crate measurements with vehicle context. A 2023 follow-on field test compared orange crates with PCM inserts against controls on one winter route with the vehicle refrigeration off; the authors report less fluctuation in PCM crates, but the tested air sensors, oranges, single route, ambient conditions, and finite PCM phase-change reserve do not establish product-core/quality benefits here. Both studies are analogues for observability, not transferable efficacy estimates.

For product-proximate placement, a 2017 artificial-apple prototype study compared its cooling time with ten real apples (within 5% for that test) and found water-filled simulators differed by as much as 16%. This supports evaluating a matched, species-appropriate thermal surrogate as a repeatable alternative to puncturing saleable produce; it does not validate a generic fruit simulator for unknown crops or this route. A 2024 qualitative study of three selected Australian vegetable chains reports practical logger adoption barriers including cost, labor to retrieve/analyze, mixed commodity requirements, and weak data sharing/accountability. Treat those as questions for this cooperative, not local facts.

## Candidate mechanisms to compare
1. **Measure before buying cooling.** Retain a standardized, secured air logger at a documented likely warm location for vehicle/route exposure. Pair it with a logger inside a representative closed crate and, where the crop is known, a calibrated probe in a sacrificial representative unit or validated crop-matched artificial fruit. Use positions spanning suspected warm/cold zones, log sensor model, response time, calibration check, sampling interval, and exact placement. This makes spikes, package buffering, and product thermal response separable.
2. **Passive packaging and initial-condition changes.** Record crate vents, stack/fill, product and crate initial temperatures, pre-cooling practice, and shade. Compare a vent-preserving insulating/reflective cover or suitable passive buffer only after checking it will not block required airflow or trap field heat. Insulation slows heat exchange in both directions; PCM provides finite buffering only after conditioning at a crop-compatible phase temperature and must be sized/reconditioned for the observed route. Neither is a substitute for removing initial heat.
3. **Handling and route factors.** Record actual route/order, stop and door-open times, crate accesses, batch/load size, load position, staging shade, and outside conditions. The changed route order and smaller batches are concurrent changes and potential confounders. Evaluate handling/route alternatives as separately approved future arms (for example, shade/staging or fewer/shorter access events); do not change today's deliveries. A door strip/curtain is a published multi-stop reefer example, but vehicle fit, ventilation, fire/egress, and cooperative workflow must be checked before proposing it as a local option.

## Pilot proposal (future work; not executed)
First resolve crop(s)/cultivar and acceptable quality loss; supplier/batch and traceability; crate construction, vents, insulation, fill mass and packing practice; initial product/crate temperatures and any pre-cooling; logger models, calibration, response and sample interval; route records, stop durations, door/opening and staging practices; load positions/sizes; weather/shade; whether route order can change; labor/logistics and cost ceiling; and who owns quality/safety criteria. These determine the candidate treatment and a meaningful endpoint.

Collect a baseline over traceable, comparable shipments without changing delivery operations. For every shipment retain batch/lot, crate and load-position IDs, product type, starting conditions, route version, stop/opening event times, and retrieval chain. Pair an ambient/vehicle-air channel and a fixed warm-zone air channel with an in-crate air channel and crop-proximate probe/surrogate; record calibration, response/sampling, and placement map. At loading and receipt, measure actual product temperature on a predeclared representative sample with a suitable calibrated method; independently score crop-specific quality (e.g. agreed defects, firmness/texture, color, weight loss, or saleable fraction) using the cooperative's own acceptance definitions. Do not substitute air traces for quality. No safety endpoint is proposed here.

After baseline variability and costs are known, pre-register a paired/block comparison that changes one factor at a time: current practice; then a low-cost handling/preconditioning candidate; then a vent-compatible passive package/PCM candidate if indicated. Route/order is a separate arm only with owner and customer permission and schedule feasibility. Determine replication from baseline variability and precision needed; do not choose a sample size or threshold before endpoints and economics are agreed. The decision criterion should require a repeatable improvement in the crop-specific product-temperature/quality endpoint against the agreed quality target, with net cost per shipment within the cooperative's ceiling. A short air-only spike is not a trigger for powered cooling. Consider powered cooling only if product-proximate exposure or quality still misses the agreed target under feasible low-cost changes and a costed comparison supports it. If not, the useful alternative is documented logger placement plus shade, improved initial-condition/pre-cooling control where appropriate, vent/load/stack correction, and measured handling improvements.
