## Recommendation

The supplied account does not identify a cause or justify new treatment. Start by agreeing with residents and the manager what outdoor experience matters, where and when it matters, then make a prospective session/event log. If access and suitable equipment permit, follow with repeated, matched blocks comparing one reversible source control at a fixed rehearsal setup. Record the rehearsal-room and courtyard outcomes separately, include quiet/no-rehearsal periods, and use the same suitable measurement chain at each repeat.

A lower-output setting for an existing amplified source, or a resident-approved quiet-mode/time window, is a useful low-cost first comparison. A separately logged temporary change to the existing movable panels can test room comfort or decay; it cannot stand in for an outdoor benefit. If an outside change is absent or ambiguous, or if path attribution matters, ask a qualified acoustician to scope a non-invasive, low-frequency-capable path study before anyone selects treatment. No construction, permanent installation, or structural alteration is recommended.

The proposed source-level comparison can show whether a tested operating change coincides with a repeatable courtyard change. It does not by itself distinguish airborne transmission from structure-coupled transmission. I accept the critic’s material incompleteness finding CI-01 and add a conditional specialist diagnostic below. Even that diagnostic may leave a mixed path unresolved: air pressure can excite surfaces, and contact or impact excitation also radiates airborne sound.

## Situation and evidence limits

The hypothetical facts are: a small rehearsal building beside a residential courtyard; intermittent “bass” complaints, including some during drum sessions and one report of worse evenings without drumming; an undocumented room rearrangement last winter; a recorder and basic sound meter that might be borrowed later; movable freestanding panels; and a manager seeking a defensible investigation before spending money. These are not measurements. There are no supplied complaint counts or timestamps, source levels, floor plan, dimensions, construction details, resident metric, access permissions, or verified cause.

“Bass” may describe low-frequency airborne pressure, vibration carried through a structure and re-radiated, both, or an unrelated event. The no-drumming report makes it useful to log amplified bass and other activity separately; it does not rule out rehearsal sound or establish any cause. The available sources establish plausible mechanisms and methods, not what happens in this building. Source scope and transfer limits are summarized in the [source index](sources/index.md).

A working causal sketch is:

    rehearsal source and setting
       ├─ airborne room sound ─ openings / façade / flanking ─ courtyard sound
       └─ contact excitation ─ floor or wall vibration ─ re-radiated sound
    other building or courtyard event ─────────────────────── courtyard sound
    room modes and reverberation ─ affect room sound and measurement positions

This is a list of hypotheses, not a finding that any route exists here.

Keep two outcome records from the beginning:

- **Rehearsal-room outcome:** sound and room decay at several marked musician/listener positions, plus whether the musicians consider the condition usable.
- **Courtyard outcome:** sound at a safe, agreed receiver point and residents’ separate, voluntary, time-stamped observations of audibility, low-frequency character, rattle/vibration, or disturbance.

A quieter indoor recording does not show reduced courtyard disturbance. Phone audio can help mark event timing, but unless its complete measurement chain is calibrated it is not a sound-level result. Neither a phone recording nor a manufacturer’s absorption figure predicts courtyard benefit.

## What the research supports

### Measurement methods and room position

ISO 1996-2:2017 is a framework for outdoor environmental sound-level determination and reporting uncertainty; it does not choose this residents’ metric or set a universal local threshold. ISO 16283-1:2014/Amd 1:2017 covers airborne insulation between rooms, and ISO 16283-3:2016 covers airborne façade insulation, with stated ranges beginning at 50 Hz and intended room volumes of 10–250 m³. Their scope does not make them courtyard ambient-noise or structure-vibration tests, and their lower frequency bound leaves some bass questions outside the stated range. ISO 10052:2021 is a simplified survey method with greater uncertainty than engineering methods; a handheld meter and improvised music test are not thereby a formal insulation assessment. Check official status and method fit again before any standards-compliance use. [S01–S04](sources/index.md#measurement-and-standards-references)

Pedersen, Møller and Persson Waye report large low-frequency variation by position, including up to 20–30 dB in simulations and measurements in three rooms. That supports repeatable readings at multiple marked positions, not a prediction of variation in this room. ISO 3382-2:2008 concerns room reverberation-time measurement. ISO 23591:2021, with its 2025 amendment, frames rehearsal-room acoustic criteria by music use and room size, and explicitly excludes sound-insulation criteria. A better room-decay result is an indoor result, not evidence of a quieter courtyard. [S05–S07](sources/index.md#measurement-and-standards-references)

### Airborne and structure-coupled hypotheses

The Løvholt et al. publisher abstract describes two-way fluid–structure modeling compared with laboratory tests of walls and walls with windows from 10–100 Hz. It reports that structural connections matter, with windows controlling transmission from 15–30 Hz and walls from 30–100 Hz. This supports coupled, frequency-dependent behavior; the aircraft/blast source context and laboratory assemblies do not predict this building. [S08](sources/index.md#s08)

The SINTEF abstract for Norén-Cosgriff et al. describes laboratory work, modeling, and full-scale testing in a typical Norwegian wooden building exposed to F-16 aircraft, including floor-vibration observations; it states that transmitted low-frequency noise excited floor vibration in that study. This supports recording vibration alongside sound when feasible. Its building, source and tested countermeasures differ from this unknown rehearsal building; its numerical outcomes do not transfer. [S09](sources/index.md#s09)

**Reviser verification of CI-01:** I reopened the SINTEF primary record and searched the exact Løvholt title/DOI. The direct ScienceDirect open returned an internal error, but the exact-title search returned the publisher-hosted abstract. Those records support air/structure interaction and the possibility of sound-induced vibration; they do not show that synchronized vibration alone identifies a structure-borne path here. The critic is right that the draft’s A–B–A source-level change alters excitation to all paths together.

### Rehearsal-room and implementation evidence

Vargas Vera et al. describe a residential-basement rehearsal-room case with bass guitar, guitar and drums, repeated source passages, enclosure measurements and modeled solutions; they explicitly leave verification of proposed solutions outside their paper. This is a useful workflow and source-profile lead, not evidence of successful installation or a complete path analysis. [S10](sources/index.md#s10)

The completed cases below are leads for questions, protocols and tradeoffs, not predictions for this site:

- **UBC Music Building renovation (BKL, completed 2022):** the consultant describes existing-floor and practice-room measurements, instrument tests, a construction inspection and structure-borne concerns in a multi-floor institutional renovation. Ask for the receiver/source measurement protocol, drawings, raw before/after records and which measured path informed each decision. The public case page reports an extensive $2.8M project but no raw or numeric after-results. [S13](sources/index.md#s13)
- **Oxford Clore Music Studios (opened 2019):** the consultant describes an environmental-noise survey used to set music-egress limits, then independent structures, wide-airspace windows, floated floors/box-in-box spaces and internal finishes. Ask how the survey point and resident/outdoor outcome were selected and whether post-opening measurements exist. It is a purpose-built college facility in a historic setting, with a construction scope outside this assignment; the case page publishes no numeric post-opening results. [S14](sources/index.md#s14)
- **Henry Wood Hall airbeds:** Shearer et al. report lab selection and installation of consumer airbeds in a former church hall, with before/after measurements aimed at lower-band reverberation for flexible smaller-ensemble/voice use. This is a reversible room-decay precedent, not an isolation or courtyard result. [S12](sources/index.md#s12)
- **Wind-band rehearsal-room redesign:** Støfringsdal and Bolstad describe a completed redesign for a 1,600 m³ professional wind-band room. Earlier 40 mm porous wall/ceiling absorbers lowered room strength, but musicians described a too-dry, imbalanced room and difficulty hearing each other, with reports of overplaying. This illustrates internal tradeoffs; scale, ensemble and outcome differ, and it does not establish exterior isolation. [S11](sources/index.md#s11)

## First information step and proposed pilot

Before measurement, the manager and residents should agree the relevant receiver experience, time window, safe and permitted courtyard point, privacy/data handling, and what would count as useful improvement. Ask whether the concern is audibility, low-frequency character, vibration/rattle, sleep disruption or another outcome. Do not substitute a legal threshold or an indoor room criterion.

Inventory the actual instruments and source settings. Seek available dated photos, room plans, equipment/panel inventories and user accounts of the old and present arrangements. Start an opt-in prospective log with start/end times, rehearsal and source categories, source settings, room/door/window/HVAC state and resident observations. Label recollection by source and confidence; it is not a historical before/after record.

**Pilot question:** does one no-cost source control produce a repeatable change at the agreed courtyard point while the rehearsal-room condition remains usable?

1. **A, comparison:** repeat a representative passage/session at its observed usual setting and source position. Record the actual settings rather than assuming them.
2. **B, trial:** repeat the same passage with one pre-agreed lower-output change only, such as the master level or bass control of an existing amplifier. Do not also move the source, change performers, rearrange panels or change doors/windows. If there is no amplified bass source, select a suitable control only after observing the actual inventory; the example does not establish that an amp exists.
3. **Repeat:** use A–B–A on comparable blocks and repeat enough to observe normal variation. Run separate no-rehearsal/quiet and, where relevant, bass/amp-only blocks. Log drums separately; if drum sessions are also a priority, test them in a separate comparison rather than bundling source changes.
4. **Observe both outcomes:** use synchronized clocks and the same checked measurement chain/settings at a fixed courtyard point and several fixed interior positions. Record instrument, microphone position/orientation, weighting and time settings, available frequency bands, calibration checks, background, missing samples and uncertainty. Low-frequency room readings can vary by position. A borrowed basic meter may be only a screen; if it cannot resolve the relevant bands or uncertainty, report that limit and seek suitable calibrated equipment or an acoustician. Use the recorder only as a qualitative event/time marker unless the full chain is calibrated.
5. **Optional vibration:** if accessible, permitted accelerometers can record floor/wall motion at marked points during the same blocks. They improve the evidence record but do not alone prove structure-borne transfer: airborne sound can also excite surfaces.
6. **Separate panel test:** only after the source comparison, hold the source conditions stable and compare the current movable-panel arrangement with one mapped alternate. Report the room-decay/comfort result separately from the courtyard result.

### Conditional path-discrimination branch added for CI-01

If the owner needs route attribution after the screening pilot, a qualified acoustician could propose a safe, non-invasive, permitted diagnostic. The critic’s proposed contrast is accepted with limits:

- compare an **airborne-dominant** test source, safely supported without deliberate rigid mechanical coupling to the floor or wall, with a **contact/impact-dominant** condition using an appropriate existing rehearsal source while any amplified source is silent;
- record the actual source condition and near-source airborne pressure, receiver-room and courtyard pressure, and vibration at accessible source-side and receiver-side structural points, all synchronized;
- match or document the source-room airborne level and spectrum as closely as feasible, and report bands and sensor limits;
- do not attach or install anything, alter the structure, use unsafe or damaging excitation, or proceed without permissions. If a safe comparable condition or suitable sensors are unavailable, leave path attribution unresolved.

This contrast may help show which responses follow a particular excitation, but it does not create a pure airborne or pure structural input. Airborne pressure can vibrate the structure; an impact also creates airborne sound; the building paths may coexist. A unique path claim would require suitable calibrated sensors, access and specialist analysis. If those conditions are not met, report the A–B–A as an outside-outcome screen only. This branch is proposed, not performed, and is not a prerequisite for the low-cost first pilot.

### Confounders and decision

Likely confounders include player effort and actual source level; source/player position; changing ensemble or occupancy; moving panels/furniture; doors, windows and HVAC; other building or courtyard activity; weather, wind and traffic; time-of-day propagation; unsynchronized instruments; microphone placement and room modes; meter bandwidth/calibration/dynamic range; phone processing; sparse timestamps; and a receiver metric not agreed in advance. Airborne and structural paths may coexist. Correlated vibration is evidence to investigate, not proof by itself.

Adopt the tested quiet source mode or resident-approved time window as a **reversible trial** only if the courtyard result changes repeatably beyond observed background and instrument variation, and the musicians accept the room condition. Agree the receiver outcome and decision threshold with residents before the pilot; no universal numeric limit is asserted. If only the room improves, retain a panel/source arrangement change for room comfort only. If outside results are unclear or sensor limits dominate, gather more matched blocks or commission a scoped measurement study before spending on treatment. If similar events persist in quiet/no-rehearsal periods, expand event/source logging before changing the rehearsal room.

## What would change the recommendation

- A prospective log ties events to a recurring non-rehearsal source: investigate that source before treating rehearsal-room acoustics.
- Residents prioritize night disturbance or vibration/rattle rather than outdoor sound level: revise receiver point, time window and observations before testing.
- A quiet source mode is not acceptable to musicians: consider an agreed time window or continue logging rather than imposing an unusable setting.
- A panel test improves room decay but not courtyard outcome: keep it only for room comfort.
- A repeatable outside result tracks one tested source condition: continue only that reversible, source-specific trial; do not infer results for other instruments or permanent treatment.
- Equipment bandwidth, calibration, dynamic range, synchronization or receiver access is inadequate: treat the result as screening and seek better equipment or a scoped specialist study.
- A controlled diagnostic cannot isolate the route with safe, permitted inputs and suitable measurements: state that attribution remains unresolved.
- Later drawings or measurements identify relevant geometry or structural continuity: reassess method and candidate path using that new evidence.
