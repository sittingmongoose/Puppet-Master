## Decision frame

The complaint anecdote supports several live hypotheses and does not identify one. “Bass” can describe airborne low-frequency pressure, vibration transmitted through a floor/wall and re-radiated, a combination, or an unrelated event that happened at the same time. The no-drumming evenings make it important to log amplified bass and other non-drum activity separately; they do not rule out rehearsal sound. No complaint count, source level, room geometry, construction, meter result, or before/after effect is available.

Keep two primary outcomes separate: (1) sound in the rehearsal room, including whether musicians can rehearse comfortably, and (2) sound at an agreed courtyard listening/measurement point. A treatment can change the first without materially changing the second. A phone recording, an uncalibrated phone SPL app, or a manufacturer's absorption figure cannot predict courtyard benefit.

## Mechanisms and what the literature supports

- **Airborne path:** sound from drums, bass/guitar amplification, voices, or other gear can pass through openings, a façade, or connected/flanking elements. ISO 16283-1:2014/Amd 1:2017 is a field method for airborne insulation *between rooms* (10–250 m³; 50 Hz–5 kHz); ISO 16283-3:2016 addresses airborne façade insulation over the same stated volume/frequency range. These are not courtyard ambient-noise rules, do not test structure-borne transfer, and start at 50 Hz, so they do not automatically cover the lowest bass. ISO 1996-2:2017 is an outdoor environmental sound-level determination framework and requires the measurement uncertainty to be reported; it does not provide a universal target for this site. Use these standards as method references; a meter and a few blocks do not by themselves constitute a standards-compliant test.

- **Structural coupling / re-radiation:** drum/kick impacts or a loudspeaker cabinet in contact with a floor or wall can excite a structure; the vibrating floor/wall can radiate sound elsewhere. A lab-validated fluid–structure model and experiments on walls/windows found structural connections mattered, with different wall/window contributions across 10–100 Hz (Løvholt et al., 2017). A full-scale Norwegian lightweight-building program combined laboratory work, modelling, and aircraft-noise field tests and measured floor vibration alongside indoor sound (Norén-Cosgriff et al., 2016). Both establish plausible mechanisms and measurement opportunities. Their aircraft sources, lightweight/Norwegian test building, assemblies, and interventions differ from this unknown rehearsal building; their numeric outcomes are not transferable. No structural work is proposed here.

- **Room modes and low-frequency readings:** measurements can change markedly by position in a small room. Pedersen, Møller & Persson Waye report up to 20–30 dB variation at low frequencies in simulations/three-room measurements and find that microphone-position methods can miss a complainant's high-level location. Therefore take repeatable readings at several fixed interior positions and preserve the map; do not infer a room-wide change from one indoor recorder. This evidence does not predict this room's modes without dimensions and measurements.

- **Reverberation is a separate room outcome:** ISO 3382-2:2008 gives a method family for reverberation time in ordinary rooms; ISO 23591:2021 (with Amd 1:2025) separates rehearsal-room criteria by music type and gives room-acoustic / room-size criteria. ISO 23591 explicitly does not include sound-insulation criteria. Neither a shorter room decay nor a flatter musician listening position proves less courtyard disturbance.

## What to learn from the room's change history

Reconstruct the move last winter before calling it a cause. Ask the manager and regular users for dates and photographs if available; record the previous and present positions of each instrument, amp/speaker, furniture, existing freestanding panels, doors/windows used, equipment isolation, occupancy and typical session. Note who remembers each fact, confidence, and contradictions. Compare only documented conditions. A current repeatable source-position A/B test may identify a useful operational setting, but it cannot recreate an undocumented “before” state.

A 2019 EAA paper about a bass/drums rehearsal room in a residential-house basement is the closest source-profile match found. It measured instrument source power, used repeated 20-second source passages, measured site airborne insulation, modelled candidate wall/ceiling solutions, and explicitly left verification of the proposed solutions out of scope. It is a useful workflow and question list, not evidence that a particular retrofit worked or that its design applies here.

For actual implementation histories, pursue two direct case records:

1. **BKL / UBC Music Building (2022):** consultant case history describes existing-floor and between-practice-room measurements, instrument tests on a pre-project visit, a construction inspection, concerns about structure-borne transmission, and extensive renovation of a 1968 multi-floor institutional building. Ask for the measurement protocol, drawings, before/after data, and which observed path drove each change. It is a real institutional renovation but differs from a small rehearsal building beside homes; the public case page supplies no underlying data or numeric after-results and reports a $2.8M project.
2. **Sandy Brown / Oxford Clore Music Studios (opened 2019):** consultant case history reports an area noise survey that set music-egress limits, then independent heavy structures, wide-airspace windows, floated floors/box-in-box practice spaces and internal treatment. Ask how the survey/receiver metric informed the design and whether post-opening measurements are available. This is a purpose-built multi-room facility in a historic college setting, with construction beyond this assignment's boundary; the page does not publish numeric performance outcomes.

As an internal-acoustics comparator, Støfringsdal & Bolstad (2023) describe a completed redesign for a professional wind band in a 1,600 m³ Norwegian rehearsal room; the preceding 40 mm wall/ceiling absorbers reduced room strength but musicians described the room as too dry, unbalanced, and difficult for mutual listening, with reports of overplaying. This warns that room treatment can trade one internal quality for another. The ensemble, room scale, and room-acoustic goal differ, and the paper does not establish exterior sound-isolation effects.

## Proposed first pilot (not executed)

**Question:** does one no-cost source control, at otherwise matched rehearsal conditions, make a repeatable difference at the courtyard point while keeping the room usable?

1. First agree with the manager/residents on a fixed courtyard listening/measurement position, relevant time windows, what “better” means to them, privacy/access, and any constraints. Ask whether their concern is audibility, low-frequency character, vibration/rattle, sleep disruption, or another outcome; do not substitute a legal threshold or a metric selected by the building alone.
2. Inventory current arrangement and sources. Keep source, passage, player, position, session duration, doors/windows/HVAC, and occupancy fixed. If amplified bass exists, run a bass/amp-only and a no-rehearsal/background control; the main comparison is repeated normal setting A versus one pre-agreed lower-output setting B, then A again (A–B–A, repeated on comparable blocks). Do not bundle lower bass, a new drum setup, and panels into one “quiet mode.” Add a separate drum block if drum complaints are also a priority.
3. Log synchronized time and source state. During each block, use the same checked sound-level meter and recorder positions inside the rehearsal room (several fixed positions, not a single point) and outside in the courtyard. Prefer frequency-band data with a known microphone/meter response and retain the meter's A/C/Z weighting and time settings; verify calibration and write down limitations. If the borrowed basic meter cannot characterize the complained-about low frequencies or the uncertainty, report only a screening result and rent a suitable calibrated meter/ask an acoustician. Recordings may label event timing qualitatively; do not treat their playback amplitude as calibrated SPL. If possible, add synchronized contact accelerometers at a floor/wall near the source and at a receiver-side structural point; these are proposed additions, not currently available equipment.
4. Repeat each matched condition enough to see normal variation; include comparable quiet periods and log traffic, voices, weather/wind, HVAC, other rehearsal activity, doors and windows. Ask residents for time-stamped observations without asking them to diagnose the path. Keep measurements of rehearsal-room level/decay and courtyard level as separate result columns. Report the frequency bands, positions, condition, background, instrument, calibration, uncertainty and missing samples.
5. **Decision:** if B produces a repeatable courtyard change larger than observed background/instrument variation and the musicians still accept the room result, trial that reversible source mode or time window as the low-cost first response. If the room improves but courtyard does not, keep it only for room comfort and do not buy room absorption as a courtyard fix. If the courtyard response is unclear, measurement limits dominate, or vibration remains synchronized while airborne readings do not separate, do not choose construction; escalate to a scoped path study. If the noise persists in matched no-rehearsal controls, expand source/event logging and investigate unrelated/background sources before changing rehearsal-room treatment.

**Likely confounders:** unknown session/source level and player effort; one-time room occupancy; simultaneous movement of source/panels; different door/window or HVAC state; other music or impact events; courtyard traffic and wind; non-coincident interior/exterior meters; meter bandwidth/calibration, AGC/phone processing and positioning; weather/time-of-day propagation; floor/wall contact; sparse/no complaint timestamps; the neighbor's preferred outcome/receiver point not yet known. Correlated vibration and sound support a structure-path hypothesis but are not alone proof, since airborne sound can also excite surfaces and paths can coexist.
