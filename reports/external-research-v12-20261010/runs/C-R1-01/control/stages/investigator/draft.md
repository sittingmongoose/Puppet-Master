## Recommendation in brief

There is not enough evidence to select a cause or justify new treatment. The defensible first move is a short prospective event/session log followed, if access and equipment permit, by a repeated, matched comparison of one reversible source-level control while sound is recorded at both a fixed courtyard point and several fixed positions in the rehearsal room. Include quiet/no-rehearsal periods. If an accelerometer can be borrowed, record structure vibration at the same times; a basic sound meter alone cannot reliably distinguish structural coupling from airborne sound.

For a no-cost first intervention, test one lower-output operating mode for an existing amplified source, or negotiate a quiet-mode/time window that residents say would help. Keep the source, passage, player, position, room state, and other performers constant during the measurement comparison. Treat temporary movement of the existing freestanding panels as a separate room-acoustics comparison. Neither the source comparison nor a panel test is a renovation design.

This sequence can establish whether a reversible option changes the receiver outcome under the tested conditions. It cannot certify a cause, a future retrofit, or a regulatory result. The recommendation changes if prospective timestamps identify a non-rehearsal source, if residents define a different useful outcome/time, or if instrument capability/access is too limited to resolve the relevant bands.

## Situation and evidence boundary

The supplied story describes intermittent complaints, some coincident with drumming and one report of worse evenings with no drumming. Rooms were rearranged last winter without a reliable before/after log. A recorder and basic sound meter might be borrowed later; existing freestanding panels can be moved temporarily. These are hypothetical facts, not measurements. No floor plan, dimensions, materials, complaints/timestamps, neighbor metric, access, or verified source is available.

Bass-frequency pressure can excite room modes, pass through airborne paths, and/or excite the building structure. Those mechanisms can coexist. A floor or wall can vibrate and re-radiate sound; synchronized vibration is useful evidence but is not conclusive alone because airborne sound may also excite surfaces. The no-drumming anecdote does not rule out amplified bass or another rehearsal source, and it does not establish that a rehearsal source caused the event.


```
rehearsal source and setting
   ├─ airborne room sound ─ openings/façade/flanking ─ courtyard sound
   └─ contact / structural vibration ─ floor or wall motion ─ re-radiated sound
other building/courtyard event ──────────────────────────────── courtyard sound
room modes and reverberation ─ affect rehearsal-room sound and measurements
```

The sketch is a list of paths to investigate, not a finding that any path exists here.

Separate the results from the outset:

- **Rehearsal-room outcome:** sound at representative musician/listener positions, including room decay and practical rehearsal comfort.
- **Courtyard outcome:** sound at the residents’ agreed place/time and their separate time-stamped experience of audibility, low-frequency character, rattling or disturbance.

A reduction in the first is not evidence of reduction in the second. A phone recording is useful only as a qualitative timing/event marker unless its full calibrated measurement chain is known; phone playback level and consumer microphone processing are not a validated SPL comparison. Do not infer courtyard benefit from a manufacturer absorption claim.

## What primary sources do and do not establish

The source records and exact versions are in source-map.json.

1. **Outdoor and room measurement scope [S01–S04].** ISO 1996-2:2017 is an outdoor environmental sound-level determination framework; it allows different measurement effort and requires the associated uncertainty to be reported. It provides no universal maximum uncertainty or local complaint target. ISO 16283-1:2014/Amd 1:2017 addresses airborne insulation between rooms; its stated range is 10–250 m³ and 50 Hz–5 kHz. ISO 16283-3:2016 addresses airborne façade insulation over a stated 10–250 m³ / 50 Hz–5 kHz range and is marked for revision. Neither substitutes for a courtyard ambient-noise record or a structural-vibration measurement. ISO 10052:2021 is a simplified survey method using a handheld sound-level instrument and manual microphone sweep, with greater uncertainty than engineering methods; it is a screen, not a shortcut to a formal report. **Inference for this case:** first agree the actual receiver outcome and log uncertainty; use a specialist if low-frequency bandwidth or attribution is decisive.

2. **Internal room acoustics [S05–S06].** ISO 3382-2:2008 describes ordinary-room reverberation-time measurement. ISO 23591:2021, with its 2025 amendment, distinguishes amplified, quiet acoustic, and loud acoustic rehearsal uses and provides room-acoustic/size criteria; it explicitly excludes sound insulation. **Inference:** ask what music the space serves and measure room dimensions before comparing in-room results with a room criterion. Do not use a rehearsal-room RT target as a neighbor-noise target.

3. **Low-frequency position effects [S07].** Pedersen, Møller and Persson Waye (2007) report large low-frequency pressure variation by position (up to 20–30 dB in their simulations and three-room measurements) and show why one location can miss another high-level location. This supports several marked interior positions and repeatability; it does not assign that variation to this unmeasured room.

4. **Air/structure interaction [S08–S09].** Løvholt et al. (2017) compare a two-way fluid-structure model with wall/window experiments over 10–100 Hz; their abstract reports that structural connections matter and that the wall/window balance differs by band. Norén-Cosgriff et al. (2016) pair lab/model work with full-scale field tests in a Norwegian lightweight building under aircraft noise and measure floor vibration as well as indoor sound. **Supported conclusion:** both routes are credible and both sound and vibration can be measured. **Limit:** the source spectra, structures, and mitigation differ; do not transfer their numeric effects or select a building treatment from them.

5. **Relevant room/source workflow [S10].** Vargas Vera et al. (2019) report on a residential-house-basement rehearsal room with bass guitar, guitar and drums. They measured/modelled source and airborne insulation information using repeated musical passages and proposed solutions; they explicitly left verification of those solutions outside the paper’s scope. This is close in source/use context and a useful workflow reference, but it is not proof of a successful installation and does not cover every path here.

6. **Implementation histories and alternatives.**
   - **S13:** The UBC Music Building acoustician’s case record (2022 renovation) reports pre-project measurements across practice rooms and existing floors, instrument tests on a site visit, construction inspection, and a structure-borne concern in a multi-floor institutional building. Ask for its measurement protocol, design details, and before/after data. The project was an extensive $2.8M institutional renovation; the public page gives no raw or numeric after-results. It is a real history to investigate, not a performance prediction for this small residential-edge site.
   - **S14:** The Oxford Clore Music Studios consultant case (opened 2019) reports an environmental-noise survey used to set music-egress limits, then independent heavy structures, wide-airspace windows, floated floors/box-in-box practice spaces, and room treatment. Ask how the receiver and survey informed those limits and whether post-opening data exist. It was a purpose-built multiroom college facility and includes construction outside this assignment; the public case does not report numeric post-opening performance.
   - **S11:** Støfringsdal & Bolstad (2023) describe a completed redesign in a 1,600 m³ professional wind-band room. The prior 40 mm porous absorbers reduced room strength but musicians described the room as too dry/imbalanced and reported difficulty hearing one another and overplaying. This real history cautions that a room-only intervention can trade internal qualities; it is not evidence about courtyard transmission or a comparable room size/source.
   - **S12:** Shearer et al. (2021) tested consumer airbeds and then installed them in Henry Wood Hall, a former church used for orchestral rehearsal. Their before/after room measurements found lower-band reverberation-time reduction for a modest-budget flexible use. This is an unfamiliar, reversible room-decay idea to ask about if the rehearsal room itself needs adjustment. It targeted reverberation, not exterior isolation or neighbor disturbance.
   
These case histories are authored by the organizations/designers involved or are implementation papers. They are more useful as leads for measurement details, commissioning records and lessons than as independent proof that an option will work here.

## Comparison and observation design

### First information step

Before the intervention, agree with residents and the manager on:

- the actual receiver/outcome (for example, audibility, low-frequency character, rattle/vibration, or disturbance during a time window);
- a fixed, safe, permitted courtyard point and who may access it;
- acceptable rehearsal changes and any privacy/data-retention conditions;
- whether the basic equipment can record the relevant low-frequency range with known calibration.

Start a timestamped prospective complaint/session log if people can report safely and voluntarily: start/end time, rehearsal/source categories, room/door/window state, and resident observation. A report can identify event timing, but residents are not asked to diagnose airborne versus structural paths.

### Proposed pilot and comparison

**Question:** does one no-cost reduction in an adjustable source state create a repeatable change at the resident-defined courtyard point while retaining an acceptable rehearsal-room condition?

- **A — comparison:** one agreed representative music passage/session at the usual operating setting and source position, logged rather than assumed.
- **B — trial:** repeat the same passage with one lower-output change only, such as the existing bass amplifier’s master level or bass control. Do not simultaneously move the amp, rearrange panels, substitute drums, or change doors/windows.
- **Sequence:** A–B–A on repeated comparable blocks; log actual settings and sound source. A–B–A checks whether background/time drift could explain a before/after difference. If the site has no amplified bass source, the team must select the adjustable source from observed session inventory; this amp example does not authorize assuming one is present.
- **Context controls:** run separate no-rehearsal/quiet periods and bass/amp-only periods if relevant. Log drumming separately because the account includes both drum and no-drum complaints. If drum events are also a target, test those in a separate pilot rather than mixing source changes into this comparison.
- **Measure:** synchronously record the room at several marked positions and the fixed courtyard point with the same meter, time settings and orientation; retain available frequency-band and A/C/Z data. Verify calibration and record meter limits. Use the borrowed recorder as a qualitative event/time record, not as calibrated SPL. If structure-coupling is a decision-critical question, add synchronized contact accelerometers at accessible floor/wall points; otherwise describe the mechanism as unresolved.
- **Room-only alternative:** after the source comparison, hold sources constant and compare the present movable-panel arrangement against one documented alternate arrangement. Record the indoor result and courtyard result separately. This tests room comfort/decay and any measured outside change; it is not an “isolation” test.
- **Path follow-up:** only if results justify it and access permits, a qualified acoustician can design a low-frequency-capable source/receiver survey of openings/façade and structure. ISO method scopes should be checked against the actual room volume and frequency; the 50 Hz lower bounds of ISO 16283-1/-3 do not resolve all bass content.

### Likely confounders and limits

Musician/player effort, amp setting not held, changing source/player positions, ensemble mix, occupancy, panels or furniture moved together, doors/windows/HVAC state, unrelated courtyard/building activity, wind/weather, traffic, time-of-day propagation, asynchronous clocks/meters, microphone placement, low-frequency room modes, meter bandwidth/calibration, phone AGC, sparse complaint timestamps, and a receiver metric not agreed in advance. Airborne and structural paths may coexist. Correlated floor vibration is evidence to investigate, not standalone proof; a recorder alone cannot provide a validated transmission path.

### Decision tied to the receiver outcome

Adopt the tested quiet source mode or a resident-approved time window as a **reversible low-cost trial** only if the courtyard metric changes repeatably by more than the observed background/instrument variation and any equipment uncertainty, and the musicians judge the indoor result usable. No universal numeric limit is supplied; the success threshold must be agreed before the pilot.

If only the rehearsal-room result improves, retain a panel/source arrangement change only for room comfort; do not represent it as reduced courtyard disturbance. If an outside change appears without a stable pattern, collect more matched blocks or a better baseline before spending. If structure vibration is synchronized but meters cannot separate the paths, ask for a targeted professional measurement rather than infer or build. If similar events persist in quiet/no-rehearsal periods, expand source and background logging before changing the room.

## What could change the recommendation

- **Prospective logs identify a recurring non-rehearsal event:** investigate that event/source and avoid using music treatment as the first pilot.
- **Residents prioritize night disturbance or vibration/rattle rather than outdoor SPL:** change the time window, receiver point, subjective diary and/or contact-vibration measure before comparing options.
- **Low output has no acceptable rehearsal form:** prefer negotiated timing, source relocation only as a separate measured condition, or a no-cost log first.
- **An existing panel test changes only room decay:** stop treating panels as an exterior solution; they may still help musicians.
- **A repeatable exterior change tracks one source condition:** continue a reversible quiet-mode/time pilot for that source; do not infer an untested instrument or permanent treatment result.
- **Basic-meter response/calibration cannot resolve the frequency of concern, or access blocks receiver observations:** the first defensible deliverable may remain a log or a scoped consultant measurement, depending on owner budget.
- **The receiver's desired location/time cannot be established:** do not declare success or failure from a convenient room-only proxy.
- **Drawings/measurements later show a relevant geometry or structure:** re-evaluate the candidate path and applicable method against that new evidence; this proposal makes no present structural assumption.
