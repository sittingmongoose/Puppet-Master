# Research proposal: low-frequency noise in a shared rehearsal building

Case: ER12-C-R1-01-FRESH · Run: C-R1-01-treatment · Stage: reviser

## Purpose and provisional direction

The situation supplied is hypothetical: bass complaints are intermittent, some occur during drum sessions, and one tenant reports the worst evenings without drumming; rooms were rearranged last winter without a reliable before-and-after log; a recorder, basic sound meter, and freestanding panels may be available. These facts motivate an investigation. They do not establish complaint frequency, measured sound, source, building material, transmission path, or cause.

Before spending on treatment, start with an event-linked baseline at two distinct outcomes—the courtyard receptor and the rehearsal room—then, if informative and safe sessions can be matched, compare normal operation with one modest, voluntary source or scheduling change. This is low-cost and reversible, and can show whether a source-linked change affects the courtyard outcome even while the path remains unknown. If events cannot be linked reliably to session conditions, begin with a complaint-and-session log and collect representative no-session periods before testing an intervention. Move the existing freestanding panels only as a separate room-acoustics comparison. An indoor change alone never establishes courtyard benefit. No construction, permanent installation, structural alteration, enforcement, or renovation design is proposed.

## What the evidence can and cannot establish

The serious explanations are compatible and may coexist:

1. **Airborne radiation or leakage.** Drums, bass amplifiers, speakers, or other sources can excite room air; sound may leave through openings, façade or roof elements, or flanking routes and arrive in the courtyard. Airborne sound can also excite a partition that radiates into another room. Room modes and source/listener positions can change indoor bass response. Reduced room reverberation is not the same as increased sound isolation.
2. **Structure-borne coupling and re-radiation.** A drum foot/pedal, instrument, speaker, or other vibrating equipment may excite a floor or connected construction. Surfaces may carry vibration and radiate sound at a receiver. This can coexist with airborne transmission. Interior sound level by itself does not identify the path.
3. **Other or mixed events.** An event on a no-drum or no-session evening could involve another rehearsal source, building plant, traffic, courtyard activity, neighboring activity, or another unobserved source. These are questions for an event log, not asserted facts about this building.

Standards and studies offer bounded methods, not a diagnosis or effect-size prediction for this building. ISO 16283-1 and ISO 16283-3 concern specified airborne insulation measurements between rooms or across façades; they are not general structural-path discriminators. ISO 12354-1 is an airborne between-room calculation that depends on element/junction data and only extends toward 50 Hz when those data exist. No such building data were supplied. ISO 1996 describes environmental-noise quantities and measurement but does not set a universal limit for this hypothetical site. Local requirements and a relevant descriptor remain owner decisions until jurisdiction, receptor, and resident-valued outcome are known. See source IDs [STD-1996-1], [STD-1996-2], [STD-16283-1], [STD-16283-3], and [STD-12354] in the [source map](source-map.json).

Low-frequency response can be spatially uneven. Hopkins and Turner’s publisher abstract supports multiple corner and central microphone positions for more repeatable airborne room-average estimates at 50, 63, and 80 Hz in its stated small-room context; it does not establish this building’s modes or a courtyard metric [STUDY-HOPKINS]. Song and Kang’s abstract reports location/area effects in particular coupled dwelling layouts with a steady source, not this building [STUDY-SONG]. The borrowed meter’s model, low-frequency response, weighting/band options, time response, range/noise floor, logging, and calibration status are unknown and must be checked before its readings are treated as quantitative. A recorder can help identify timing or event character, but an unverified or uncalibrated recording is not a validated sound level or courtyard-benefit prediction.

FHWA-HEP-18-065 says interior sound can be difficult to separate into airborne and groundborne contributions; it describes groundborne noise as commonly inferred from measured vibration and emphasizes suitable transducer location, attachment, and frequency range. Its procedures and criteria are highway-specific and must not be imported as a rehearsal-building limit or path verdict [GUIDE-FHWA]. In particular, **no detected vibration is not evidence for an airborne path** unless a suitable synchronized vibration channel was available, correctly coupled and placed on a plausible receiving surface, and sensitive over the relevant bands and event period. Even then, non-detection only weighs against the tested structural route under those conditions; it does not rule out other coupled routes or mixed transmission.

## Options and when they are useful

| Reversible option | Mechanism and role | Limitation or evidence that would change priority |
|---|---|---|
| Lower a bass/overall source setting, or agree on a quieter time window | Changes source operation at low cost and can help before the path is known. | Does not identify path and cannot explain no-session events. Retain only if repeated courtyard observations and resident feedback move as intended while rehearsal remains usable. Test one change at a time. |
| Move equipment away from a suspected floor/wall coupling point | Changes source location and possible mechanical/acoustic coupling or room-mode excitation. | No coupling point is known. Keep source output, performer, passage, and other geometry stable; otherwise placement is confounded with performance. |
| Reposition existing freestanding panels | Can alter in-room reflections/modal response and may help rehearsal quality. | It is not a façade barrier. Measure courtyard and room separately. A room-only improvement supports an internal-acoustics benefit, not reduced outdoor disturbance. |
| Targeted low-frequency absorption, including flexible concepts | A band-specific absorber may change room response. Shearer et al. report reduced low-frequency reverberation time after airbeds were studied and installed in Henry Wood Hall. | This is internal room conditioning, not neighbor isolation. First identify the relevant room response; do not infer courtyard benefit from absorption claims [CASE-AIRBED]. |
| Temporary source-to-floor isolation comparison, only if evidence justifies it | A correctly loaded temporary support could test a structural-coupling hypothesis without changing the building. | Requires appropriate vibration sensors, safe support, and actual load/frequency conditions; do not select a material or thickness from a brochure. Gjers’ drum-classroom results were slab- and frequency-dependent with placement/contact caveats [CASE-GJERS]. The SR 11 sheet is load-, shape-factor-, frequency-, and amplitude-dependent and is not a performance guarantee [PROD-SYLOMER]. This is a future test possibility, not a product selection or installation recommendation. |
| Complaint-and-session log | Low-cost first deliverable when events are intermittent or attribution is weak; identifies periods worth instrumenting. | Does not quantify a path or level. Continue until representative rehearsal and no-session periods are captured; do not infer causality from one coincident report. |

## Proposed pilot

### A. Agree on the receptor, outcome, and access

Ask affected residents what matters to them—rumble, thud/rattle, audibility, sleep interruption, or another response—and where/when they notice it. Agree a repeatable courtyard receptor point and a separate in-room reference point, mark both on a sketch, and establish access/privacy boundaries for measurements, event notes, and any recording. If neighbor-property access is unavailable, use an authorized reachable boundary point and state the limitation. Identify the primary reported quantity and analysis bands after checking resident priorities, local requirements, and instrument capability; do not nominate a universal threshold.

### B. Event-linked baseline and instrument qualification

Across representative rehearsal and no-rehearsal periods, log UTC start/end; room and session state; drums, bass, or other amplified sources; source position/settings; doors/windows; panel arrangement; HVAC/plant; obvious traffic/courtyard events; weather/wind; operator actions; and complaint timing/character if reported. Preserve quiet/no-session periods. Mark masked, contaminated, clipped, or otherwise unrepresentative intervals as unresolved.

Before measurement, identify device/model, microphone response, weighting and band capability, time response, range/noise floor, logging, calibration status, and clock behavior. Record settings, calibration checks before/after, microphone position/height/orientation, event/source description, weather, site sketch, and deviations. Fix microphone positions and settings across comparisons. If low-frequency response, calibration, or background separation is inadequate, borrow a suitable analyzer rather than stretch the data. Preserve raw project measurement files and field notes under the project’s applicable records process; no raw evidence was created in this desk review.

The preferred setup has synchronized suitable quantitative sound channels at the courtyard and inside. If only one quantitative meter is available, use it at the courtyard for repeated matched source conditions and use a fixed recorder only for event context; repeat comparable blocks with the verified meter indoors to assess the separate room outcome. Since those readings would not be simultaneous, call them matched but lower-confidence observations. Do not calculate same-event transmission loss or use the recorder as calibrated indoor SPL. If repeatable passages or sessions cannot be obtained, prioritize the event log and call the intervention comparison inconclusive.

### C. Reversible comparison

Keep the current panel arrangement and compare repeated, similar passages under two conditions: **A, reference:** normal agreed source operation; **B, source condition:** one modest agreed bass/overall reduction *or* a practical agreed time window. Do not combine a source change and time-window change. If room benefit is also wanted, perform a separate crossover: **C, room condition:** reference source operation with the existing panels moved to one marked alternative arrangement. Counterbalance order where practical and repeat comparable blocks enough to estimate within-session variability.

Hold players, passage, instruments, source placement, settings except the intended source change, doors/windows, HVAC, duration, microphone positions/settings, and panel arrangement (except in C) as constant as feasible. Log departures rather than smoothing them away. Likely confounders include changing performer force, room use, source location, session mix, plant state, open doors/windows, wind/weather, traffic/courtyard activity, mic placement, unsynchronized clocks, background masking, and non-simultaneous indoor/outdoor measurements. Compare repeated changes against background and measurement uncertainty. Do not select a pass threshold after seeing treatment outcomes.

### D. What observations can distinguish

- A courtyard pattern that repeatedly tracks logged source operation and changes under B supports a source-linked contribution; it does not alone distinguish airborne leakage from structural coupling.
- An event during no rehearsal or one that does not covary with recorded sessions redirects work toward other source identification. It does not by itself identify the other source.
- Sound-only readings cannot establish the path. To investigate a structural route, add synchronized surface-vibration measurements using suitable accelerometers/geophones, a known frequency range, sound measurements, controlled source operation/location, and firmly coupled sensors at justified source/receiver surfaces. Treat non-detection as informative only for the routes and bands adequately covered; otherwise retain uncertainty.
- Several patterns may occur together. If background, wind, traffic, or an unrepeatable performance masks the event, mark it unresolved and gather another comparable observation.

### E. Decision the pilot can support

Use the resident-defined courtyard outcome as the primary external decision, with repeatability/background uncertainty. Keep room results separate:

- If B produces a repeated meaningful courtyard change and indoor rehearsal remains acceptable, retain that reversible source/schedule adjustment while the path remains under study.
- If C improves the room outcome but the courtyard result does not change beyond uncertainty, treat it as an internal room-acoustics benefit only.
- If controlled source/location changes alter adequately measured vibration and the receiver outcome, seek a focused structural-path assessment before considering any building work.
- If disturbance persists in no-session periods, expand event identification and the log rather than buying room treatment.
- If results are masked, inconsistent, below useful measurement capability, or sensor-limited, make no efficacy claim; improve instrumentation/repeatability or consult an acoustician.

These are proposed checks and decision rules, not a universal decibel threshold. Whether any change is meaningful must be tied to the resident’s stated outcome and adequate measurement quality. No pilot, site, instrument, or product check has been executed in this research.

## Room rearrangement and implementation history to investigate

Treat last winter’s room rearrangement as a causal lead, not a cause. Seek dated former/current plans, photos, room-use/schedule records, equipment inventories, work orders, panel specifications, and incident notes if they exist. Map source and panel positions, doors/windows, adjacent rooms and surfaces, HVAC/plant state, and authorized measurement points. Ask whether source position/settings, operating hours, maintenance, repairs, or other building work changed at the same time. Distinguish dated records or measured comparisons from retrospective recall; do not reconstruct a nonexistent “before” measurement.

Three reported implementations inform questions, not predictions:

- **Emslie and Hanson, AAS 2008 private drum studio** [CASE-EMSLIE]. A free-standing isolated studio was built inside a new garage extension after disruptive upstairs drumming. The non-peer-reviewed paper describes boundary background/drumming observations at two neighbor-side positions and later outside measurements after construction, plus a separate indoor family-room outcome. It discusses isolation, cavity absorption, damping, glazing, doors, ventilation, seals, gaps, vibration bridges, and a roof that limited façade performance; a heavier block-wall concept was abandoned after slab-capacity concerns. Crucially, the pre/post boundary evidence is **not a same-position, same-source matched before-and-after experiment**: the paper reports a 64 dBA level at a front boundary and a nominal 47.5 dBA result at an elevated deck at the rear of the neighbor’s property. It is a history to mine for tested details and limits, not a target, design, or expected benefit here.
- **Gjers, AAS 2024 drum/classroom case** [CASE-GJERS]. The commercial school classroom was above a work/library receiver and included 180 mm and 450 mm concrete slab zones. Sound meters and accelerometers were used. A limited kick/snare/tom component set on a small SR11 platform produced a reported 5 dB Dw change in the 180 mm comparison; source setup differed in the thicker-slab comparison, placements/contact points changed, vibration included amplification and reduction bands, and authors called for more investigation. It motivates controlling geometry and pairing sound/vibration if later indicated; it is not an outdoor-neighbor test or product guarantee.
- **Shearer et al., Henry Wood Hall airbeds** [CASE-AIRBED]. This peer-reviewed study concerned flexible low-frequency reverberation control in a former church/orchestral hall. It reports laboratory airbed screening/inflation work and lower low-frequency reverberation time after installation. It supports an internal-acoustics alternative to screen if wanted, not façade isolation or courtyard benefit.

## Critique dispositions

The independent critique broadly found the draft faithful to the brief and open plan. That agreement is evidence to consider, not authority. I checked the two specific findings against their primary records and make these dispositions:

1. **Material wrong:** none identified in the checked consequential claims. **Disposition: accept.** The cited methods/cases remain bounded examples; none establishes a building-specific cause or benefit.
2. **Material incomplete—interpreting absent vibration:** **Disposition: accept and amend.** FHWA §7.0 states that indoor sound often cannot distinguish airborne from groundborne contributions; §§7.1.2 and 17.2 discuss transducer location, attachment/coupling, and low-frequency capability. The supplied recorder/basic meter do not establish that a suitable vibration channel exists. The diagnostic text above therefore treats absent vibration as inconclusive unless synchronized, suitable, correctly placed/coupled measurements cover the event and relevant bands; even a valid non-detection is limited to the tested routes/conditions [GUIDE-FHWA].
3. **Unsupported site claims:** none identified. **Disposition: accept the finding.** No cause, measurement, material, complaint count, product outcome, local limit, or predicted courtyard benefit is asserted.
4. **Minor wording—Emslie comparison:** **Disposition: accept and amend.** Direct inspection of the AAS2008 PDF confirms the “not peer reviewed” notice and the separately described boundary and elevated-deck measurements. The result must not be read as matched before/after evidence. The implementation section states that explicitly and preserves its limited use as a history of details to investigate [CASE-EMSLIE].
5. **External inputs still unresolved:** **Disposition: retain uncertainty.** No floor plan/dimensions, construction/junction details, observation/access permissions, complaint timestamps, resident-valued metric, meter/recorder model/capability, or jurisdiction/local requirement was supplied. The released plan also leaves the relevant frequency range, tolerable interference with sessions, and budget open. These gaps prevent a site diagnosis, selected compliance metric, sensor qualification, path calculation, or efficacy prediction; no budget, frequency range, or session interruption is assumed. Resolve them only through authorized project inputs, not assumptions.
6. **Positive coverage assessment and reviewer handoff:** **Disposition: accept with the amendments above.** Preserve separate outcomes, source/schedule and room/path alternatives, the log, the room-change investigation, implementation histories, pilot confounders, and conditional decisions. The pilot and any later engineering survey remain proposals.
