# Research proposal: low-frequency noise in a shared rehearsal building

## Purpose and provisional direction

This proposal is for a defensible, reversible investigation before spending on treatment. The supplied situation is hypothetical: there are intermittent bass complaints, including some evenings without drumming; rooms were rearranged last winter; and no reliable before/after record exists. Those facts motivate testing several explanations. They do not verify a source, path, material, measured level, or dominant cause.

A useful first step is an event-linked baseline paired with a small reversible source comparison. It can test whether courtyard disturbance follows rehearsal operation and whether a modest source/schedule adjustment is useful. If the reported timing cannot be matched to session conditions, a complaint-and-session log is the right first deliverable until informative events are captured. In parallel, move the existing freestanding panels only as a separate room-acoustics comparison. Do not treat an indoor change as evidence of an outdoor benefit. No permanent treatment, construction, enforcement, or building alteration is proposed.

## Causal model and serious hypotheses

Think in terms of excitation → path → receiver → frequency content and perception:

1. **Airborne sound:** drums, bass amplifiers, speakers, or other sources excite room air. Sound can leave through openings or façade/roof elements and reach the courtyard. Airborne sound may also vibrate a partition and radiate into another room. Room modes and source/listener position can change what is heard indoors. A measured decrease in room reverberation is not the same as greater sound isolation or lower courtyard sound.
2. **Structure-borne coupling:** a drum foot/pedal, instrument, speaker, or other vibrating equipment may excite a floor or connected construction. Energy can travel through more than one structural route and re-radiate as sound at a receiver. It can coexist with airborne transmission. An indoor sound-level reading by itself does not separate the two; the FHWA measurement handbook notes this ambiguity and describes surface-mounted vibration transducers [GUIDE-FHWA].
3. **Other or mixed events:** some complaints may track a source other than drumming or may occur independently of rehearsal. Other rehearsal instruments, building plant, traffic, courtyard activity, or neighboring activity are questions for an event log, not claims about this site. Mixed sources and paths remain possible.

The room arrangement change is a specific lead to investigate. Seek old and current layouts, dates, photos, and records; mark source positions, panels, doors, windows, HVAC/plant state, and adjacency. Ask whether source positions, operating hours, maintenance, or other work changed at the same time. Such concurrent changes could make a before/after improvement look attributable to one factor when it was not.

Existing research provides methods and boundary conditions, not this building’s answer. ISO 16283-1:2014 addresses field measurement of airborne insulation between rooms, 50–5000 Hz and 10–250 m³, and includes non-diffuse fields; the ISO record lists Amendment 1:2017 and confirmed the base edition in 2025 [STD-16283-1]. ISO 16283-3:2016 concerns airborne façade insulation in its stated 50–5000 Hz and room-volume scope, but its lifecycle status was “to be revised” when checked [STD-16283-3]. Neither is a general method for separating structural coupling. ISO 12354-1 models airborne between-room performance using direct/flanking element data; its extension to 50 Hz is conditional on element and junction data, which are absent here [STD-12354]. These are possible later specialist methods, not checks performed in this research.

Low-frequency response may vary spatially. Hopkins and Turner’s field-measurement study proposes corner and central microphone positions for more repeatable airborne room-average estimates at 50, 63, and 80 Hz; it does not establish this building’s modes or a courtyard limit [STUDY-HOPKINS]. The borrowed sound meter’s model, weighting, band resolution, microphone response, time response, dynamic range, logging and calibration status must be checked before using its values. A recorder can document timing/event character if appropriate, but without suitable calibration and frequency response it cannot stand in for a sound-level measurement or predict courtyard benefit.

## Options to compare

Promote only if repeated courtyard observations and resident feedback respond while rehearsal needs remain acceptable. |
| Move equipment away from a suspected floor/wall coupling point | Changes the source’s mechanical/acoustic coupling and its location relative to room modes, if the layout permits. | Source location is unknown; must be tested with repeatable placement and source operation. ## Proposed first pilot

### 1. Agree the receptor and outcome

Before collecting data, ask affected residents what matters: rumble, thud/rattle, audibility, sleep or another effect; where and when they notice it; and what outcome would count as a meaningful improvement to them. Confirm permissions and privacy boundaries for courtyard access, room access, logging and any recordings. Identify applicable local requirements only after jurisdiction is established; no universal threshold is selected here.

Choose and mark a repeatable courtyard measurement location at the agreed listening/receptor point, plus a repeatable in-room reference location. Map positions on a sketch. Do not assume access to a neighbor’s property; if access is unavailable, name the reachable boundary point and state the resulting limitation.

### 2. Capture event and background context

For representative rehearsal and no-rehearsal periods, log UTC start/end, room use, whether drums/bass/other amplified sources were used, source position and settings, doors/windows, panel arrangement, HVAC/plant, obvious courtyard/traffic events, weather/wind, operator actions and any complaint/report timing and character. Use a fixed recorder only as an event/context record unless its measurement chain is suitable and calibrated. Include quiet periods and “no session” periods rather than assuming all nighttime bass is from the rehearsal building.

For quantitative values, verify the meter’s low-frequency and weighting/band capability, time response, microphone orientation, range/noise floor, calibration and clock. Record the device/model, settings, calibration check before and after, mic position/height/orientation, and contaminated or unrepresentative intervals. Preserve raw files and field notes. Choose any principal reported quantity and analysis bands before examining treatment effects; retain frequency-specific data if available. State measurement uncertainty and background masking. If the meter cannot resolve the frequency or level of interest, borrow a suitable analyzer rather than stretching the data.

### 3. Use a repeatable reversible comparison

First compare repeated, similar source passages under:

- **A — reference:** normal agreed source operation and current panel layout;
- **B — source condition:** one modest, agreed bass/overall level reduction, or an agreed time window if timing is the practical option, with other conditions held steady;
- **C — room condition:** reference source operation with the existing freestanding panels moved to one marked alternate layout.

Counterbalance the order where practical and repeat matched passages enough to estimate within-session variability. Keep players, passage, instruments, source locations, master/source controls, doors/windows, HVAC, panel condition (except in C), mic positions/settings and measurement duration as constant as feasible. Log deviations rather than smoothing them away. Do not combine a source change and panel move in one comparison; that would obscure which change mattered.

The preferred arrangement uses quantitative sound-level channels at the courtyard and inside at the same time. The supplied gear is only a recorder and a basic meter, with capabilities unspecified. If a second suitable meter/logger is not available, put the verified sound-level meter at the courtyard point during matched A/B/C trials, use the fixed recorder for event timing only, and repeat comparable blocks with the meter indoors to measure the separate rehearsal-room outcome. Because those inside/outside readings would not be simultaneous, report them as matched but lower-confidence comparisons; do not calculate a same-event transmission loss or use the recorder as calibrated indoor SPL. If repeatable passages or conditions cannot be obtained, prioritize the event log and treat the intervention comparison as inconclusive.

### 4. Distinguish paths without assuming one

- A courtyard level/spectrum that repeatedly tracks source operation and changes with a controlled source adjustment supports a source-linked contribution, but does not alone identify whether it arrived through air, openings, or structure.
- Airborne transmission becomes more plausible if a controlled change in radiated sound is reflected in the courtyard outcome without corresponding receiver-surface vibration evidence. This is only suggestive unless the source and structural channels are measured.
- Structural coupling needs source/receiver surface-vibration measurements (e.g., appropriately mounted accelerometers) alongside sound measurements and controlled changes in source position or a correctly specified temporary isolating support. The supplied recorder and basic meter alone cannot establish that path.
- An event during no rehearsal, or one that does not co-vary with logged rehearsal operation, directs investigation toward other event sources; it does not identify which source without further observation.
- More than one pattern may hold. If an event is masked by background, wind, unrelated traffic/activity, or an unrepeatable performance, mark it unresolved and gather another comparable observation.

### 5. Decision the pilot can support

Compare changes at the courtyard receptor against the resident-defined outcome and the repeatability/background uncertainty. Keep the in-room result separate:

- If B produces a repeated, meaningful courtyard change and the in-room result remains acceptable, retain that reversible source/scheduling option while the cause remains under study.
- If C improves the in-room experience but courtyard disturbance does not change beyond uncertainty, treat it as an internal room-acoustics benefit only.
- If source relocation or a later temporary support changes surface vibration and the receiver outcome under controlled conditions, commission a focused structural-path assessment before considering any building work.
- If troublesome events persist in no-session windows, expand source identification and the event log rather than buying room treatment.
- If results are below background, inconsistent, or sensor-limited, make no efficacy claim; improve instrumentation/repeatability or consult an acoustician.

These are receiver-tied decision rules, not a universal dB pass threshold. A resident-defined improvement and suitable measurement uncertainty are required for a decision. The pilot is an observational/reversible study, not renovation design.

## Real implementation histories and what to investigate

**Emslie and Hanson, AAS 2008 drum studio.** This real private studio was built inside a new double-garage extension after years of loud upstairs drum practice. Their proceedings paper reports background and drumming observations at two neighbor-side boundary positions, later measurements outside the completed studio, and a separate indoor-family-room outcome. Its history includes a vibration-isolated free-standing inner studio, cavity absorption, constrained-layer damping, triple glazing, communicating doors, silenced ventilation, custom corner bass traps, acoustic seals and close inspection of gaps/bridges. The proposed heavy block wall was dropped after a structural engineer raised slab capacity concerns; the final roof was a limiting façade element. The case provides concrete questions about installation continuity, ventilation, windows/doors, junctions, and component weakest links. It is explicitly marked non-peer-reviewed and concerned one NSW residential property, local consent criteria, a dedicated isolated drum studio, and particular measurement positions. Its numbers are not this building’s target or forecast [CASE-EMSLIE].

**Gjers, AAS 2024 drum/classroom study.** A commercial building converted to a school music classroom had 180 mm and 450 mm concrete slab regions above a future work/library receiver. The study used room sound meters and accelerometers, and tested a limited kick/snare/tom set on a small platform with Sylomer SR11 pads. In the 180 mm zone, the reported airborne Dw comparison changed by 5 dB; comparisons in the thicker region differed in source setup. Contact points and some placements changed, paths were not separated, and vibration showed frequency-specific amplification and reduction. Authors call for more investigation. This history is relevant to a structural-coupling test’s instrumentation and confounders, but the school/slab arrangement is not a courtyard rehearsal building [CASE-GJERS].

**Shearer et al., Henry Wood Hall airbeds.** The peer-reviewed 2021 study concerned flexible low-frequency reverberation control in a former church used as an orchestral rehearsal space. Laboratory testing selected an airbed model and examined inflation; room measurements before and after installation showed reduced low-frequency reverberation time. It is a useful reversible internal-acoustics alternative to screen if musicians want room-response improvement. Its abstract does not establish sound transmission to a neighbor or courtyard [CASE-AIRBED].

For this building, seek any dated pre/post-rearrangement floor plans, photos, rehearsal schedules, equipment inventories, work orders, panel specifications and incident notes. Interview users about exact changes in room use, source placement/settings, doors/windows, HVAC and panel locations. Ask whether source movement, shorter sessions, quieter hours, maintenance or simultaneous building work coincided with apparent improvement. Distinguish a real measured comparison from retrospective recall. The brief says no reliable before/after log exists; none is presumed.

## Clause-by-clause disposition of the supplied brief

- **Project situation:** treated as hypothetical motivation only; no complaint count, measurement, construction material, source or cause was added.
- **Request—research proposal and path distinction:** provided a causal model and observation path for airborne, structure-borne, independent-background and mixed events; evidence is explicitly limited to comparators and method scopes.
- **Request—existing research’s reach:** included applicable standards, field-study abstracts, and three real implementation histories with context and limits [source index](sources/index.md); none is presented as a prediction for this site.
- **Request—mechanism, implementation details, recommendation changes:** mechanisms and the conditions that would redirect the priority appear above; records/interviews identify the prior arrangement and implementation details to seek.
- **Outcome separation:** courtyard and in-room outcomes have separate sensors/decision interpretations. Indoor improvement alone never establishes outdoor benefit.
- **Source/schedule versus path/room option and low cost:** compares low-cost source setting/schedule, source relocation, panel repositioning, targeted absorption, and a conditional temporary isolation test.
- **Observation plan:** includes source/session/no-session tagging, background and weather/courtyard context, independent receiver data, vibration sensors if pathway separation is needed, and mixed-path handling.
- **Changed arrangement and real comparable history:** treats last winter’s rearrangement as an open lead and discusses Emslie/Hanson, Gjers, and the distinct indoor-only airbed implementation with their differences.
- **Proposed pilot, comparison, confounders and decision:** specifies A/B/C conditions, repeatability, sensor limits, likely confounders, receiver-tied decisions, and an inconclusive outcome when masking or data quality prevents attribution.
- **Boundaries:** no construction, permanent installation, structural alteration, landlord enforcement, or local limit is recommended; products are only research leads. No phone recording or manufacturer absorption claim is used to predict courtyard benefit.
- **Still-unresolved inputs:** floor plan, room sizes, construction/junctions, access, complaint times, metric, and sensor specifications remain open; consequences and reversible assumptions are stated above.
