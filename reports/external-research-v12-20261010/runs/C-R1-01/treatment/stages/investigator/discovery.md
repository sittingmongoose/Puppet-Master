## Decision framing and evidence gaps

Keep two outcomes separate throughout: (1) the sound or disturbance experienced at the courtyard receptor and (2) sound quality/level inside rehearsal rooms. A lower indoor recording or shorter in-room decay does not establish an outdoor benefit. Define what the neighbors mean by “worst” (bass rumble, thuds, rattling, audibility, sleep interruption, or another response), at what location and time, before choosing a metric. The project supplies no jurisdiction, requested metric, room dimensions, floor plan, structure, complaint times, access rights, or reliable before/after record; no legal limit, geometry, source level, material, or cause can be inferred. Applicable local requirements are an owner decision after jurisdiction and use are known. ISO environmental-noise descriptions explicitly do not set limits [STD-1996-1].

The winter room rearrangement is a live causal lead, not proof of causation. Recover dated layouts/photos if they exist, then map current and former source, panels, doors, windows, boundary surfaces, and any connected floor/wall/ceiling details. Record actual instrument and amplifier/speaker setup, playing mode, HVAC or other plant state, and whether the event happened with no rehearsal. Do not fill missing history with a reconstructed “before” measurement.

## Serious, compatible explanations

- Airborne leakage/radiation: room sound can pass through openings or façade/roof elements and arrive in the courtyard; airborne excitation can also vibrate a partition and radiate on the other side. Frequency-dependent facade or room response, openings, and flanking paths matter. ISO field procedures address airborne insulation between rooms or across a facade in specified frequency/room-volume scopes, not a diagnosis of ground-borne transmission [STD-16283-1, STD-16283-3]. ISO 12354-1’s detailed calculation is bounded by element/junction data; it may extend to 50 Hz only if those data are available, which this brief does not supply [STD-12354].
- Structure-borne coupling and re-radiation: drums, a foot-operated bass drum, or other equipment may mechanically excite a floor or connected wall; vibrating surfaces can then radiate low-frequency room sound. Groundborne and airborne energy can coexist. An interior sound meter alone often cannot distinguish them; surface vibration needs a suitably mounted vibration transducer and should be paired with air-pressure measurements [GUIDE-FHWA, CASE-GJERS].
- Other or mixed events: a complaint during an evening without drumming could be another rehearsal source, HVAC/plant, traffic, neighboring activity, or another unobserved event. Those are alternatives to investigate, not asserted sources. Synchronized event logs must include session state and “no session” periods; multiple paths and background may overlap.

Low frequencies in small rooms can be spatially uneven. A single microphone location can therefore mislead; a study of airborne insulation proposes corner plus central positions for better repeatability at 50, 63 and 80 Hz [STUDY-HOPKINS]. ISO 16283-1/3 permit field results for non-diffuse as well as diffuse fields within their stated scope, but those are more formal insulation procedures, not a substitute for agreeing on the disturbance metric [STD-16283-1, STD-16283-3]. Check the exact meter and recorder model, microphone response down to the frequencies of interest, weighting/band options, time response, calibration status, clipping/noise floor, and timestamps. A basic meter’s capabilities are unknown. An uncalibrated recorder can help label an event; it is not a validated courtyard level or benefit prediction. The FHWA handbook warns that room sound alone can confound airborne and groundborne contributions and describes vibration sensors attached to surfaces [GUIDE-FHWA].

## Reversible approaches and alternatives to screen

1. First, document and observe. Agree a courtyard receptor and an in-room reference point. Use synchronized measurements at both points where feasible, with fixed marked positions and instrument settings; include an event diary and matched quiet/background periods. If only one quantitative meter is available, alternate it across repeated matched blocks and say that the resulting pathway comparison is weaker. Preserve separate outdoor and indoor spectral/level summaries, calibrated files, field notes, and any neighbor-reported outcome. Record wind, weather, doors/windows, source/program, source settings, room use, panel layout, and other audible events. Treat periods when the source is not clearly above background as unresolved, not as evidence of no source.
2. Source or scheduling control (low-cost comparison): after an agreed baseline, compare repeated, similar rehearsal passages under normal operation and one modest, voluntary change such as reduced bass/overall setting or an agreed time window. Keep passage, source position, room arrangement, doors/windows, HVAC, and mic positions stable; counterbalance order and repeat. Ask separately whether the change preserves rehearsal usefulness. This is operationally reversible and would help decide whether a near-term schedule/source adjustment reduces courtyard exposure, regardless of path. It cannot explain evenings with no session.
3. Room intervention comparison: move existing freestanding panels between two marked arrangements while holding the source condition fixed. Measure multiple in-room locations because modes and absorber location can change the room response. Also measure the courtyard independently. A panel result that changes only the indoor outcome is still useful for rehearsal acoustics but is not evidence of reduced courtyard disturbance. Studies of coupled dwelling rooms show absorber location/amount can affect low-frequency room levels, with diminishing returns or over-damping; that study’s room layouts and steady-state source do not match this building [STUDY-SONG].
4. Possible structure path test, only if evidence justifies it: a properly load-matched, temporary source isolation platform/pad could be compared with direct placement while recording source and receiver surface vibration plus indoor/outdoor sound. Do not pick a material/thickness from a brochure. A 2024 drum-classroom case found frequency- and slab-dependent outcomes, and its platform test changed component contacts and location; it calls for further path testing [CASE-GJERS]. Getzner’s SR 11 datasheet is explicitly shape-factor/load/frequency dependent and subject to change, so it is an example to investigate, not a selected solution [PROD-SYLOMER]. A temporary isolation trial is not a construction or recommendation to alter the building.

A practical initial pilot is a paired, event-tagged baseline followed by one counterbalanced reversible comparison: instrument the source room and the agreed courtyard point concurrently (or use matched repeated blocks if a second quantitative channel cannot be borrowed); collect representative rehearsals and no-session windows, including the time periods residents identify. Then compare normal operation with a single agreed source-level/scheduling change while keeping panel arrangement fixed. If the source trial is measurable and safe, a later separate panel-position crossover can assess indoor room benefit. Predeclare analysis bands/weightings and the disturbance response after checking local requirements and resident priorities. Compare paired changes with measurement uncertainty/background rather than imposing an invented pass threshold. Decision: adopt a reversible source/schedule adjustment only if repeated courtyard results and resident reports move in the intended direction while indoor needs remain acceptable; test a panel move for indoor benefit only if that is also wanted; if complaints persist in no-session windows, expand event identification; if indoor/outdoor patterns remain ambiguous, borrow proper accelerometers or consult an acoustician before spending on isolation. These are proposed checks; none has been executed here.

## Comparable implementations found (not transferable designs)

- Emslie and Hanson’s AAS 2008 paper is a real private drum studio constructed inside a new extension/garage after disruptive upstairs drumming. It measured two boundary positions and a separate post-build exterior result; it also reports a distinct internal-family outcome. The design had a free-standing isolated studio, controlled detailing, custom bass traps, and staged absorption measurements. The chosen masonry concept was abandoned after a structural engineer raised slab-capacity concerns; the roof later limited façade performance; doors, seals, ventilation penetrations, vibration bridges, and gaps required close attention. It reports lower courtyard-neighbor-side levels after construction, but is explicitly marked non-peer-reviewed, arose under a particular NSW consent/background criterion, and differs sharply from a shared rehearsal building with unknown construction. It is a history to mine for what was tested and which details mattered, not a result to promise here [CASE-EMSLIE].
- Gjers’ 2024 case concerns a commercial office floor converted to a school music classroom above a workspace/library, with 180 mm and 450 mm concrete slab zones. Accelerometers and paired source/receiver meters were used. A small Sylomer platform produced a 5 dB change in the 180 mm-slab Dw comparison of kick/snare/tom components, but the heavier-slab comparison used a full-kit condition versus a smaller component set; placements/contact points differed, paths were not disentangled, and vibration results included both amplification and reduction bands. This history argues for controlled source geometry, surface-vibration channels, and an explicit mixed-path caveat; it is not an outdoor-neighbor trial [CASE-GJERS].
- Shearer et al. document Henry Wood Hall, a former church used for orchestral rehearsal. Airbeds were laboratory-screened, an inflation condition was examined, and before/after hall measurements showed reduced low-frequency reverberation time. The use case was flexible internal room acoustics between sessions, not sound isolation or courtyard noise [CASE-AIRBED]. Song and Kang’s coupled-room study also supports investigating arrangement and absorber location, but its tested dwelling topology, steady noise, and absorber-area findings cannot establish panel performance in this building [STUDY-SONG].
