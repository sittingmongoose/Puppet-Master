# Independent discovery — ER12 / C-R2-01-control / investigator

## Scope and evidence state

This discovery is based on the exact supplied brief, before release of the plan. The case setting is invented and supplies no specimen-level evidence, product identity, environmental series, incident denominator, or material test results. The explanations below are hypotheses and literature-grounded mechanisms, not diagnoses of these labels. I have not read or inferred the unreleased plan. Source identifiers S01–S09 are fixed in [the source index](sources/INDEX.md) and [source-map.json](source-map.json).

The immediate recommendation is a reversible, staged investigation. Keep current labels in place and defer a collection-wide materials or process change. Preserve an incident record, reconstruct which combinations of stock, printer, sleeve and application were used, and monitor the store while preparing controlled coupons on spare or matching empty sleeves. Keep dimensional stability, attachment reliability and information readability as independent endpoints. No label removal, solvents, heat, destructive sampling, or test adhesive application to collection objects is proposed.

## Mechanisms and useful leads

### Paper dimensions and curl

Paper absorbs and releases water vapour. A controlled study of commercial pulp sheets reports expansion as RH rises and shrinkage as it falls; repeated moisture cycles produced material-dependent changes, including an initial transient and irreversible shrinkage that differed between sheets dried with restraint and freely dried sheets (S01, abstract; § Background, Methods and Results). Its chamber profile was 50→20→90→50% RH at 23°C, four cycles, with at least 24 hours at each step. That profile is a laboratory study, not a prescription for this collection, not a label specification, and not a storage lifetime model. The relevant lead is to measure each stock and its grain/machine direction under a justified exposure and avoid assuming that one “paper” behaves like another.

Printing can be coupled to paper response. A primary paper study used two office-paper grades and a water/glycerol model inkjet ink; it followed sheet curl and glycerol distribution over one week. The authors connect delayed curl to co-solvent migration, and discuss how one-sided wetting and printer drying can alter curl direction/magnitude (S02, pp. 4–9). This supports a conditional question—what printing process, ink/toner/ribbon, coverage, feed/drying path and paper are involved?—not a claim that this case used inkjet ink. It is not evidence about handwritten annotations or the whole label/sleeve bond.

The sleeve label may have a face stock plus a pressure-sensitive adhesive, but the brief does not say so. NARA’s dated procurement specifications show that label stock is a system: its required face stock/adhesive, printer process, ribbon or toner, substrate and lot controls vary by use. Its 2015 laser specification calls for a paper face stock with an acrylic adhesive; the thermal-transfer specification specifies polyester or polypropylene face stock with acrylic adhesive and a hard-resin ribbon, and distinguishes curved from flat housing surfaces (S03–S04). Both describe labels for archival housings/furniture and do not establish that either construction is suitable for this sleeve. The laser spec expressly excludes direct application to documents or records. The specified 90° peel and print/readability checks are procurement conditions for NARA’s use, not a pass threshold to copy here or proof of “archival” performance.

If records show a polyethylene or polypropylene sleeve and an acrylic PSA, one 2025 laboratory paper gives a further testable lead: PSA bonding varies with low-surface-energy HDPE and application temperature (S07, abstract). The tested modified adhesive, HDPE panels, measured 24-hour peel/tack, and industry co-author do not establish performance on an unidentified, aged, treated sleeve or under this store’s RH history. The initial inference is only that sleeve polymer, surface treatment/contamination, adhesive chemistry, pressure, contact, dwell and temperature could matter; verify these rather than assume them. Curl can also impose edge-peel stress, so measure curl and attachment separately and then examine their interaction on coupons.

A conservation field overview describes substrate, printed information and attachment as separate label components, documents varied adhesive types, and lists testing gaps around plastic, adhesive, and printing compatibility (S08, §§ 4.3–4.4 and Future Research). It is contextual guidance for natural-history collections, not governing evidence for this store. It reinforces the need for exact product records and tests rather than choosing from an “archival” advertisement.

### Readability and attachment are not proxies for one another

A flat label can detach; a well-attached label can curl; both can remain intact while printed or handwritten information becomes unreadable. Score each outcome separately. NARA’s model specifications separately ask for a clean, legible, abrasion-resistant print, scan readability, bond strength and flatness (S03–S04). Those conditions concern specific label systems and housings. They do not cover the case’s handwritten notes, so take a fixed-condition image and a checked transcription of annotated labels before any handling or approved pilot; keep originals untouched and link the image/transcription to a unique specimen ID. Do not assume that annotation copying has already been authorized.

Record attachment by edge lift, corner lift, detachment, ooze, adhesive transfer, face-stock tear and failure plane; record image/handwriting readability from consistent images or a double-reader check. Any peel test is on sacrificial label/sleeve coupons only. Do not pull at in-place labels to measure strength. Consult the collection owner/conservator before copying, replacing, or relocating annotations.

## Alternatives and history that discriminate causes

A paper-stock or adhesive change is only one candidate. A nonadhesive option is a slip-in ID card/pocket or a removable loop/tag carried by the outer sleeve or a secondary enclosure, so the collection object receives no adhesive. National Museums Scotland’s collection guidance uses a Tyvek tag as an option for fragile or loan objects (S09, “When not to label”); that is an analogy, not a validated sleeve design. Test visibility, snagging, retrieval, object movement, and mix-up risk on mock-ups. Another useful interim path is to retain known current labels while improving temperature/RH monitoring and incident documentation. CCI guidance explains that RH is local and time-dependent, recommends recording instruments near collections, and says seasonal interpretation may require a full year (S05, “Measure” / monitoring sections). It also warns that existing damage alone does not establish current climate causation. This supports observation and risk assessment, not an assumed room setpoint or a claim that humidity caused these labels to fail.

Request manufacturing and implementation records before attributing cause:

- For each face stock and adhesive: maker, exact product/code, version/revision, lot and manufacture date, paper basis weight/thickness, coating/sizing and grain direction if paper, polymer type if synthetic, adhesive chemistry and coat weight, liner, technical sheet/SDS, test report, storage/conditioning instructions, reformulation or discontinuance notices, and the date/lot change entered service.
- For each printer setup: make/model, print process, firmware/settings if logged, cartridge/ink/toner or ribbon make/code/lot, resolution/coverage, temperature/drying/fusing settings, feed path, stock orientation, service/maintenance events, and date commissioned. Identify whether handwritten labels were processed at all.
- For application: sleeve maker, exact resin/grade and surface treatment/coating/additives if documented, age/lot and prior handling; label placement/orientation; cleaning method; pressure/tool/strokes, temperature/RH at application, dwell/cure before storage, and staff/work instruction version. Do not introduce solvent cleaning as a trial.
- For storage and failures: map batches, dates and positions; retain purchase orders, invoices and sample packaging; note supply-change dates, moves, HVAC incidents, water events and printer/application procedure changes; retrieve any actual temperature/RH record, sensor location/calibration and annotation practice. Log confirmed failure counts against the inspected population rather than saying “a few” without a denominator.

If former supplies or printer settings cannot be recovered, label the old/new comparison as non-reconstructable. Cross-over coupon tests can separate the factors only when compatible, identified supplies and comparable printer/application conditions exist. A recreated product or process is not automatically the historical one.

## Bounded later study and decision path

1. **Define owner decisions and baseline.** Assign nonmeaningful IDs, photograph representative attached labels in place without lifting them, map their locations, note handwriting and visible failure modes, and estimate the affected proportion by a defined inspection of the relevant store population. Stratify by known lot/change date and position. Establish who owns annotation copies, which labels are replaceable, acceptable failure/readability limits, access needs, and whether a mock-up/mechanical tag is operationally acceptable. There is no supplied allowable failure rate.
2. **Recover records and monitor.** Request the specifications and implementation history above. Log calibrated temperature/RH near the affected shelf and a reference location at a predeclared interval (for example, 15 minutes) and retain logger calibration/placement metadata. Continue for 12 months to include seasonal conditions. This is proposed monitoring, not executed monitoring; CCI specifically notes local variability and the value of a full seasonal record (S05). Do not alter room controls based on this anecdote alone.
3. **Screen on coupons, not collection items.** After confirming stock, printer, adhesive and sleeve identities, use spare same-lot sleeves or documented matching empty specimens. A bounded 12-week comparative screen can compare the current identified combination with one candidate stock/adhesive system selected only after specifications review, and a no-adhesive insert/tag mock-up. If both old and new printer/process configurations can be reconstructed, cross current/candidate stock against both printer setups and use the same documented application protocol; otherwise hold the printer constant and state that the historic printer effect remains unresolved. Add a current-practice application control and a standardized application control only if historical/current application steps are known. Use at least six coupons per adhesive condition, distributed across at least three production lots when available; if only one lot exists, report that limitation. Include unprinted/unattached paper controls and bare-sleeve controls. Randomize chamber positions and blind visual scoring where feasible. Six replicates support exploratory comparisons, not rare-failure certification.
4. **Set conditions from observations.** Use actual sleeve/product instructions and the measured store profile to select conditioning and any RH-cycle envelope. Keep a stable-condition comparison. If the study compresses or widens the observed cycles, call it an accelerated challenge, report the exact temperature/RH/dwell/ramp, and do not convert the result to years in storage. S01’s 20–90% RH cycle and NARA’s procurement methods are source examples, not defaults. Any test that heats coupons must be separately justified and isolated from the collection; no heat is applied to collection materials.
5. **Measure independently and preserve information.** At baseline and weekly/after each defined cycle, measure coupon length/width in known sheet directions, curl/flatness using fixed supports and photographic scale, and dimensional change after return to a consistent reference condition. Separately record edge/corner lift in millimetres, spontaneous detachment, adhesive ooze/transfer, failure mode and any coupon-only peel result. Separately score print/handwriting visibility, abrasion/smear under a defined non-destructive coupon procedure, and barcode decoding if relevant. Take fixed-light, fixed-distance photographs before/after; code labels; keep original annotation images and verified transcriptions under restricted collection records. Note exact chamber/log conditions, lot, operator and observation time with every result.
6. **Decide by gates, not a composite score.** Before observing results, the owner/conservator sets minimum acceptable performance and the harm threshold. Reject any option that loses or obscures identity, makes annotations unreadable, risks adhesive contact/transfer to a collection object, or fails the owner-defined security/access needs even if its average dimensional score is good. Compare each of the three outcomes and failures by condition and lot. If evidence supports an option, pilot it only on a small, approved group of empty/matching sleeves while retaining links to existing identifiers and monitoring new failures. Widen practice only after the pilot and owner review. If evidence conflicts or the old process cannot be recreated, retain current identified materials under monitoring and report the unresolved cause.

The 12-week coupon screen is an exploratory comparison; the 12-month environmental record is observation under ordinary storage. Neither promises lifetime durability. No accelerated exposure from these sources justifies a storage-life conversion.
