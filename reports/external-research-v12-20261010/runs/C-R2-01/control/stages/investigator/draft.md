## Proposal

Do not change labeling practice across the collection on the anecdote alone. First establish which labels, sleeves, printer configurations, application procedures and environmental episodes correspond to the failures. Leave collection labels in place. In parallel, document attached labels without lifting them and begin temperature/RH monitoring at the affected storage location and a reference point. Use a bounded, staged coupon comparison on spare sleeves or documented same-material empty sleeves once the products and process are identified. Make a later change only through an owner-approved small pilot with linked identifiers, protected annotations, separately reported outcomes and a review point.

The mechanism is not yet attributable to adhesive. Dimensional change of paper, print-side wetting/drying or delayed ink-component migration (only for a matching print process), bond behavior at the identified sleeve surface, application, and their interactions remain plausible. A change in stock and printer at about the same time creates confounding. The reported months of variable room humidity do not quantify the actual exposure or establish causation.

## Evidence and conditional mechanisms

### Dimensional stability

Paper is hygroscopic: it expands as humidity rises and contracts as it falls. In a primary controlled study, commercial pulp sheets showed transient first-cycle behavior and some irreversible shrinkage; the response differed for sheets dried with restraint versus freely dried. The authors used a 50→20→90→50% RH cycle at 23°C, held at least 24 hours at each level (S01). This supports measuring dimensions and curl as their own outcome and recording stock/manufacture direction and constraints. The authors’ specific pulp sheets, chamber cycle and equilibration cannot be copied as a default for the unidentified labels or actual store.

Printing can add another material process. Maass and Hirn used a water/glycerol model inkjet deposit on two office papers, monitored curling and glycerol distribution for a week, and proposed migration of nonvolatile ink co-solvent as one mechanism of delayed curl; their article also explains how wetting and printer drying can shape curl (S02). This finding matters only if the actual printer/ink process is similar. It does not establish inkjet use, explain handwriting, or prove that the adhesive or sleeve failed. Determine the printing technology and media first.

### Attachment and interactions

A label’s face stock, print, adhesive and substrate form a system. NARA’s October 2015 procurement specifications illustrate that requirements depend on the printing process and the housing surface: its laser-label specification uses paper face stock with acrylic PSA; its thermal-transfer specification calls for polyester or polypropylene face stock with acrylic PSA and hard-resin ribbon, with a note for curved surfaces (S03–S04). The laser specification explicitly says its stock is not for direct application to documents or records. Both address specified labels on storage housings/furniture, not this unidentified sleeve. They do not name a conservation-safe stock or validate a marketed “archival” product. Their 90° peel, flatness and image checks are possible design references, not automatic case criteria.

If documentation identifies a PE/PP sleeve and an acrylic pressure-sensitive adhesive, an HDPE-focused 2025 laboratory study suggests that low-surface-energy interface and temperature can influence PSA adhesion (S07). It studied a specific modified adhesive and HDPE; it does not transfer to another polymer, surface treatment, old sleeve or long-term storage. Treat sleeve polymer/grade, surface treatment/coating/contamination, adhesive chemistry, application pressure, contact time, temperature and label curvature as testable leads. A curled face can impose peel stress at edges. That interaction is plausible, not observed here. Measure curl and attachment separately before looking for combined effects.

NARA’s two printer-specific specifications also show why “stock changed” and “printer changed” are incomplete descriptions: product code/lot, construction, compatible printer, toner/ink/ribbon, surface and workmanship criteria are needed to reproduce a label (S03–S04). The SPNHC field review identifies label substrate, printed information and attachment mode as distinct components and records outstanding testing questions for plastics, adhesives and printing (S08). It is natural-history guidance and a standards/literature review, not a test of these sleeves.

### Readability and alternatives

Dimensional stability, attachment reliability and readability must stay separate. A detached label may still be legible; a firmly attached label may be curled; either can lose a handwritten annotation or print contrast. Capture fixed-condition images and a checked transcription for labels with annotations before any approved handling or pilot, and retain original labels untouched. Do not presume that copying or replacing annotations is authorized. NARA’s procurement specs separately require print/scanner readability and attachment/flatness for their specified systems, but they do not validate handwriting here (S03–S04).

Compare any identified paper or adhesive change with at least one useful alternative:

1. **Keep the current identified system temporarily and improve traceability/monitoring.** CCI guidance says RH is local and time-dependent, recommends recording instruments near collections, and notes that seasonal case behavior may require a full year to observe (S05). The logger proposal is monitoring, not a claim that humidity caused the failures and not a target RH recommendation. Avoid room-control changes without collection-specific data.
2. **Try a nonadhesive mock-up.** Consider a slip-in card/pocket, removable loop/tag on the outer sleeve, or label on a secondary enclosure. National Museums Scotland’s guidance offers a Tyvek tag as an option for fragile/loan objects (S09); the context differs, so this is a research lead rather than a design endorsement. Evaluate visibility, retrieval, snagging, added handling, and risk of misassociation on mock-ups only.
3. **Compare a paper/adhesive change only after identity and specifications are known.** Pick one changed material component at a time where compatible materials allow; if a candidate changes multiple components, call it a whole-system comparison, not proof of which component caused a difference. Do not choose on an “archival” advertisement alone.
4. **Consider a storage-practice or enclosure change only after monitoring and an owner/conservator review.** CCI recommends matching response to sensitivity and evidence; it does not establish this room’s correct conditions (S05–S06).

## Records to recover before testing

Request for each label stock: exact maker/product/code and revision; lot/manufacture date; paper basis weight, thickness, coating/sizing, grain or machine direction; synthetic facestock polymer and additives; adhesive family/chemistry and coat weight; liner; print compatibility; application instructions; technical data/SDS, quality-control or accelerated-ageing reports; storage/conditioning directions; product reformulations/discontinuance. Obtain purchase invoices, catalog records and retained unused sheets if available.

For each printer period, record make/model and print technology; firmware or settings only where actually logged; ink/toner/ribbon type/code/lot; coverage/density; temperature, drying/fusing, feed path, stock orientation; maintenance and commissioning dates; and a sample print if one is already available. Identify whether a printer change coincided with ink/ribbon changes or only device hardware.

For implementation, record sleeve composition and supplier/grade/lot/date, documented surface treatment/coating, sleeve age and prior handling; application date and staff/work-instruction version; location/orientation; cleaning method; pressure/tool/strokes; temperature/RH; dwell/cure; and later handling, stacking or rubbing. Do not try solvent cleaning. Record supply-change dates and overlap periods. For environment, request original logger files, sensor position, calibration, sample interval, room moves, HVAC/water incidents and monitoring gaps. For each affected batch, record the number inspected and number of each failure type to replace “a few” with a denominator.

If previous stock, printer/media, sleeve type or application cannot be reconstructed, state that the historical comparison cannot isolate that factor. A new product with the same generic name may reflect a changed formulation or production lot. A recreated process is not evidence of the historic process.

## Bounded later study

### Stage 0 — non-interventive baseline and decisions

Before testing, agree who can authorize imaging/transcription, whether labels can ever be replaced, what “failure” means for this store, an acceptable loss/readability threshold, the minimum identity-security standard, whether annotations may be copied, how much handling is acceptable, and what would justify a pilot. None is supplied.

Assign IDs without changing the originals. Define the affected population and inspect a sample stratified by known date/lot/location. Photograph representative labels in place under fixed lighting and scale; note curl direction/height, lifted edges, missing labels, print/handwriting condition, and whether the label remains associated with its sleeve. Do not pull, peel, heat, wet, or clean labels. Count failed, at-risk and apparently intact labels by stratum; record missing history as unknown rather than infer a cause.

### Stage 1 — identify materials and follow ordinary storage

Recover the records above and deploy calibrated temperature/RH loggers at the affected shelf and a comparison location, with placement, model, accuracy, calibration, interval and retrieval dates recorded. A proposed 15-minute interval is an operational choice to resolve short changes, not a published case requirement. Keep the logger from contacting labels or objects. Follow for 12 months, inspect at a fixed monthly interval, and record every newly observed failure against the mapped denominator and last observation date. A full-year monitor is for seasonal context, consistent with CCI’s caution on localized/seasonal RH (S05); do not claim correlation alone proves mechanism.

### Stage 2 — coupon comparison

After identifying stock, adhesive, printer/media, sleeve and application, run a bounded 12-week exploratory coupon screen using spare stock and spare, same-lot sleeves or documented matching empty sleeves. Never apply experimental material to collection objects. Use a factorial comparison only where the combinations are compatible and historically reproducible:

- material system: current identified construction versus one candidate that changes paper or adhesive alone where feasible; otherwise compare complete systems and do not claim component-level attribution;
- print setup: current versus former/alternative printer/media only if it can be reconstructed with evidence; otherwise hold print setup constant and leave historical printer contribution unresolved;
- application: documented current practice versus a standardized measured practice, only if current practice is known well enough to reproduce;
- exposure: stable reference condition versus a humidity/temperature profile bounded by measured store conditions or credible historical monitoring.

With all four factors available, a 2×2×2×2 layout has 16 combinations. Use six coupons per combination (two from each of at least three production lots when available), randomize chamber positions, and code samples for blinded scoring. This is a bounded exploratory screen, not certification; six specimens cannot establish a rare-failure rate. If suitable legacy material/lots or a second printer are unavailable, drop that factor rather than simulate an unsupported history. Add a no-adhesive paper control and a bare-sleeve control. Include the nonadhesive insert/tag as a separate usability mock-up, not an artificial adhesive factor.

Use a stable-condition control. Select any cyclic exposure from the actual record and product constraints before starting, and state exact temperature, RH limits, dwell, ramp and source rationale. A cycle widened or compressed for speed is an accelerated challenge. Do not lift S01’s 20–90% paper cycle or NARA’s procurement criteria into the case without justification. Do not translate an accelerated result into years in storage. If no prior seasonal record exists, use early logger data only as a provisional envelope and preserve that limitation; continue ordinary monitoring in parallel.

### Independent outcomes and recording

At baseline and weekly/after each declared cycle, measure:

- **Dimensional stability:** label width/length in known sheet directions, curl/flatness using the same support, orientation, lighting and scale; record before and after return to a declared reference condition.
- **Attachment reliability:** lift at each edge/corner in millimetres; spontaneous detachment; adhesive ooze/transfer; face-stock tear; and failure plane. Perform any peel test only on sacrificial coupons and identify test geometry, rate/angle, dwell/conditioning and substrate. Do not copy a NARA acceptance value without owner justification.
- **Readability:** fixed-light/fixed-distance image; separate print and handwriting scores; abrasion/scan test only on coupons under a predeclared procedure; barcode decoding if relevant; independent double reading of transcriptions. Do not conduct rub tests on in-place annotated labels.

Use a failure record with specimen ID, original/replica status, product and lot codes, printer/media settings, application method/operator/date, chamber/shelf location, temperature/RH trace, observation time, measurement, failure category/severity, photo link, annotation status and reviewer. Preserve annotation image and verified transcription linked to, but not replacing, the original record. Never count readability success as adhesion success or vice versa.

### Decision process

Before viewing outcomes, the owner and conservator define non-negotiable gates for identity, legibility, attachment and contact risk, plus the acceptable failure rate and who can approve a material/use change. Report each endpoint, condition and lot separately; do not collapse them into one score. An option that loses identity or makes an annotation unreadable is not rescued by lower curl. Any suspected transfer/contact risk, collection-item damage, loss of accession linkage or inability to retrieve the annotation stops the candidate pending review.

If an option meets the predeclared gates and evidence fits the actual sleeve/process, pilot only on a small approved set of empty/matching sleeves, keep a crosswalk to former identifiers, retain originals as the owner directs, and review at fixed intervals. Expand only after pilot results and owner approval. If results disagree across lots, a condition cannot be reconstructed, or acceptable criteria are missing, keep the known practice under enhanced observation and report uncertainty rather than roll out a collection-wide fix.
