# ER12 investigator discovery — C-R2-01-treatment

## Case boundary and conclusion

The supplied situation is an anecdote, not a diagnosis: the collection, sleeve resin/finish, label construction, printer, humidity record, affected denominator and failure timing are unknown. Both label stock and printer reportedly changed close together. No specimens, monitoring data, lot records or acceptance threshold were supplied. The current evidence supports a controlled investigation and an interim monitoring/indexing option, not a collection-wide replacement.

Treat three outcomes independently:

1. **Dimensional stability:** paper face-stock length/width change by direction and out-of-plane curl/warp.
2. **Attachment reliability:** edge lift, bonded-area loss, buckling at the interface or spontaneous detachment from the sleeve.
3. **Readability:** printed and handwritten information stays legible, complete and correctly associated with its sleeve.

A label may curl while still attached; a stable paper face may detach; an attached label may become unreadable. A single “label failure” score would hide these different decisions.

## Plausible mechanisms and discriminating evidence

- Paper fibers sorb moisture; paper’s dimensional response can be direction-dependent. The hygroexpansion literature reports larger cross-direction than machine-direction change, and the magnitude also depends on fiber orientation, sheet structure and manufacturing/drying history [S04, S05]. Humidity cycling can therefore cause repeated dimensional strain, but the case gives no measured cycle or grain direction.
- Curl can arise from unequal moisture or composition through a sheet’s thickness, one-sided print/coat effects, structural two-sidedness, stock roll-set, or environmental change. A primary study measured delayed curl in model inkjet-printed A4 sheets and proposed co-solvent migration over days [S06]; it is relevant only if the actual printer/ink resembles that experiment. It is not a case diagnosis.
- A paper label constrained on a polymer sleeve can develop edge stress when the face stock and sleeve respond differently to environmental change. Adhesion may additionally depend on sleeve resin/finish, surface contamination, label adhesive chemistry, contact pressure, dwell/cure, label size/edge geometry, and printer compatibility. “Inert sleeve” is not a resin or surface specification. The LoC examples select tests by application substrate and printer process; they separately evaluate bond, curl/lift, and print performance [S01–S03].
- Printing may be a contributor rather than a bystander. Printer type, paper path/heat or water-based ink, media profile, ribbon/toner/ink, print coverage, drying/conditioning and driver settings can affect curl, legibility or feeding. The exact mechanism depends on actual equipment. A Canon troubleshooting example identifies damp/unsupported media, paper type and operating environment and warns that curl-setting changes can reduce toner fixation or speed [S08]. This is only a model-specific lead.
- Application practice is a competing cause: uneven pressure, short dwell, applying in a different room condition, dust/oil, curved sleeve surfaces, re-positioning, or label placement across flex lines may create local failures. The LoC specification controls pressure and a 7-day dwell for its particular procurement test [S02], not as a universal instruction for these materials.

These mechanisms interact. The timing of two supply changes confounds an informal before/after comparison; the adhesive has not been identified as having changed. A failure cluster by lot, printer setting, operator or room location is a lead, not proof of causation. The J-STAGE newsprint study found web-position differences in paper expansion and printing registration, which justifies asking about lot/roll traceability but cannot be projected onto label stock [S09].

## Records to recover before selecting products

Create one row per affected and unaffected label: unique sleeve/label ID, date first observed, location, label dimensions/orientation, visible curl and edge-lift extent, whether detached, print/annotation readability, standardized images, handling/relocation events and reviewer. Record a denominator and avoid replacing “a few batches” with an unverified rate.

Request original and replacement stock SKU, face-stock/coating, adhesive type and construction (or written confirmation that it is unknown), liner, supplier technical data sheets and revisions, purchase/receiving dates, manufacture and batch/roll numbers, shelf/storage conditions, printer make/model/technology/serial, driver/firmware/media setting, ribbon/toner/ink, print coverage and operator setup. The LoC label procurement spec asks for the selected printer model and the laser-label package has manufacturer, year and manufacturing-batch identifiers [S02, S03]. Reconstruct application date/operator, surface preparation actually used, pressure/tool, dwell/cure, sleeve source/resin/finish, storage location and any RH/T records. For any unavailable identifier, retain “unknown”; do not infer it from appearance.

The relevant version history is also absent. Search dated purchase orders, supplier change notices, archived product sheets and invoices for product-code, formulation, face-stock, liner, printer, firmware/driver and setting changes; record publication/revision dates and the actual roll/batch that was used. The public LoC specifications are procurement examples updated 2016-09-30 and list distinct plastic/metal label specifications for laser and thermal-transfer printers [S01–S03]. Their scope excludes rare/high-value and photographic uses; they neither endorse a product nor establish suitability here. The 80°C/50% RH, 28-day exposure in 700-713 is explicitly accelerated aging, not ordinary storage and not a validated lifetime conversion [S02].

## Alternatives worth comparing

| Approach | Potential value | Main uncertainty to resolve |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the current materials temporarily and improve monitoring | Avoids rushed material change; maps RH/T, onset and affected lots while preserving existing labels | Monitoring cannot by itself prevent detachment; owner must set a trigger and acceptable risk |
| Change the face stock or a complete label/adhesive construction matched to the identified sleeve and printer | Could reduce movement, improve attachment or improve print/readability | Require full construction and batch data; do not assume adhesive alone or “archival” marketing explains performance |
| No adhesive on the sleeve: a separately indexed card, a loose ID insert where a conservator confirms it cannot touch/obscure or abrade the contents, or a purpose-designed external label holder on a replacement mock sleeve | Removes the sleeve bond as a failure point and protects original handwriting from a forced relabel | Could be misplaced, obscure content, add handling/static, or lose the sleeve-to-record association; compare retrieval accuracy and no-contact fit |
| Standardize printing/application without changing stock | Helps isolate user/process variation and may be lower disruption | Must be based on actual printer and supplier instructions; printer-specific settings can trade curl against print quality |

NARA guidance supports using polyester sleeves and retaining bond paper for some one-sided paper records, while cautioning about static, media transfer and handling; it also calls for supervisor consultation about copying/moving originals [S07]. This is contextual support for evaluating an insert or an index, not an instruction to put new material in these sleeves. A separate shelf/box index is the least contact-intensive alternative if inserting a card might obscure or rub the collection contents.

## Bounded later study and decision process

**Before the trial:** the collection owner/conservator decides whether affected sleeves may be monitored in place, whether any existing label is replaceable, how annotations may be copied, what loss or misassociation rate is acceptable, which records are high-value/photo/fragile, and whether a label holder or insert is permissible. Do not remove or alter current labels. Photograph each face with a scale and color target; transcribe every handwritten annotation twice independently and reconcile differences; preserve the original image and a crosswalk. This backup does not authorize removal.

**Baseline:** over 2 weeks, map RH and temperature at the affected shelves and a naturally less-variable comparison location if available, using checked loggers at the label/sleeve height. Record logger identity, calibration/check date, sampling interval and data gaps. Survey all visible affected labels non-destructively; match them to purchase and batch history. Do not set an RH “target” before a conservator and collection owner assess the materials and use.

**Mock-up trial:** only after materials and process are identified, use new empty sleeves or coupons of the same verified sleeve resin/finish and face-stock/adhesive lots. No collection objects or existing labels enter the experiment. Run a bounded 6-month ordinary-storage study in the monitored room, with no artificial accelerated-aging or imposed extreme humidity. A reference location may be included if it exists naturally. Use four conditions: (A) documented current combination/application; (B) same stock/adhesive, alternative known printer/process; (C) alternative face-stock or complete label construction, same verified printer and standardized application; and (D) no-adhesive holder or cross-referenced loose card on a mock sleeve. If old stock or printer cannot be recovered or cannot safely/compatibly be used, mark the corresponding contrast unavailable and do not claim the printer or face-stock effect was isolated. If the face and adhesive are sold as a bonded system, compare the whole product and leave their individual contributions unresolved.

Use 12 replicate mock labels per condition across at least three separately printed/applied runs (four per run), plus unlabelled sleeve controls and unbonded paper witnesses where available. This is a pilot for common failure modes, not proof of a low collection-wide failure rate; choose any final acceptance sample size only after the owner supplies a tolerated rate and a statistical decision rule. Randomize sample positions and blind photo scoring where feasible. Follow the actual printer/vendor application conditions; record operator, surface state, pressure/tool, room RH/T, print mode/coverage and cure/dwell.

Measure the three outcomes separately: (1) free paper witness length/width change in known directions and calibrated curl height, plus attached-label outline/curl; (2) edge lift as mapped perimeter/area, buckling and naturally occurring detachment/time-to-event, without pulling or peeling; (3) print and handwriting legibility at fixed imaging conditions, with exact transcription agreement and association to the correct sleeve. Inspect at baseline, weeks 1, 2 and 4, then monthly through month 6 and after any recorded environmental excursion. Log all units, even missing/detached ones, photo IDs, RH/T, operator and failure code. Define “edge lift,” “detached,” and “unreadable” before study; never recode a failed unit as an exclusion.

**Decision gate:** retain, revise, or reject a candidate only against owner-approved thresholds for dimensional change, attachment failure and legibility/record association. A missed humidity cycle, incomplete source trace, too few observations, unclear annotations, or an unresolved sleeve polymer means “inconclusive,” not “pass.” Ordinary six-month experience can inform a bounded decision but cannot promise lifetime durability; the LoC accelerated protocol cannot be converted into storage years without validated assumptions. If change is later approved, stage it by one low-risk cohort, retain originals unless a separate conservation decision authorizes otherwise, and recheck readability/association.
