# Uneven runoff entry into a demonstration rain garden

## Proposal at a glance

The available description does not establish whether water passes the curb opening because of an intended high-flow bypass, an approach or inlet condition, or a change in local geometry. It also does not establish whether water enters and then fills the available surface storage, drains slowly, or follows an unknown underdrain or overflow route. These are separate questions. The first useful step is an owner-approved review of design and maintenance records, followed—if the owner permits safe access—by a small natural-storm observation pilot. The pilot should record entry, storage, recession, and downstream routing separately. A qualified site partner can then decide whether a survey or a later diagnostic test is warranted.

This is a research proposal for discussion with a qualified city or site partner. It does not select a repair, prescribe dimensions, or conclude that the garden is defective. The project description is hypothetical. The reported volunteer work and possible construction-delivery compaction are unverified leads, not findings. No surveyed grades, event rainfall, soil measurements, catchment boundary, facility layers, or installation history are available.

**Recommended sequence:** confirm the partner's primary concern and access conditions; recover the design, installation, and maintenance record; observe a few naturally occurring events under safe and authorized conditions; then escalate only if the observations and applicable local criteria justify qualified review. If no safe or approved vantage exists, stop at the records review and ask the city partner to lead any observation.

## Separate outcomes and mechanisms

### 1. Entry capture and surface routing

For each event, distinguish whether runoff approaches the opening, reaches it, enters the garden, or passes it. A blocked or poorly maintained flow path, leaves or sediment, mulch, opening alignment, local gutter slope, or a changed edge grade could affect entry. These are hypotheses to check, not known conditions. A curb-cut practice may also accept some flows and intentionally pass excess flow along the gutter when its design volume or storage capacity is reached. Thus, pass-by alone does not show a malfunction. Interpret it against the as-built flow path, event conditions, approach flow, and whether the garden was already ponded [S02, S03].

A qualified partner should compare the actual opening and approach geometry with plans and a survey before considering a routing change. EPA guidance discusses curb-cut placement and grading, as well as site-dependent alternatives such as a runnel or low-profile berm for some steep-flow conditions. These are alternatives for technical review, not instructions to change this curb [S03].

### 2. Temporary storage

Record whether water enters, the apparent maximum ponding extent or level, whether an overflow is reached, and when visible ponding recedes. A fixed staff gauge or reference may help if the responsible partner installs or approves it. Without surveyed elevations and a known stage-storage relation, photographs or visible ponding cannot quantify remaining storage or establish that the garden is full. An untimed photograph without rainfall and inflow context cannot distinguish failed entry from later recession [S01, S02].

### 3. Infiltration and drainage

After entry, water may remain temporarily in surface or media storage, move through engineered media into native soil, discharge through an underdrain if present, or leave by overflow. The garden's media, underdrain, subgrade, and groundwater conditions are unknown. Slow visible recession is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Possible explanations include antecedent wetness, sediment or surface clogging, limited available storage, media or native-soil conditions, an outlet or underdrain issue, or a combination. Compaction associated with a delivery is only one unverified hypothesis.

A high seasonal or year-round water table is a possible boundary condition if it interacts with the garden bed or media. The EPA Bioretention Design Handbook says such interaction can reduce void space available for runoff capture and describes noninfiltration practices as preferable in areas with high year-round or seasonal water tables [S09]. This supports considering groundwater context in a records or qualified site review; it does not establish that groundwater is high at this hypothetical site or that it explains any observed drawdown. The handbook's separation guidance is national design guidance, not a local criterion for this unidentified project.

The Minnesota Stormwater Manual gives a general one-to-two-day drainage statement, while Minnesota and EPA maintenance material discuss ponding observations as possible prompts for further evaluation [S01, S04, S05]. Those sources do not identify the cause at this site or establish a governing local threshold. In particular, a roughly 48-hour observation is a conditional investigation prompt in some guidance, not a universal compliance test.

### 4. Downstream effects

If safe access allows, document the designed or observed overflow and gutter-bypass route, and any visible local erosion or ponding. The drainage boundary and receiving conveyance are unknown. Visible ponding or an example from another project cannot establish flood protection, pollutant removal, or regulatory compliance. Water-quality sampling is outside the current proposal and has not been done.

## Storm, antecedent, debris, and geometry context

Rainfall depth by itself is not an adequate event label. For each naturally observed storm, record depth, duration, and available intensity data, preferably at a temporal resolution useful for the small local catchment. Record the prior dry interval or another consistently obtained pre-event wetness indicator, recent rainfall, season, and any concurrent rain during drawdown observations. The availability and spatial or time-resolution mismatch of a nearby gauge must be stated.

Also record the condition of the inlet, gutter, mulch, leaves, and sediment; recent cleaning or other maintenance; and whether water was already ponded before the event. Local slopes, curb geometry, garden geometry, overflow elevations, and contributing area must come from plans or a qualified survey, not assumptions. Storm intensity and duration, antecedent wetness, debris, and geometry may affect more than one process at once, so comparisons should keep those conditions visible rather than attribute a pattern solely to compaction.

A 2026 field study analyzed three wet seasons from two contrasting roof-fed bioretention systems and relates event and antecedent-media-storage conditions to drainage and runoff response [S10]. Its abstract emphasizes that storage results depend on hydraulic loading, media configuration, and drainage pathways. The two facilities differ from this hypothetical street-side garden, so their performance values are not a prediction or target here. The exact five-minute rainfall-method detail noted in the earlier discovery could not be verified from the accessible publisher material in this review and is not relied on in this proposal. Short-interval intensity data remain a useful option if suitable local data exist.

## Evidence, alternatives, and transfer limits

The Minnesota and EPA materials provide mechanisms and inspection or design considerations, not site findings. Minnesota guidance may not govern an unidentified jurisdiction; local criteria and the actual as-builts control. The EPA Green Streets Handbook includes design examples and dimensions, none of which are adopted here [S01–S07, S09].

For pretreatment, Minnesota guidance makes selection conditional on flow type, debris, space, topography, and maintenance capacity: vegetated filter strips are associated with sheet flow and small gardens, forebays with channelized flow, and screens with leaves or larger debris [S07]. A University of Minnesota project summary describes field tests of grass-lined and rock-lined inlets, Rain Guardian Bunker and Turret devices, and a shallow sump. It reports intensity-dependent capture, lower gross-solid retention in the tested sump under overflow, and relative maintenance differences among the tested configurations [S08]. These are bounded study findings, not a product endorsement or evidence that any option fits this site. The full linked report was not reviewed in the inherited source work.

The Minnesota BMP assessment framework distinguishes quick visual inspection from capacity testing, simulated-runoff testing, and longer monitoring; the effort and result differ, and a visual inspection does not produce a numerical performance result [S06]. EPA's road-runoff maintenance memorandum says inspection frequency depends on conditions such as drainage area, land use, watershed activity, and rainfall, and describes repeated level readings or a staff gauge for drawdown observation [S05]. These sources support a staged plan, not an immediate test or diagnosis.

If the owner later asks a quantitative scenario question, EPA's SWMM manual represents LID practices using parameterized layers and a water balance [S11]. The v5.2.4 release record (Build 5.2.4, August 2023; commit 7952ca8) specifies corrections to LID-layer water-flux rates, an invalid-input check for LID with an underdrain, depressed-gutter transect points and slope, and effective hydraulic head for a curb inlet with an inclined throat [S12]. These are version-specific corrections; the release record does not establish that v5.2.4 is the latest version available in every distribution. A future model would require actual catchment, rainfall, geometry, media, underdrain, and overflow inputs. No software was installed and no model was run.

Evidence from other rain gardens transfers only as a hypothesis about mechanisms or a guide to measurements. Differences in design, geology, climate, catchment loading, event selection, drainage boundary, age, maintenance, and measurement protocol constrain comparisons. None of the cited material establishes this site's design intent, performance, groundwater, local regulatory requirement, or benefit.

Build a dated chronology from work orders, inspection reports, photographs, volunteer notes, street-sweeping records, leaf or sediment removal, mulch addition, plant replacement, and construction or delivery access. For the possible delivery, verify its date, route, equipment, load if known, and whether it crossed or stored materials near the garden edge. Record whether the volunteer work occurred before or after relevant observations. Treat every scenario clue as unverified until supported by records or direct partner observation. If records are missing, preserve that gap and ask the partner to arrange a qualified, non-invasive survey or inspection rather than filling it with a literature value.

Untimed photographs without rainfall, inflow, and time-since-event context may show a condition but cannot separate entry, storage, and drawdown. Historical photos are useful only when their date, viewpoint, event context, and maintenance state are known.

## Proposed later observation program

### Preparation and permission gate

Before field observation, confirm the owner, the partner's priority (entry capture, drawdown, or both), written or otherwise documented permission, safe observation points, the responsible observer, applicable local criteria, and an emergency contact. Review the records first so the observer knows the intended flow and overflow routes. If access is unapproved, traffic or high flow prevents a safe vantage, lightning or another hazard arises, or the partner identifies a public hazard, stop field observation and refer the condition to the responsible city contact. If safe access cannot be established, remain at records review.

### Natural-event pilot

With permission, observe approximately four to six naturally occurring events spanning the range of locally observed rainfall and antecedent conditions. This is a scoping pilot for pattern-finding, not a statistical certification, failure-rate estimate, or design-storm test. Do not manufacture a blockage, redirect runoff, add or remove mulch for comparison, change a curb, or ask volunteers to enter the roadway or moving water.

For each event, use a consistent event sheet and record:

- Date and time; rainfall source and gauge location; measurement resolution; event depth, duration, and available intensity; and any known gaps or spatial mismatch.
- Prior dry interval and any safe, consistently defined pre-event wetness indicator; season; and concurrent rain during post-event checks.
- Pre-event condition of the opening, gutter, mulch, leaves, and sediment; recent cleaning or other maintenance; and whether the garden was already ponded.
- From an approved vantage, whether approach flow reached the opening, entered, or passed it; whether observed capacity or ponding might already have limited capture; and the observed overflow or bypass route.
- Maximum visible ponding relative to a stable reference installed by the responsible partner if permitted. Without such a reference, label visual extent as qualitative rather than a measured depth.
- Visible water level or ponding recession at consistent safe post-event times—for example soon after, then around 6, 24, and 48 hours—plus any concurrent rainfall, erosion, or downstream ponding.
- Photo locations and times, with permission and without recording members of the public.

Use an existing city or nearby official rainfall gauge when possible. State its distance and temporal resolution and do not treat it as a site measurement when it is not. A logger or additional gauge is an optional later refinement only if the partner approves it.

### Comparison and interpretation

Keep entry, apparent storage, recession, and downstream observations in separate columns. Compare event classes with similar rainfall and antecedent conditions where the sample permits. Note naturally occurring clean and debris-affected conditions and log routine maintenance before and after events. Uncontrolled storms and routine work do not provide a clean causal experiment; report the limits instead of treating a before-and-after difference as proof.

### Escalation and stop conditions

- Stop or pause observation whenever permission or safe access is absent, or a hazard prevents a safe vantage. Refer hazards to the site owner or city partner.
- If runoff passes the opening during modest observed events despite an apparently clear approach and the garden does not appear already full, request a qualified review of the as-built flow path, local survey, and intended bypass before discussing a routing alternative.
- If water enters and storage appears to fill, first determine whether the event and observed bypass match the intended design and owner objective. Do not label expected high-flow overflow a failure.
- If water enters but recession is repeatedly slow, compare the observations with recovered design documents and the applicable local criterion. If no local criterion is found, a repeated observation near or beyond 48 hours may be a discussion trigger for qualified review because some guidance uses it for further evaluation; it is not a known local limit or diagnosis [S04, S05].
- After records and observations, the qualified partner may decide whether non-invasive inspection, a permitted capacity or infiltration test, or review of the media, native soil, groundwater, or underdrain is warranted. Any such test or invasive work requires separate authorization and is outside this proposal.

## Uncertainties and owner decisions

The following remain unknown, and each changes what can be measured or inferred:

- **Drainage boundary and contributing area:** Without a mapped boundary, runoff input and bypass cannot be quantified, and a water balance is unsupported.
- **Slopes, opening geometry, and local elevations:** Without as-builts or a qualified survey, approach flow, intended bypass, apparent storage, and any routing concern cannot be interpreted quantitatively.
- **Media, plants, native soil, and underdrain:** Without records or qualified inspection, observed recession cannot isolate infiltration from underdrain discharge, storage, or surface/media conditions.
- **Overflow route and receiving conveyance:** Without them, downstream consequences and intended excess-flow routing are unknown.
- **Rainfall distribution, antecedent wetness, and debris state:** Without comparable event records, differences among storms cannot be attributed to a single mechanism.
- **Installation and maintenance history:** Without dated records, the recent mulch, leaf clearing, and possible delivery cannot be linked to any observed behavior.
- **Public access, safety, permissions, and monitoring equipment:** These determine whether any field observation is feasible and what level or rainfall measurement is allowed.
- **Partner's objective:** Until the city states whether entry capture, drawdown, or both matter most, the study cannot define an acceptance criterion or rank alternatives.

Before field work, the owner or qualified partner must decide which outcome is primary; what bypass is acceptable for the actual purpose; which local criteria apply; who owns and maintains the garden; where observers may safely stand; whether a gauge, level reference, or logger is permitted; what records and staff are available; what evidence and budget would justify a survey or later test; and whether a model would answer a future, well-defined question.

## Conditional water-balance use

If the partner can define boundaries and measure the relevant terms, two linked balances could help organize a later study:

- At the approach boundary: runoff arriving at the opening equals water entering the garden plus water bypassing the opening.
- At the garden boundary: water entering plus direct rainfall is balanced against the change in surface or media storage, overflow, underdrain discharge, exfiltration, evapotranspiration, and measurement residual.

A balance could show whether measured inputs and outputs close within uncertainty. It would not isolate infiltration if important terms such as storage change, overflow, underdrain flow, or evapotranspiration are missing. The current brief provides no drainage area, survey, rainfall, media or drain parameters, or overflow data, so no numerical balance can be populated. This proposal includes no executed balance or site diagram.
