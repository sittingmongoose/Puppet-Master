# Independent discovery — uneven runoff entry into a demonstration rain garden

**Basis and status.** This discovery was developed from the original brief alone, before release of the exact plan. All site details are hypothetical. No site visit, records review, rain or soil measurement, field validation, infiltration test, engineering calculation, or model run has been performed. The delivery, leaf clearing, and mulch addition are unverified scenario clues, not established causes. The city partner's priority between entry failure and slow drawdown is still unknown. Source identities and bounded notes are in [sources/index.md](sources/index.md) and [source-map.json](source-map.json).

## Separate outcomes and competing explanations

1. **Entry capture and routing.** Determine whether runoff reaches the curb opening, whether it enters the garden, and where any bypass goes. Leaves, sediment, mulch, a blocked flow path, opening alignment, local gutter slope, or a change in edge grade could affect entry. Yet a curb-cut garden may intentionally accept ordinary flow and let excess flow continue along the gutter when the cell is full; bypass alone does not establish a defect [S02, S03]. Record flow approaching the opening separately from flow that reaches or passes it.

2. **Temporary storage.** Record whether water enters, where it ponds, the apparent peak level/extent against a fixed reference, whether it reaches an overflow, and when visible ponding recedes. Without surveyed elevations, as-builts, or known basin geometry, ponding cannot quantify available storage or establish that the garden is full [S01, S02].

3. **Infiltration and drainage.** Captured water can move through engineered media into native soil, or into an underdrain if one exists. The underdrain, media, native soil, and overflow arrangement are unknown. Slow drawdown is therefore a symptom with competing explanations; compaction is only one hypothesis. Surface clogging, sediment loading, antecedent wetness, storage geometry, a high water table, or an unknown drain path may also matter [S01, S04, S05]. Do not diagnose infiltration from entry observations alone.

4. **Downstream effects.** Log the designed/observed overflow or gutter bypass route, any erosion or local ponding, and the receiving conveyance, subject to safe access. Visible ponding or a literature example does not establish flood protection, pollutant removal, or regulatory compliance.

Rainfall depth alone is not an adequate event label. Across observed events, compare duration, intensity (preferably short-interval data when available), antecedent wetness/dry period, and pre-event debris condition. A recent field study explicitly used five-minute rainfall to derive event depth, duration, mean intensity and maximum 30-minute intensity and paired those with antecedent media storage; its two Beijing roof-fed systems had different media, loading, and drainage boundaries, so its numerical performance must not be transferred to this street garden [S10]. The University of Minnesota pretreatment trials also found different capture under low- and high-intensity tests, but they tested five inlet pretreatment configurations rather than whole rain-garden behavior [S08].

## Evidence and useful approaches

- Minnesota's living manual separates gardens with and without underdrains, describes curb-cut routing, says ponded water should drain in a day or two, and calls for routine inlet checks and care to avoid trampling/compaction. It is useful mechanism and inspection guidance, not a site-specific or necessarily governing standard here [S01].
- Minnesota's bioretention overview describes high-flow bypass after maximum ponding and explains that offline curb-cut arrangements can let excess water continue down the gutter. It also warns about erosion and short-circuiting where systems are online. This makes design records and event context essential before labeling the observed pass-by as failure [S02].
- EPA's February 2021 Green Streets Handbook discusses curb-cut placement in the gutter flow path and grading toward the practice; it describes a low-profile berm or runnel where steep gutter flow needs redirection. It also compares grated openings, trench drains, pretreatment, bypass/overflow, and underdrains. These are discussion alternatives only: the handbook's examples do not justify a construction change without local survey, hydraulic review, access/permission, and applicable standards [S03].
- Pretreatment choices depend on flow type, debris, available space and topography. The Minnesota manual associates vegetated filter strips with sheet flow/small gardens, forebays with channelized flow, and screens with leaf/large debris [S07]. A 2019 University of Minnesota field-testing report summarized by its Water Resources Center compared grass-lined and rock-lined inlets, Rain Guardian Bunker and Turret units, and a shallow sump. Sediment/gross-solid capture differed by intensity; overflow reduced gross-solid retention in the tested sump; Bunker/Turret were easier to inspect/maintain than grass/rock. This can inform a partner's alternatives discussion, but neither hardware performance nor maintenance ranking proves suitability at this unmeasured site [S08].
- Minnesota maintenance guidance recommends post-event and routine inspection of inlet, pretreatment, main area, bypass/outlet, sediment and vegetation; it notes foot traffic and vehicles can compact/damage a practice and that leaves, sediment and plant debris can block flow [S04]. EPA's road-runoff maintenance memo says inspection frequency depends on drainage area, land use, activities and rainfall; it describes a staff gauge or repeated level observations for drawdown and gathering design specifications and past maintenance history before assessment [S05]. A 48-hour standing-water observation appears in guidance as a trigger for further evaluation, not a universal local compliance threshold [S04, S05].
- A modest assessment can start with visual inspection (quick, inexpensive, limited diagnosis), then escalate to capacity testing or longer monitoring only if the first-stage evidence warrants it. Minnesota's assessment framework distinguishes those levels and their effort and uncertainty [S06]. If appropriate permissions and a qualified partner are later available, a capacity test can be considered after records/event observations; no such test is proposed as already performed.
- If the city later asks a quantitative scenario question, EPA SWMM represents bioretention as surface/soil/storage/drain layers with a water balance [S11]. Its v5.2.4 release (commit 7952ca8) records fixes to LID-layer fluxes and curb-inlet/gutter calculations [S12]. This makes version pinning relevant, but a model would require actual drainage area, surveyed grades, rainfall, media/drain parameters and overflow paths. No model was run, and simulation would not settle the current question without those inputs.

These references are analogs, not evidence about the hypothetical site. Minnesota guidance may not govern an unidentified jurisdiction; EPA handbooks are guidance; the UMN tests measure pretreatment under their own test method; and field results depend on design, soil/geology, catchment loading, drainage boundaries, event selection, age, and maintenance. No external result should be used as a local performance guarantee or as a substitute for a qualified partner's site records and observations.

## Proposed observation-first program for partner discussion

**Stage A — establish what exists.** First identify the city/site owner, observation permission, safe public vantage, and the partner's primary concern. Request original plans/as-builts, curb/inlet and overflow details, design or acceptance/commissioning records, media and underdrain details, any initial soil/infiltration data, catchment boundary and drainage map, surveyed elevations, and applicable drawdown/overflow criteria. Reconstruct installation and maintenance chronology from work orders, photos, volunteer notes, street-sweeping records, mulch/leaf/sediment work, planting/replacement, and construction/delivery access (date, route, equipment, load if known). Treat the recent delivery and volunteer work as leads to verify, not findings. If records are absent, ask the partner to arrange a qualified non-invasive survey before interpreting geometry.

**Stage B — small event log.** With permission, observe a set of safely accessible natural storms spanning the available local range of depth/intensity and antecedent wet/dry conditions; a small pilot (for example, 4–6 events) is for pattern finding, not statistical certification. For each event, log timestamp, source and resolution of rainfall data, depth/duration/intensity, prior dry interval or available pre-event soil-moisture proxy, temperature/season, leaf/sediment/mulch condition, recent cleaning, and photo locations. Volunteers should not stand in the roadway or approach moving water during a storm. From an approved vantage, record: (a) whether runoff reaches the opening and whether it enters or passes; (b) maximum ponding relative to a partner-installed fixed staff gauge/reference if permitted; (c) overflow/bypass route; and (d) any downstream erosion or ponding. At safe post-event times, record water level or visible ponding recession (e.g., soon after, then 6/24/48 hours) and note concurrent rainfall. Use existing city/nearby gauge data where possible; mark spatial or time-resolution mismatch. If a logger is permitted, treat it as an optional later refinement, not a prerequisite.

**Stage C — compare conditions without manufacturing a failure.** Log routine cleaning, leaves, mulch and sediment removal before/after each event. Do not intentionally leave a blockage, alter the curb, redirect runoff, or use volunteer maintenance as an uncontrolled experiment. Compare similar event classes and antecedent conditions before and after routine work, while noting that uncontrolled storms cannot support a clean causal estimate.

**Stage D — escalation and alternatives.** If flow passes the opening during modest events even when the approach and opening appear clear, ask a qualified partner to check surveyed gutter/curb geometry and intended bypass elevations before considering a routing alternative. If water enters and storage fills, distinguish expected high-flow overflow from persistent ponding. If repeated event observations show slow recession after modest events, compare with the recovered design/local criterion; absent one, repeated standing water beyond about 48 hours may justify partner review because that is used as an investigation trigger in some guidance, not because this hypothetical garden is known to have that limit [S04, S05]. A qualified partner may then decide whether non-invasive inspection, a permitted capacity/infiltration test, or underdrain/media investigation is warranted. Do not excavate, modify the curb, perform an infiltration test, or prescribe dimensions in this proposal.

Alternatives for later partner comparison are: routine inlet/flow-path maintenance; surveyed review of curb-cut position/grade and the existing bypass; pretreatment suited to actual sheet versus channelized flow and leaf/sediment loads; and a soil/underdrain investigation if capture is occurring but drawdown remains slow. The observation-first option gives the partner evidence before structural or soil interventions. Weights, geometry, permissions, safety, maintenance capacity, and applicable requirements determine which comparison is feasible.

**No local result is asserted.** The unresolved drainage boundary, grade, opening, garden geometry/layers, overflow path, rain distribution, public-access limits, permissions and owner priority affect both study design and the meaning of every observation. Results should report the four outcomes separately and state what remains unknown. Field observations, any later partner-approved test, and any future model must be labeled separately; none has been executed in this reference stage.
