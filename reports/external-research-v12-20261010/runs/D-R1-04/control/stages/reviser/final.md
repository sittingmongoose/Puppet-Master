## Executive study position

First determine whether water fails to reach or enter the curb opening, enters but occupies storage longer than expected, or reaches an overflow/outlet and affects a downstream route. These are separate questions. Some bypass may be intended for a particular design or storm; the city’s actual objective and the as-built design must establish whether it is a problem. Do not infer poor infiltration from water passing the opening.

Keep four event outcomes separate:

1. **Entry/capture:** whether gutter flow reaches the opening and enters, bypasses, or does both.
2. **Temporary storage:** whether water ponds after entry, its visible footprint/depth over time, and whether pre-event wetness or available storage may matter.
3. **Infiltration and drawdown:** recession of surface water and any separate discharge through an underdrain or outlet. Ponding is a clue to investigate, not a diagnosis of soil condition.
4. **Downstream effects:** where gutter bypass, garden overflow, or outlet discharge goes, and whether there is visible scour, erosion, or an access/property concern.

The drainage boundary, slopes and inlet elevations, storage/overflow levels, soil and media layers, underdrain, storm record, and permissions are unknown. Accordingly, runoff volume, capture ratio, storage capacity, causal attribution, flood protection, pollutant removal, and regulatory compliance cannot be calculated or claimed. A simple water balance is unsupported until the contributing area, rainfall, entry/bypass, storage, and outlet/overflow terms can be measured or otherwise established.

## Mechanisms and evidence that would distinguish them

**Entry/capture.** Flow direction and cross-slope, local grade, curb-opening orientation/elevation/condition, small pavement grade changes, inlet blockage, sediment/leaves/trash/shifted mulch, approach-flow rate relative to opening capacity, contributing-area changes, and intended bypass can all affect capture. Start by observing whether water reaches the opening and by comparing the existing condition with records; do not assume the opening is supposed to accept every flow. EPA identifies drainage-area delineation and grade as inputs to runoff/capture assessment and discusses inlet/flow-path choices; those choices depend on site conditions and local rules. An undersized or misaligned inlet can bypass, but that general possibility does not diagnose this opening. ([R-01], [S-01])

**Storage and drawdown after entry.** Establish that water entered before interpreting ponding. Basin geometry and available storage, prior wetness, event timing/intensity, media-surface sediment, underlying soil, vegetation/root condition, underdrain/outlet configuration or blockage, and overflow elevation may affect observed water levels and recession. Underdrained and non-underdrained gardens have different discharge paths. The as-built configuration and local criterion must be known before calling drawdown abnormal. Minnesota guidance lists blockage, sediment, and other possible troubleshooting factors; its 48-hour post-rain check occurs in a contextual maintenance schedule and is not presumed to be this site's governing threshold. ([S-02], [R-02])

**Compaction and recent work.** Traffic, equipment storage, excavation, or stockpiling can compact soil; wet and clay soils are more susceptible. The reported delivery is only a lead: its route, unload point, timing, contact with the edge, and any rut/settlement evidence are unknown. Mulch or leaves could affect an inlet or outlet, but their recent addition/removal does not prove they caused the pattern. Log what changed and when, and request records before considering a qualified soil/media investigation. Do not diagnose compaction from ponding or perform a test or remediation as part of this proposal. ([R-02], [R-04], [S-03], [S-04])

**Storm and downstream conditions.** For each event, consider total rainfall, duration, short-interval/peak intensity when available, antecedent rainfall/dry period as a wetness proxy, visible pre-event debris, and maintenance period. These variables can change several processes together, so event associations do not isolate cause. A Queens study found inlet bypass could limit captured runoff at one sandy-soil facility even though nearly all water that entered infiltrated there; event capture varied and the authors associated performance with storm duration, total rainfall and peak-hourly intensity. The same article treats maintenance frequency as anecdotal/future work. Those findings support separating capture from infiltration and logging storm covariates; the study's setting and percentages do not predict this case. ([R-05], [C-05])

The investigator's source record reports that an Omaha USGS report described different outflow for two similar-depth events with different duration/intensity, and that a Madison report considered intensity and antecedent dry days in its studied gardens. These are site-specific examples only. The critic could not independently reopen the detailed Omaha text and reviewed only limited official Madison material; this reviser did not independently retrieve either report's full text or event tables. I therefore retain those examples as prior-stage reported context with that limitation, do not rely on their detailed event pairs as independently rechecked evidence, and transfer no numeric result or site parameter. ([S-07], [S-08], [C-06], [C-07])

Where bypass or overflow leads is a separate observation. The MPCA overview describes high-flow bypass after a cell reaches its maximum ponding depth and distinguishes offline/online arrangements; it also discusses erosion and energy-dissipation considerations. That means bypass can be intentional in some designs. It does not establish this garden's design intent, capacity, or safe route. ([R-03], [S-10])

- Approved design and as-built survey: drainage boundary/contributing area, roadway and garden grades, opening position/orientation/elevations, basin/rim/ponding/overflow levels, intended bypass, outlet/underdrain, and downstream destination.
- Specified and installed soil/media/aggregate/plant layers; original soil or infiltration-test records; pretreatment details; construction sequence; inspection and closeout reports/photos; sediment controls; traffic, stockpile, and access restrictions; repairs and prior complaints.
- Maintenance agreement and responsible party; inspection checklists/dates; leaf, trash, and sediment removal; mulch type/placement/renewal; plant changes; inlet/outlet cleaning; street sweeping, snow storage/deicing, utility or curb work; and dated photos or work orders.
- Delivery date, route and unload point, whether it crossed the garden edge, and any documented rutting, settlement, or grade change. For volunteer work, request dates, material, location, and what was actually cleared or added.

EPA guidance notes that roadway as-builts can help establish slope, dimensions, and inlet location, while construction and maintenance practices affect function. MPCA guidance offers possible maintenance/troubleshooting causes, not a diagnosis. Missing records remain unknown; visible ponding cannot reconstruct them. Evidence from other gardens transfers only as a source of hypotheses and study variables. Catchment, geometry, event conditions, operation, and measured outcomes differ across examples. ## Modest later observation and escalation program (proposed, not executed)

1. **Agree the question and permission.** Ask the city whether entry, drawdown, or downstream consequence is the primary concern and whether some bypass is intended/acceptable. Confirm the owner/operator, safe observation points, public-access limits, local drawdown/overflow criteria, records access, and permission for any temporary gauge or fixed reference. Do not enter the road or private area without authorization.
2. **Establish a fixed baseline.** On dry days, take repeatable, time-stamped photos from an approved safe position. Note the visible inlet/approach, debris, mulch, vegetation, ponding area, erosion/settlement, and visible outlet/overflow. These are observations, not a survey, soil measurement, or water-quality assessment. Do not rearrange materials for the study. If the owner must perform routine or safety maintenance, log date, scope, and before/after condition.
3. **Observe a modest natural sample.** During one partner-approved local season, aim for roughly 6–10 safely observable storms spanning contrasting durations, short-interval intensities, and antecedent wetness where weather permits. This is exploratory, not statistically powered. Use representative quality-controlled interval rainfall data if available; a temporary gauge is optional only with partner approval. Log event start/end, total rainfall, interval/peak intensity if available, and prior 24–72-hour rainfall or days since rain. Antecedent rainfall is a proxy, not a soil-moisture measurement. If a useful event class does not occur, report the gap rather than extrapolating.
4. **Record four outcomes separately for each event.** Log observer/date/time, rain source, rainfall descriptors, pre-event debris and maintenance, whether approaching flow enters/bypasses/both, whether ponding forms, maximum depth at an approved fixed reference if one exists, visible overflow/outlet flow and destination, erosion/scour, and any missed observation. Recheck shortly after rain ends and around 6, 24, and 48 hours later; record actual times. Do not infer flow volume from photographs or enter moving water/traffic.
5. **Compare cautiously.** Group events by rainfall amount, duration, available intensity, antecedent-rain proxy, debris, and maintenance period. Compare similar events when available and note when several factors changed together. Treat owner cleaning or other work as a new condition period; a few before/after events cannot establish causation. If observations do not discriminate capture from drawdown, ask the partner whether approved flow/level instruments, a qualified survey, or a qualified soil/media/outlet investigation is warranted.
6. **Stop or escalate.** Stop volunteer observation during lightning/unsafe weather, traffic, moving water, poor visibility, or unauthorized access. Promptly notify the city partner about a blocked inlet/overflow, active scour or settlement, water reaching a road, access route, building, or neighboring property, or ponding beyond the applicable local/design criterion. Do not delay owner safety maintenance for a comparison. Leave diagnosis, tests, repairs, routing changes, and design decisions to the qualified partner.

The event count, timing, observation methods, and escalation criteria above are proposals, not field validation. An optional later field packet could use a one-page event sheet with separate fields for capture, ponding, drawdown, downstream route, weather, and maintenance; label photos with date/time/viewpoint; keep one fixed observation point; and include an ‘unknown/not observed’ option. These improve repeatability without implying precision. The 48-hour check is a suggested observation point only; it is not a universal compliance limit. A later quantitative water balance would require a delineated catchment, rainfall/input measures, inlet and bypass flow, storage geometry/levels, and outlet/overflow measurements with a qualified plan. No such study was executed here.
