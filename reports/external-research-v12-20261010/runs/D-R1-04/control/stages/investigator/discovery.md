# D-R1-04-control investigator discovery
Prepared before release of the exact plan. Research was guided by the assigned hypothetical brief alone; no parent conversation, other candidate, evaluation, or campaign history was used. No local records, site access, field observations, survey, rainfall series, model, or test were available. The reported intermittent curb flow, mulch/leaf work, and delivery are brief statements, not independently verified observations.

## Decision frame
Treat four outcomes separately: (1) runoff reaching and entering the curb opening; (2) temporary surface or subsurface storage after entry; (3) infiltration and/or discharge through any underdrain or outlet; and (4) where bypass or overflow goes downstream. Water passing the opening does not, by itself, diagnose a soil or storage problem. A garden that receives water but drains slowly presents a different question. A dry surface at one visit does not prove how much runoff entered, where it went, or whether it met any treatment or regulatory goal.

The city partner should first say whether the primary concern is missed entry, standing water/drawdown, or an off-site consequence. Keep all three visible in the study so an unclear priority does not erase a distinct outcome. Because the drainage boundary, grades, opening and overflow geometry, media layers, underdrain, storm history, and permissions are unknown, quantitative flow capture, capacity, or causal attribution is not presently supportable.

## Mechanisms to distinguish
- **Entry/capture:** gutter flow direction and cross-slope; curb-cut orientation, elevation and condition; small pavement divots or grade changes; an opening partly blocked by leaves, sediment, trash or shifted mulch; inflow rate/volume relative to the inlet; whether the intended design deliberately bypasses excess flow; and changes to the upstream contributing area. EPA’s 2023 handbook ties capture to drainage area, grade, inlet placement/orientation and debris and describes several inlet and flow-path alternatives [S-01]. The MPCA overview distinguishes offline bypass from online overflow once the cell fills [S-10].
- **Storage:** whether water actually entered, the water level and footprint through time, available basin storage, and whether the garden was already wet. Unknown rim, ponding, and overflow elevations prevent a storage calculation. Rainfall depth alone is insufficient context.
- **Drawdown/infiltration:** media-surface sediment clogging, underlying soil texture or layering, construction or traffic compaction, vegetation/root condition, antecedent moisture, and the presence or blockage of an underdrain/outlet. A high water mark or prolonged ponding is a clue, not a diagnosis. MPCA troubleshooting lists sediment, ineffective pretreatment, high sediment loading, debris and grading among possible causes [S-03]. Compaction is plausible after traffic, especially in wet/clayey soils, but the delivery does not prove contact with the garden or compaction; a visual symptom cannot establish soil condition [S-04].
- **Downstream route:** where gutter bypass, garden overflow, and any underdrain discharge lead; whether observed flow is concentrated, eroding, or reaching an access/road/property area. These are observations to report to the partner, not evidence of flood protection, pollutant removal, or compliance.

Storm descriptors matter across the outcomes: total rainfall and duration, peak short-interval intensity when available, previous rainfall/antecedent dry period as a proxy for wetness, and whether the surface was already wet; also debris condition and any maintenance between storms. In an Omaha USGS study, similar rainfall totals occurred with different event durations and maximum intensities and different outflow; the authors also identify antecedent moisture and intensity timing as possible contributors [S-07]. A Madison USGS study found intensity and antecedent dry days relevant to saturation in its two deliberately constructed gardens with different soils and vegetation [S-08]. A Queens field study found that inlet bypass could limit captured runoff even where the facility infiltrated nearly all water that entered; its authors associated capture variation with storm duration, total precipitation, and peak hourly intensity [S-09]. Together these sources support event comparison, not a transferable percentage or a single-cause conclusion.

## Installation and maintenance history to request
Because the site is hypothetical and no site files were provided, this stage could not verify original installation or the recent work. Ask the city partner for the approved design and as-built survey; contributing-area map; curb/inlet, rim, bypass/overflow and outlet elevations; original soil/media/underdrain and pretreatment specifications; soil or infiltration test records from installation; construction sequencing and inspection/closeout photos; traffic and stockpile restrictions; and any repair or complaint history. EPA guidance notes that as-built roadway plans can identify slopes, dimensions and inlet locations, and that construction inspection, sequencing and compaction can affect function [S-01]. These records reveal intended bypass, storage and underdrain behavior before current symptoms are interpreted.

Request maintenance agreements, inspection checklists, dates and descriptions of leaf/debris removal, mulch installation or renewal, sediment/erosion work, plant changes, inlet/outlet cleaning, and any street sweeping, snow storage, utility or curb work. For the delivery, ask when it occurred, its route and unload point, whether the garden edge was crossed, and whether dated photos or work orders show ruts, settlement, or a grade change. Record the volunteer leaf clearing and mulch work dates/materials and what was actually touched. MPCA guidance identifies debris and mulch as possible clogging factors and warns maintenance traffic can compact a practice [S-03]; EPA recommends written schedules, responsibility assignments and tracking [S-05]. These are leads, not proof of causation. If records are absent, preserve that uncertainty.

## Alternatives for partner discussion
| Path | What it would resolve | Benefits and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Inlet or surface-routing review | Whether gutter flow is aimed at, obstructed from, or exceeding the opening; whether the flow path has changed | Screen existing opening/grade first. The EPA handbook catalogs depressed or angled curb cuts, gutter aprons, inlet sumps/pretreatment, flow splitters, and multiple-inlet arrangements; the MPCA manual describes offline and online bypass/overflow. These are design alternatives only. Increased capture may change the volume reaching the garden; structures add cleaning, safety, erosion, and downstream-routing needs. No dimensions or curb changes are proposed. |
| Maintenance and soil-management investigation | Whether leaves, sediment, mulch, vegetation, media surface, or prior traffic/history could explain the pattern | Low-disturbance inspection and work-order review are useful first. If entry is confirmed but drawdown remains unusual, a qualified partner can review the as-built soil/media and outlet records before deciding whether authorized soil characterization is warranted. Do not infer compaction from ponding alone; do not conduct an infiltration test or remedial work in this stage. |
| Observation first | Which outcome varies across real storms and whether it changes with rainfall, wetness, debris, or maintenance | Least disruptive and most aligned with the current unknowns. It can identify patterns and safety concerns but cannot quantify a water balance or prove causality without measured flow and suitable controls. MPCA’s assessment framework calls visual inspection quick but limited, while full monitoring is much more demanding [S-06]. |

The 2023 EPA handbook records past inlet issues and fixes: some early New York inlets were replaced or modified after poor conveyance, while a Portland opening was fitted with a bar after vehicles could enter. Those examples demonstrate that inlet fixes carry site-specific access and maintenance tradeoffs; they do not establish the right fix here [S-01].

## Modest later observation program (proposal only)
1. **Agree scope and permissions with the city partner.** Confirm which outcome matters most, who owns/maintains the practice, safe observation locations, public-access restrictions, and the local drawdown/overflow criteria. Obtain records above and identify the visible inlet, garden surface, intended overflow, and discharge route without disturbing them. A survey is needed before geometry, drainage area, or design capacity is calculated.
2. **Establish a fixed baseline.** On dry days, take repeatable, time-stamped photos from an approved safe location. Note curb/gutter and garden condition, visible leaf/sediment/mulch blockage, plants, erosion/settlement, and whether any outlet is visible. Do not clear or rearrange materials for the study; if the owner must do routine or safety maintenance, log date, scope, and before/after condition.
3. **Track a small natural sample of storms.** During one partner-approved local observation season, aim for roughly 6–10 safely observable events, including contrasting shorter/longer duration, lower/higher short-interval intensity, and relatively dry/wet antecedent conditions where weather permits. This is exploratory, not a statistically powered performance evaluation. If the season lacks a useful contrast, report the missing class instead of extrapolating. Use a nearby quality-controlled gauge with interval data if representative; otherwise consider a temporary rain gauge only if the partner approves its placement and maintenance. Log event start/end, total rainfall, interval/peak intensity if available, and prior 24–72-hour rainfall or days since the previous event. Treat antecedent rainfall as a proxy, not a soil-moisture measurement.
4. **For each event, record each outcome separately.** From a safe position, use consistent, time-stamped photos or a field sheet to note whether approaching gutter flow enters, bypasses, or both; observed debris at the opening before/after; whether garden ponding forms; maximum depth at an approved fixed reference if available; visible overflow/underdrain flow and its destination; erosion/scour; and recession checks shortly after rainfall ends and around 6, 24, and 48 hours later. Record exact observation times and missed observations. Do not enter the road or moving water and do not infer a flow volume from a photograph.
5. **Compare events without overstating causality.** Group by rainfall total, duration, short-interval intensity, antecedent rainfall proxy, and pre-event debris/maintenance condition. Compare similar events when they occur, and note when multiple factors changed together. If cleaning or another owner action occurs, treat it as a new period; sparse before/after events cannot establish that the action caused a change. A more quantitative stage would require partner-approved flow/level instrumentation and a qualified monitoring plan.
6. **Escalate or stop.** Stop volunteer observations during unsafe weather, traffic, moving water, poor visibility, or where access would enter a travel lane/private area without permission. Notify the city partner promptly for visible inlet/overflow blockage, active scour or settlement, water reaching a road, accessible route, building, or neighboring property, or persistent ponding beyond local criteria. MPCA uses a 48-hour post-rain check as general O&M guidance but notes local/design context; it is not assumed to be the governing threshold here [S-03]. Leave diagnosis, tests, repairs, routing changes, and any design decision to the qualified site partner.
