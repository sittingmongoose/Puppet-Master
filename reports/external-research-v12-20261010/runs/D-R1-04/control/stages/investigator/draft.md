# D-R1-04-control — investigator draft

**Purpose.** Research proposal for discussion with a qualified city/site partner. This is not a construction, drainage, repair, or compliance design. All site details remain hypothetical. The first task is to find out whether the reported variability is at the curb opening, in storage/drawdown after entry, or in the downstream route.

## Proposed study position

Start with records and safe observation, before selecting a repair. A useful first outcome may be to document that some bypass is expected or acceptable for certain events, but that choice depends on the city’s objective and the original design intent. Do not assume every storm should enter the garden. Do not assume that flow passing the opening indicates poor infiltration.

Track four measures separately:

1. **Entry capture:** whether runoff approaching the curb opening enters, bypasses, or does both.
2. **Temporary storage:** whether water ponds after entry and how the visible depth changes during and after rainfall.
3. **Infiltration/drawdown:** how quickly surface water recedes, and whether an underdrain or outlet conveys part of the water.
4. **Downstream effects:** where bypass, overflow, or outlet flow goes and whether visible erosion, scour, or access/property impacts occur.

Capture depends on approach direction, grade, opening orientation and condition, debris, and flow rate. The EPA bioretention handbook describes inlet-type and flow-path alternatives and notes that drainage area, grade, placement and clogging affect capture [S-01]. It also records location-specific inlet problems and fixes: some first-generation New York inlets were modified or replaced after poor conveyance; Portland added a bar after vehicles could enter one type of opening. These examples show that increased capture, safety, sediment collection, and maintenance access can interact. They are evidence for screening questions, not a site remedy.

After entry, storage and drawdown depend on basin geometry, available storage, prior wetness, media and underlying soils, sediment, vegetation, underdrain/outlet condition, and storm timing. An underdrained garden can discharge water that entered through its media; a no-underdrain garden may rely more directly on underlying soils. Verify the as-built configuration before interpreting ponding. The MPCA overview treats high-flow bypass and overflow as separate design paths, and its O&M guidance lists multiple possible causes for extended ponding or blocked entry [S-02, S-03, S-10].

## Installation and maintenance history

Request the records before diagnosing current behavior:

- Approved plans and as-built survey: contributing area, roadway/garden grades, curb opening and flow alignment, ponding and overflow elevations, bypass/outlet route, and any underdrain.
- Soil/media and construction records: specified and installed layers/materials, original soil or infiltration testing, construction phasing, sediment controls, inspection/closeout notes, photographs, and traffic/stockpile restrictions.
- Operations records: maintenance responsibility and agreement, inspection dates, leaf/sediment removal, mulch type/placement/renewal, plant replacement, erosion repairs, inlet/outlet cleaning, street sweeping, snow storage/deicing, utility or curb work, and earlier complaints/photos with event timing.
- Delivery record: date, access route, vehicle/load, unload point, whether it crossed the garden edge, any available before/after photos, rutting/settlement, or surface disturbance. Ask what the volunteers cleared and where/when mulch was added.

EPA guidance notes that as-built roadway plans can help identify slope, dimensions and inlet location, and that construction inspection, phasing, material, grading, and compaction can affect operation [S-01]. EPA and MPCA guidance also call for maintenance schedules, responsible parties, inspection, and debris/condition tracking [S-03, S-05]. These records could distinguish intended offline bypass from an unexpected obstruction or grade change and establish whether an underdrain exists. Missing records remain an uncertainty; they cannot be recreated from visible ponding.

## Evidence and transfer limits

- An Omaha USGS study measured rainfall-event inflow/outflow at two bioretention gardens. In one garden, two examples with similar event totals but different duration and maximum intensity had different outflow; the report also identifies antecedent moisture and timing of high intensity as possible factors. Some high-ponding events were excluded from event analysis. This supports event stratification, but the study’s garden dimensions, instruments, soils, and conditions do not transfer to this case [S-07].
- A five-year Madison USGS comparison used purpose-built roof-fed gardens with different soils and vegetation. Its abstract reports that intensity and antecedent dry days influenced saturation timing and that outcomes varied between the studied soils/planting. It does not establish this site’s soil or performance [S-08].
- A Queens field study reported that inlet bypass limited capture despite high infiltration at that site; it associated capture variation with event duration, total rainfall, and peak hourly intensity. Its sandy native soil, low loading ratio, and studied inlet geometry are different and unknown here; its capture percentages are not predictions [S-09].
- MPCA guidance identifies debris, surface sediment, ineffective pretreatment, grading, and compaction among possible considerations and uses a 48-hour post-rain inspection as general guidance in a stated context. Local rules and the actual design govern; this proposal does not treat 48 hours as a universal compliance threshold [S-03].
- The EPA assessment framework describes visual inspection as a quick, limited first level and full monitoring as much more effort. Therefore this proposal aims to discriminate mechanisms, not produce a regulatory credit or broad performance claim [S-06].

A simple water balance is **unsupported now**: no contributing-area boundary, rainfall record, runoff input, inlet/bypass flow, storage elevations, outlet/overflow data, or soil properties are supplied. A later partner-approved balance might be considered only after a survey and adequate event measurement; an assumed balance or diagram would be desk work, not validation.

## Proposed later observation program (not executed)

1. **Agree the decision question and permissions.** The city should state whether its first concern is entry, drawdown, or downstream consequence; identify the owner/operator, safe observation points, public-access limits, approved local drawdown/overflow criterion, and whether any temporary gauge or reference marker is permitted. If the objective accepts limited capture for some storms, record that as the baseline rather than assuming full capture is required.
2. **Review documents and photograph a baseline.** Using an approved safe location on dry days, take repeatable time-stamped photos of the inlet, approach, garden, visible outlet/overflow, debris, mulch, plant condition, erosion, and settlement. Note that these are visual observations, not a survey or soil measurement. Do not move debris or alter maintenance for the study. If routine maintenance cannot wait, the owner should proceed and the observer should log what changed and when.
3. **Observe a modest sample of natural storms.** For one agreed local season, aim for roughly 6–10 safely observable events with contrasting duration, short-interval intensity, and antecedent rainfall where weather permits. This is exploratory and not statistically powered. If the natural season lacks a contrast, state the missing conditions or extend only by partner decision. Use representative quality-controlled interval rainfall data if available; otherwise use a temporary gauge only with partner approval. Record event start/end, total rainfall, interval or peak intensity when available, and previous 24–72-hour rainfall/days since rain as a wetness proxy—not as a soil-moisture measurement.
4. **Record each outcome separately.** For each event, log date, observer, rain source, duration, total/intensity, prior rain, pre-event debris/maintenance, and whether approaching gutter flow entered, bypassed, or both. Use safe time-stamped photos to record ponding formation, a maximum visible depth at an approved fixed reference if available, whether water recedes, and any visible outlet/overflow route, scour, or downstream impact. Recheck shortly after rainfall ends and around 6, 24, and 48 hours afterward; record exact times and missed checks. Do not enter the road, garden, or moving water during storms. Do not convert visual observations to flow volumes.
5. **Compare cautiously.** Compare events by rainfall amount, duration, available intensity data, antecedent-rain proxy, debris state, and maintenance period. Look for similar storms with different entry/ponding behavior; note when variables changed together. Treat any owner-led cleaning as a new condition period. A few before/after events cannot prove that maintenance caused a change. If event observations cannot distinguish capture from drawdown, ask the partner whether approved flow/level instrumentation or qualified soil/media review is warranted.
6. **Stop and escalate.** Stop volunteer observation for lightning, unsafe traffic, moving water, poor visibility, or unapproved private/public access. Promptly notify the city partner of a blocked inlet/overflow, active erosion or settlement, water reaching a road/access route/building/neighboring property, or ponding beyond the applicable local/design criterion. Do not delay owner safety maintenance to preserve a comparison. No volunteer repair, excavation, curb work, runoff redirection, or infiltration testing is part of this plan.

## Corrections, optional improvements, and owner decisions

**Corrections to the brief:** None. The hypothetical assumptions and stated unknowns are internally consistent with the evidence limits. Do not change the report of intermittent flow, recent mulch/leaf work, or possible delivery compaction into established site facts.

**Optional improvements to a later field packet:** Add a one-page event sheet with separate fields for capture, ponding, drawdown, downstream route, weather, and maintenance; label each photo with date/time and viewing location; maintain one fixed observation point; and include an “unknown/not observed” option. These improve repeatability without implying measurement precision.
