# Research proposal: moisture and crisp texture in cooperative dry-snack bags

**Case:** ER12-D-R1-01-FRESH  
**Status:** Investigator-stage proposal based on the supplied hypothetical brief and the literature review below. No food or package sample, production record, measurement, or test result was supplied.

## Provisional path

Do not order a pilot film on the anecdote alone. First reconstruct the supplier and cooling transitions from traceable records, then run a small blocked comparison that changes one package factor and one handling factor while keeping product and storage conditions controlled. A no-film-change cooling/storage arm is useful and reversible. If returned-pack records show variable seals, test seal consistency on the current film before attributing the issue to film permeability.

Three outcomes must stay separate throughout: retention of the preferred texture, package integrity, and food safety. Better crispness does not demonstrate an intact seal or safe food. A visually intact package does not demonstrate barrier performance. No conclusion about shelf life, compliance, or sale readiness follows from the anecdote or a film data sheet.

## What is known, and what is not

The cooperative changed the bag supplier and cooling routine in the same month. Some returned samples reportedly have poor texture and the bags look intact. The case is hypothetical and has no supplied tests. This is not a natural experiment with a known result: the changes coincided, and no batch-linked records establish what happened to any returned sample. The current film, offered film, seal design, equipment, cooling duration, recipe, starting moisture, package dimensions, fill mass, storage route, return age, and customer-valued texture attribute are unknown.

The candidate mechanisms below are hypotheses to discriminate, not findings about the cooperative:

1. **Water-vapor transfer through the film.** A product with lower water activity than its surroundings can gain moisture; temperature, humidity, product composition, film structure, thickness, and exposed surface all matter. Moisture content and water activity are related through product-specific sorption behavior. Water can soften or plasticize a crisp matrix and change fracture or sound. Arimi et al. reported a critical activity interval of 0.51–0.59 for one Crackerbread product under one equilibration and puncture/acoustic method [S01]. Hough et al. found different ideal activities for four commercial biscuits, and that the perceived change depended on the sensory cue [S02]. These values are not thresholds for this unknown snack.
2. **Moisture redistribution within the snack and sealed pack.** A newly baked piece may have internal moisture gradients. During cooling and later storage, water can redistribute within a piece and between the product and package headspace even if there is no ambient leak. Published biscuit work connects geometry and internal moisture distribution with post-bake checking [S07]; cracker sorption rates also differed with cellular morphology [S03]. The case has no initial measurements to support or reject this route.
3. **A seal path or other package defect.** The seal is a separate ingress path from the flat film. Seal contamination, wrinkles, material properties, process variation, and later handling are recognized contributors to flexible-package leak formation [S08]. In a retail-biscuit study, measured package water-vapor transfer for rollpack and horizontal-flowpack packages was 1.1–3.0 times the film-area-based theoretical value; seals were impaired, especially at the transverse seal. Vertical-flowpack packages in that study had better seal integrity [S12]. This is a historical comparator, not a diagnosis or machine-setting recommendation for the cooperative.
4. **Cooling, ambient humidity, and handling.** Exposed product can encounter humidity and temperature changes during cooling, waiting, packing, storage, transport, or return handling. These can affect the starting state, subsequent moisture movement, and damage. Record the actual path; do not replace it with nominal room conditions.
5. **A package-material exception at high humidity.** If a candidate or current structure contains EVOH, its moisture-barrier behavior may depend strongly on humidity. A four-grade EVOH study at 23 ± 2 °C found water permeability increased by up to two orders of magnitude above water activity 0.75 [S13]. This only applies if the film construction contains EVOH and must not be transferred to an unidentified film. A separate dry-food case found high humidity compromised one PA/EVOH-containing bag’s barrier performance [S04].

More than one mechanism can operate at once. A supplier change may alter layer stack, gauge, sealant, stiffness, sealability, or total package area; a cooling change may alter product temperature, exposed time, or moisture distribution. Film WVTR alone cannot account for all of these.

## Comparable implementation and production history

The evidence supports useful comparisons but does not identify a winning material for this snack.

- **A failed package-level seal comparison:** Vercelino Alves et al. described six wrapper structures, including BOPP/LDPE and metallized-BOPP constructions, and examined retail biscuits from nine product types and six manufacturers made on three form-fill-seal machine types. Their material WVTR comparisons were reported at 38 °C/90% RH: metallized-BOPP structures were 0.5–1.0 g/m²/day and the other tested structures 2.3–4.0 g/m²/day. The rollpack and horizontal-flowpack packages had impaired seals, especially at the transverse seal, and measured package transmission 1.1–3.0 times the theoretical film-area estimate, while vertical-flowpack seals were reported as good [S12]. The accessible publisher abstract does not disclose full machine maintenance history or exact run settings. It supports measuring the finished package rather than assuming the film value controls the result; it does not imply that one machine type is better for this cooperative.
- **A cookie material comparison:** Baele et al. reported caramelized cookies in PP, acryl-coated PVdC/PP, and metallized PP flowpacks at 22 °C/50% RH. The metallized-PP package extended textural acceptability in that study. The abstract reports WVTRs of 5, 4.2, and 0.4 g/m²/day, respectively, but does not report their measurement conditions or the full package/seal details; do not compare those values directly with a supplier data sheet [S04]. In a separate whole-milk-powder comparison in the same report, high humidity compromised one PA/EVOH/PA/PE bag; that is a humidity exception from another product, not a cookie result [S04].
- **A controlled same-batch biscuit comparison:** Balestra et al. split biscuits from one production batch among metalized OPP/paper, metalized PLA/paper, and metalized OPP/EVA/paper variants. Bags averaged 300 g and 1,078 cm². The accelerated condition was 35 °C/50% RH for 105 days, with scheduled destructive sampling. Moisture and water activity differed by material, but the abstract reports no significant textural effect [S05]. This is a useful example of batch control and of measuring texture separately from hydration; the accelerated condition and formulation differ from the cooperative’s unknown product.
- **A zipper-bag implementation:** Jo et al. made 40–45 μm filler-embedded LDPE zipper bags, 25 × 30.5 cm, and used a continuous-band heat sealer. They compared the bags with typical LDPE zipper bags at 25 °C/50% RH for 20 days and monitored weight, moisture, water activity, and texture in several snack types [S06]. The active bag had 21% lower reported water-vapor capability and delayed several deterioration measures, but the outcome varied by snack. This is a reclosable-bag home-storage study, not a direct package substitution for sealed samples.
- **Cooling and product structure:** The BRICE collaboration compared round/thick biscuits with thinner rectangular pierced biscuits over 15 days and used center/surface water measurements; checking was reported within 1–2 days after baking in those biscuits [S07]. Its short report does not specify the cooperative’s cooling line or a transferable cooling target. A separate cracker-cooling thesis abstract identifies product temperature, cracker depth, and line speed as variables in a cooling model; the sponsor-proprietary body is unavailable [S14]. Together they support recording geometry, line/loading conditions, initial distribution, and early storage observations, but provide no transferable setpoint.

An implementation failure with measured package/seal behavior was found [S12]. The reviewed literature did not disclose maintenance cost or a recurring maintenance failure history applicable to this case. Across-study WVTR values are not normalized here: the test conditions differ or are absent, and no film-to-package or shelf-life calculation was made. The cited temperatures, RH values, storage periods, and package formats are comparator conditions only, not proposed cooperative settings. The food-product comparator studies are mainly about crackers and biscuits; the package-integrity reviews and methods are broader. Some full articles are restricted; those access limits are recorded in [the source map](source-map.json). No supplier-specific change record, regression report, or failure history was supplied for the cooperative.

## Proposed controlled pilot

### Questions and factors

The pilot should answer two separate questions: does the package system change moisture ingress or integrity under controlled handling, and does a no-film-change cooling/storage practice change the chosen texture outcome? A modest first design is a blocked 2 × 2 comparison:

1. Current package + current handling baseline.
2. Candidate package system + current handling.
3. Current package + standardized cooling/storage handling.
4. Candidate package system + standardized handling.

The candidate-package factor must be chosen before the run. It can be a new film/package system **or** an integrity/seal-control intervention on the same film. Do not change material and seal process as separate uncontrolled changes and then assign the result to one of them. If a new film needs a different compatible sealing window, document that window and interpret the factor as the entire package system. A separate seal factor can be studied later if resources allow.

### Traceability and confounder control

Block by independent production batch and split each batch across all treatment arms. At least three independent batches would be a practical exploratory floor if production allows; it is not a power guarantee. Set package replication from baseline variability, a preselected meaningful effect, and available lab capacity. Do not count multiple snack pieces from one bag as independent package replicates. Randomize treatment assignment within each batch and record the randomization. Keep recipe/ingredient lots, bake conditions, product geometry, fill mass, package dimensions, time from oven to measurement, and storage schedule the same where possible; record any differences. Use distinct unopened bags for destructive or opening-based timepoint measurements rather than repeatedly opening one bag.

Recover and record: supplier and film lot; actual layer structure and gauge; package width/length/area, fill mass and headspace; sealing machine and seal type; time-stamped equipment/process/maintenance changes; closure observations; production batch; cooling duration and product temperature; cooling/holding/storage temperature and RH; packing time; transport and handling events; return date and sample condition. A returned package code must resolve to those records. Do not infer a seal setting from the literature.

### Observations

At the start of each batch, characterize the product before packaging with moisture content, water activity, piece mass and geometry. If the product has a meaningful surface/interior distinction, sample those locations separately to look for a starting gradient. If feasible, measure package headspace temperature/RH using separate sacrificial or instrumented packs so the measurement does not compromise integrity samples. Record product temperature and elapsed exposed time at packaging. Measure the customer-defined texture attribute at baseline and scheduled storage points; choose an instrumental fracture/acoustic method that fits the product, and add a sensory assessment only if the cooperative can define and safely support it. The historical crackerbread activity threshold [S01] and other biscuit sensory results [S02] cannot substitute for the cooperative’s endpoint.

Log actual temperature and RH during storage and, if available, cooling and staging. Pair texture with product moisture and water activity. Identify each film roll, package, batch, timepoint, and test. For package integrity, inspect seals systematically and use a package-level leak method that is suitable for the actual format, alongside seal-strength sampling where appropriate. F88/F88M measures seal force/failure mode; use one test fixture/technique consistently across the series. F2096 addresses gross leaks, is destructive, and reports 250 μm sensitivity at 81% probability; neither method establishes a moisture shelf life or food safety [S10, S11]. Obtain current and candidate film WVTR values with methods and conditions, or compare both under the same agreed film-test conditions [S09]. Do not present a supplier data sheet as an executed test.

### Decision rule

Before unblinding treatment labels, the cooperative should define (a) the texture cue customers value, (b) the minimum practically meaningful difference, and (c) the intended storage and handling distribution. Advance a candidate package only if it gives a repeatable, practically meaningful texture advantage over the current-package baseline across independent batches under the same handling, with no deterioration in the separately recorded package-integrity outcome. A decrease in moisture uptake without a meaningful texture gain is not a crispness solution. If standardized handling improves texture in current film, prefer that reversible no-film-change path while keeping the film decision open. If results are inconsistent by batch or there is a treatment interaction, do not declare a winner; add controlled batches or revisit the mechanism. Package sizing and inventory duration remain owner decisions informed by cost, portion, waste, and distribution data.

Food safety requires a separate qualified assessment based on the actual product, process, package, and intended handling. The pilot described here is not a safety study and cannot establish a safe shelf life, regulatory compliance, or readiness for sale.

These gaps prevent selecting a specific film, setting test conditions and sample size, calculating an expected moisture gain, choosing a valid texture threshold, attributing the historical complaints, or making a shelf-life or safety claim.
