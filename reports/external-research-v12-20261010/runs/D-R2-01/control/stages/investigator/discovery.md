## Findings relevant to competing explanations

- **Droplets are ambiguous.** Guttation can produce droplets at leaf tips or margins under night conditions when transpiration is low, with high humidity, cool air and warmer root zone described as favorable conditions. By applying the sources' general dew-point condensation condition to leaf surfaces (an inference, not a case observation), condensation is a separate possibility when leaf surface temperature reaches or falls below the surrounding air's dew point [S2, S8]. The Missouri extension note describes guttation and rare bacterial-entry circumstances, but neither its appearance nor that general possibility diagnoses this crop or a pathogen [S1]. Record whether droplets are at margins/tips or broadly on surfaces, whether leaves are dry/wet before irrigation, time of first appearance and disappearance, crop position, and concurrent air/leaf/root-zone conditions. Do not treat droplets alone as proof of root-zone excess, condensation, infection, or disease risk.

- **Root-zone water and growth are linked, but not in a universal dose-response.** A controlled greenhouse study of basil, dill, parsley and sage in 11.4-cm pots with 75% peat/25% coarse perlite and controlled-release fertilizer maintained substrate VWC from 0.15 to 0.45 m³/m³ for four weeks. Growth traits generally changed with VWC; responses differed among species/traits, and water-use efficiency did not uniformly improve at the driest treatment. “No visual damage” at a low treatment is evidence only for that crop setup and period, not a safe setting for this unknown crop [S6]. A separate sensor-controlled soilless-basil study used perlite:coco (1:1), 0.20/0.30/0.40 m³/m³ setpoints and two leaching rates; growth was similar across setpoints, while fresh weight tended higher at higher leaching, plausibly because leaching lowered EC. Its abstract is accessible, while its publisher full text is subscription restricted in the examined record [S7]. Together these studies argue for measuring growth, applied water, and substrate state separately, and for recording substrate/EC and crop context before comparing outcomes.

- **Position and equipment may matter.** A primary study across two southwestern Ontario commercial greenhouses monitored ‘Genovese Gigante’ basil and ‘Get Mee’ bellflower, and measured within-house evaporation as an integrated proxy for local climate. Evaporation was nonuniform; maxima were about 1.6–1.9 times minima in sampled greenhouses. Substrate-water profiles also varied considerably. The study did not establish that a door caused a gradient, nor that the observed irrigation regimes caused growth differences; sensor adequacy and placement depended on crop and greenhouse heterogeneity [S5]. Treat “near the door” as a prespecified location stratum to map, not as a mechanism.

- **Air movement and ventilation are distinct.** Extension guidance describes ventilation as replacing indoor air with outside air, while internal horizontal-airflow fans recirculate air. Ventilation can remove moist air and help control temperature/humidity; in cool weather it can increase heating demand, and natural ventilation depends on wind/thermal gradients and may be less uniform. Seasonal conditions and greenhouse design govern applicability; published example humidity thresholds for tomato leaf mold are not thresholds for these herbs [S2]. The Virginia Tech operating guide recommends representative, aspirated crop-height sensors away from walls, fans and doors, and describes HAF circulation practice. These are inspection/design cues, not instructions to impose an unverified schedule on this greenhouse [S3].

## Approaches and alternatives worth comparing

1. **Observation-first (lowest disturbance):** map every pot/plant by bench coordinates, species/cultivar, age/size, substrate/container and irrigation zone; take standardized dated photographs; score lower-leaf color/symptoms and growth separately; record droplets by location and timing; retain the present schedules during a baseline unless an independent welfare concern requires a safe stop. This can show whether an apparent door gradient persists after structured sampling and whether changes co-occur with dawn climate or irrigation events.
2. **Irrigation/substrate inquiry:** reconstruct timer program and actual delivery; measure output at representative emitters/positions and check drainage/standing water; log applied volume by zone; sample substrate moisture at consistent depth and representative high/average/low positions. A calibrated moisture probe or gravimetric check is a candidate measurement approach, not a treatment prescription. Manufacturer calibration, substrate-specific response, placement, and independent check sensors matter [S5, S6, S8].
3. **Ventilation/microclimate inquiry:** reconstruct circulation-fan and vent schedules and actual operation; inspect fan direction, belts, shutters, dust/blockage and measured airflow; map canopy-height temperature/RH (and, if available, leaf-surface temperature/leaf wetness) across door, center, and opposite-side positions, with timestamps across night, dawn, and irrigation. UF fan guidance documents that reversed rotation after wiring work may go unnoticed and that dust/unbalance can materially reduce airflow, so the schedule alone is not evidence of delivered circulation [S4]. Log outside conditions and season; ventilation may trade humidity removal against heat/energy and must not be changed from generic targets [S2].
4. **Comparable implementation evidence:** the sensor-based basil and herb studies are useful precedents, but differ in species, cultivars, pot size, peat/perlite versus perlite/coco substrate, greenhouse, controls, crop stage and duration. The microclimate study involved commercial Ontario houses and measured location effects; it is not a direct match for an informal teaching bench. Report those differences alongside every transferred result [S5–S7].

## Operating-history reconstruction

The two schedule changes happened before symptoms were noticed, so their simultaneous timing is confounded. Obtain dated before/after timer and controller settings, who changed them and why, irrigation event duration/frequency, water source/volume and line/zone changes; compare programmed operation with actual valve/pump function and measured delivery. For the fan system, recover controller events and service/wiring history; identify whether the changed control is internal circulation or exhaust ventilation; inspect physical direction, belts, blades/shutters, obstructions/screens, and airflow. Record sensor model, position relative to canopy/door/fan/intake, calibration dates, data interval, missing periods and whether a control sensor was independently checked. Record crop moves, spacing, sowing/transplant dates, substrate/lot, watering practices, seasonal light/weather, and any other management changes. Do not assume that the timing of a change proves causality.

## Proposed measures and decision logic

Keep four outcome families separate: (a) leaf symptoms, including affected-plant count and lower-leaf position/age; (b) plant growth, using repeatable height/canopy or leaf-area/count measures; (c) water use, metered or logged volume by zone/plant plus drainage where feasible; and (d) possible diagnostic condition, which requires competent specialist assessment where indicated. Add environmental covariates (air temperature/RH, calculated dew point/VPD if sensor quality supports it, canopy/leaf temperature, light, and a direct leaf-wetness observation) and substrate moisture/EC where practical. Use calibrated, independently checked sensors, report location, sampling interval and calibration, and preserve raw logs; greenhouse conditions vary in space and time and control sensors may fail [S8]. No values are available now.

For a later baseline, propose 7–14 days spanning at least several representative dawns and irrigation cycles, with unchanged routine as the observational comparator where safe. This is a planning range, not a literature-validated minimum; extend or reschedule if weather, crop stage, or the production cycle makes it unrepresentative. Pre-register a map and scoring rubric, repeat observations at a fixed pre-irrigation and post-dawn time, and block by bench zone plus cultivar/age/substrate. If baseline evidence points to delivery/root-zone variability, pilot only one reversible irrigation/delivery factor against current practice in matched, randomized blocks while holding ventilation constant. If it points to air movement or condensation conditions, pilot one reversible, locally safe circulation/ventilation factor while holding irrigation constant. If controls operate house-wide, use a cautious, documented time-series comparison matched as far as possible by outside conditions and call it exploratory rather than randomized evidence. Set crop-welfare stopping rules with the instructor before any pilot; no dose, setpoint or treatment is proposed here. If the instructor's objective is water use, growth or teaching consistency, choose that as the primary outcome in advance and retain the other measures as secondary outcomes.

If a sample is requested, follow the receiving lab's rules; NC State's greenhouse guidance, for example, asks for representative symptomatic material, dry foliage, slightly moist (not soggy) media, and symptom-distribution photos [S9].
