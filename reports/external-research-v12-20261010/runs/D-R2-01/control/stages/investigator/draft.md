# Draft research proposal — Uneven leaf symptoms in a teaching greenhouse

Case: ER12-D-R2-01-FRESH  
Run: D-R2-01-control  
Status: investigator draft following discovery freeze and release of the assigned plan. The plan itself is not reproduced in this research packet.

## Proposal in brief

Do not change the teaching crop protocol from the anecdote. First establish which plants, leaves, positions and mornings show each observation, then check what the two changed controls actually delivered. Treat root-zone water/delivery and greenhouse air movement/microclimate as competing, potentially interacting hypotheses. Keep a low-disturbance observation-first option available. Only after a baseline, a defined instructor priority and crop-welfare stop rules should a later authorized pilot compare one reversible factor at a time.

This is a research proposal, not today's treatment advice. It does not identify a cause, diagnose a pathogen or deficiency, set an irrigation or fan target, or report a crop intervention. The information supplied does not include species/cultivar, crop age, substrate, irrigation hardware, environmental records, bench coordinates, samples or outcomes.

## What is known, inferred, and still unknown

**Reported in the hypothetical brief:** a bench of potted herbs; informal reports of pale lower leaves and occasional morning droplets; both the irrigation timer and circulation-fan schedule changed before noticing symptoms; plants near the door seem less affected; the instructor wants a proposal before changing protocol.

**Not observed or measured:** exact crop identities and ages; symptom count/severity; locations on each leaf; droplet source; irrigation volume/uniformity; substrate moisture or EC; temperature, RH, dew point, airflow or leaf wetness; actual controller/fan operation; seasonal conditions; before/after differences; diagnostic results.

**Reasoned inferences, not case findings:**

- Because two schedules changed before the symptoms were noticed, timing alone cannot distinguish their effects. The changes are confounded until their dates, actual operation and independent outcome measures are reconstructed.
- Greenhouse heterogeneity is plausible: a primary study found substantial within-house evaporation and substrate-water variation, but did not establish a door effect. The informal door pattern should therefore become a mapped comparison, not a causal conclusion [S5].
- By applying general dew-point condensation physics to leaves (an inference, not a case observation), leaf-surface cooling to/below ambient dew point is one possible route to surface water. Guttation is a different possibility: the extension source describes leaf-tip/margin droplets under particular overnight/root-pressure conditions. Droplets alone cannot distinguish these routes or identify a cause [S1, S2, S8].
- Root-zone water can affect growth, but published herb responses vary by species, substrate, setpoint and study length. A growth response, water-use change, symptom response and diagnosis are separate outcomes [S6, S7].

The position evidence does not support assuming that plants near the door are protected or exposed. A monitored study of greenhouse basil and bellflower found that local evaporation differed across positions and substrate VWC varied, with sensor placement/count dependent on crop and microclimate [S5]. ## Compare three useful approaches

### 1. Observation-first

For an initial period, preserve the current routine where safe and make the anecdote reproducible: draw a bench map; label positions and crop cohorts; photograph and score the same plants/leaves at fixed times; record visible droplets before and after irrigation and near dawn; record watering events and weather/control state. This option has the least crop disturbance and can show whether the informal door pattern survives structured observation. Its limitation is that observation alone may not identify cause if the two control schedules remain correlated or key variables are not measured.

### 2. Irrigation/substrate inquiry

Treat irrigation as delivery plus root-zone condition, not just a timer setting. Check the historic/current program, event duration, zone/line layout and actual valve/pump function. Measure representative emitter or application output with a repeatable collection/weight method, note drainage, and compare substrate moisture at consistent locations/depths. A substrate-moisture probe, gravimetric check, water meter, or sensor-based scheduling system may be useful measurement/control approaches only after substrate-specific calibration and safe operating bounds are known. Sensor-based herb studies show both the potential and transfer limits: Currey et al. used peat/perlite with four culinary herbs for four weeks; Montesano et al. used perlite/coco basil and different setpoints/leaching. Neither supplies a universal value for this crop [S6, S7]. Do not lower watering as an unvalidated diagnostic trial.

### 3. Ventilation/microclimate inquiry

Reconstruct which control changed: internal circulation, exhaust ventilation, natural vents, or a combination. Recover schedules and actual run states, inspect maintenance/service records and confirm that fan rotation and airflow are real. Map environmental readings at canopy level across door, center and opposite-side positions; include overnight/dawn intervals and outside weather. An independent, calibrated logger is preferable to relying only on the control sensor. UF/IFAS ventilation material describes seasonal humidity/heat tradeoffs; Virginia Tech guidance discusses representative sensor placement; UF/IFAS fan guidance documents plausible maintenance faults, including reversed rotation after repair. These are inspection evidence, not thresholds or prescriptions for this unmeasured house [S2–S4, S8].

Internal air circulation and ventilation should not be treated as interchangeable. A circulation fan may reduce local stagnation without exchanging air; venting exchanges air but may increase heat/energy demand in cool weather. Natural ventilation also depends on winds/thermal gradients and may be less uniform [S2, S3]. Do not copy tomato humidity examples or winter fan practice as herb setpoints.

## Reconstruct operating history before interpreting transfer

1. **Irrigation:** obtain dated old/new timer values, change reason and operator; event durations/frequencies; water source, line/zone or emitter changes; controller event logs; pump/valve failures; plant moves; and any changes in hand watering. Compare programmed events with measured delivery at representative positions. Record drainage and substrate/media history.
2. **Air system:** obtain dated fan/vent schedule and controller history; identify whether each component circulates or exchanges air; retrieve repair, wiring and maintenance history; record fan direction/speed, belts, blades, shutters, screens, blockage, vent position and measured airflow. A scheduled “on” event is not proof of actual air movement. The UF/IFAS guide specifically says repair/wiring can reverse rotation and describes airflow loss from dust/unbalance [S4].
3. **Measurement provenance:** record greenhouse/bench map, sensor make/model and exact location relative to crop, door, fan, vent and intake; calibration date and method; sampling/logging interval; missing data; and whether the greenhouse control probe is independently checked. Greenhouse guidelines emphasize sensor placement, calibration, time/location detail and independent measurements because environments vary and control sensors can fail [S8].
4. **Crop and season:** record crop species/cultivar, sowing/transplant dates, age/size mix, pot/substrate type/lot, spacing/bench moves, routine water/media management, seasonal light/weather and other changes. The published studies differ in crop, substrate, climate, irrigation and duration; list these differences rather than treating them as interchangeable [S5–S7].

This reconstruction investigates operation/history without assuming a fault or changing an operating system.

## Baseline and later limited pilot

### Proposed interpretable baseline

- **Duration:** plan 7–14 days, covering several representative mornings and irrigation cycles. This is a practical proposal, not a validated universal minimum; extend it if seasonal conditions or crop stage are not representative.
- **Map and sample:** enumerate the bench; capture position, species/cultivar, crop age/size, pot/media, watering zone, door proximity and visible symptom status. If the bench is too large to score every plant, select a documented stratified sample across door/center/far positions and cohorts; keep the plant, not each leaf or sensor reading, as the unit for plant-level comparisons.
- **Fixed observation:** use a simple written rubric for lower-leaf color/symptom extent, leaf age/position and plant growth (repeatable height/canopy width or leaf count). Take standardized photographs from the same view. Record droplet location (tip/margin/broad surface), presence/time, and whether leaves were wet before irrigation. Do not convert scores into diagnoses.
- **Separate endpoints:** (a) visible symptoms, (b) growth, (c) water applied and drained, and (d) any condition needing diagnosis. Symptom reduction alone is not a cause or crop-quality result. Add only feasible covariates: calibrated substrate moisture/EC, air temperature/RH, dew point/VPD where instruments support it, leaf-surface temperature/leaf wetness, light and control state. Report where/when each reading was taken.
- **Analysis:** graph repeated outcomes by time and mapped position; compare crop cohorts and watering zones; align with actual irrigation and fan/vent events. Document missingness, uncertainty and any deviations.

### Proposed limited comparison

After baseline review and a confirmed instructor priority, select **one** factor that the observations support and can be changed reversibly without compromising plant welfare:

- If delivery/root-zone heterogeneity is the signal, compare current practice with one preapproved, low-risk delivery/scheduling contrast in matched, randomized blocks, while holding ventilation constant. Do not use a literature-derived setpoint without local calibration or introduce deficit watering.
- If air movement/dawn microclimate is the signal, compare one preapproved reversible circulation/ventilation contrast in independently controlled comparable zones, while holding irrigation constant. If the system is house-wide, use an explicitly exploratory, time-series comparison matched as far as practical for outside weather/crop stage; do not call it randomized causal evidence.
- If neither signal is clear, continue structured observation or seek specialist input rather than forcing an intervention. Set stop criteria in advance with the instructor (worsening symptoms, wilting or other welfare concern); stop and seek advice if reached. No specific crop treatment, pesticide, fertilizer dose or control setpoint is recommended.

A pilot estimates whether the next study is feasible and what variability it must handle; it is not proof of yield improvement, disease prevention or food safety. Determine replication/power from actual bench size and baseline variability rather than inventing a sample count now.

## Escalation and owner decisions

Request local specialist/plant-clinic assessment if symptoms spread or intensify, new lesions or root/stem signs emerge, plants wilt/decline/die, or the pattern remains unexplained after verified irrigation/environmental records. Escalate sooner if a diagnostic answer is necessary before changing the teaching protocol. Preserve photographs, map, crop/media history and controller records. Follow the receiving clinic's current submission rules; NC State's greenhouse page is an example, not a universal protocol [S9].

Before any pilot, the instructor should decide:

1. Is the primary decision about plant welfare, water use, or teaching reliability? Rank one primary outcome and name secondary outcomes.
2. Which crop conditions and operating functions must remain unchanged for teaching, and what reversible change is permissible?
3. What signs trigger an immediate welfare stop and specialist call?
4. Who can authorize sensor placement, access operating logs and submit representative samples?

## Corrections, optional improvements, decisions, and unresolved points

**Corrections to unsupported interpretations:** a droplet is not proof of guttation, condensation, overwatering, disease or infection; pale lower leaves do not establish deficiency or pathogen; the door pattern does not establish microclimate cause; timing after two schedule changes does not isolate either schedule; generic humidity or sensor thresholds from other crops are not local operating targets.

**Optional improvements proposed:** fixed-time symptom photographs and scores; bench and crop-cohort map; independent calibrated monitoring; output collection at multiple irrigation positions; logs of schedules, maintenance and actual equipment operation; prewritten stop rules; spatial blocks and a single-factor pilot only when safe. These depend on staff time, equipment, bench size and instructor approval.

**Owner decisions:** primary outcome and teaching objective; permitted and prohibited operational changes; crop-welfare stop criteria; whether specialist sampling is authorized; acceptable measurement burden/energy limits. No row-by-row product decision is implied.

**Already covered:** all original clauses above are retained.  
**Rejected:** immediate intervention; assumed prior schedule was correct; single-cause conclusion; setpoint/chemical prescription; “symptoms improved, therefore cause found”; direct transfer of one study's sensor value.  
**Uncertain:** actual droplet source; cause(s) of pallor; whether door position matters; whether schedules changed delivered conditions; crop/media/season; whether diagnosis is needed; which outcome the instructor values most.
