## Competing explanations and observations that could distinguish them

| Account to investigate | Plausible mechanism, not a finding | Useful discriminating observations |
|---|---|---|
| Irrigation timing, delivery, drainage, or substrate state | A timer change may change event timing, frequency, or duration. Unequal emitters/zones, drainage, pot size, substrate, or plant stage could produce different root-zone conditions. Root-zone water can affect growth, but that does not identify the cause of pallor. | Recover old/new settings and event records; check pump/valve operation and representative output by line and position; measure applied volume and drainage; measure substrate moisture at consistent depth and mapped positions; record substrate and EC where feasible. |

| Crop cohort, age, substrate, or other management | Species, cultivar, crop age, transplant date, media, spacing, plant moves, or hand-watering history can alter water demand and normal appearance. | Map each plant or a documented stratified sample by cohort, size/age, media/container, watering zone, position, and moves. | Condition requiring specialist diagnosis | Biological or abiotic causes remain possible; this vignette cannot distinguish them. | Preserve dated photographs and distribution history. Morning droplets are not proof of overwatering, condensation, guttation, infection, or disease risk. Record whether droplets are at tips/margins or broadly on surfaces, first appearance/disappearance, whether leaves were wet before irrigation, and the simultaneous air/leaf/root-zone observations. Guttation is described as hydathode exudation at tips or margins under favorable overnight/root-pressure conditions [S1]. Applying general dew-point physics to leaf surfaces makes condensation another possibility, but that is an inference to test, not an observation in this case [S2, S8]. Pale lower leaves likewise do not establish a nutrient deficiency or pathogen.

## Evidence and transfer limits

The literature supports a measurement and comparison plan, not a cause or setpoint for this greenhouse.

- **Spatial variation and operating context (S5).** Henderson et al. monitored ‘Get Mee’ bellflower in one southwestern Ontario commercial greenhouse and ‘Genovese Gigante’ basil in another. The bellflower trial used 10.2 cm by 11.2 cm containers, a 78% peatmoss/22% perlite substrate, and subirrigation; the basil trial used a peat-based mix and subirrigation. Both houses listed heat maintained at 17.5 °C and vents set to open at 20.5 °C, but mean outside temperatures differed: 4.8 °C for the bellflower trial and 14.1 °C for basil. The evaporation assessment also included a 1960s research greenhouse; greenhouse A was an early-2000s commercial design, while B was built in 2015 and had more advanced climate-control systems. The study found spatial evaporation and substrate-water variation, but did not establish a door effect or show that a fan/vent change caused growth differences. Its crops, media, production systems, house age/design, outside conditions, and control context are not interchangeable with this teaching bench [S5].
- **Controlled culinary-herb study (S6).** Currey et al. grew basil, dill, parsley, and sage for four weeks in 11.4 cm pots with 75% peat/25% coarse perlite and controlled-release fertilizer. Their glass-glazed Iowa State greenhouse used fog cooling, radiant hot-water floor/perimeter heating, an environmental computer, day/night air setpoints of 23.0 ± 1 °C and 18.0 ± 1 °C, shade curtains tied to outdoor light, and supplemental high-pressure-sodium lighting. VWC treatments and plant responses were specific to that equipment, crop material, substrate, and duration. Growth responses differed by species/measure; the study is not a safe moisture target for an unknown crop [S6].
- **Abstract-only soilless-basil study (S7).** The accessible abstract describes greenhouse basil in perlite:coco (1:1), sensor setpoints, and leaching comparisons; it reports similar growth across setpoints and a fresh-weight tendency with higher leaching that may relate to EC. The publisher full text could not be accessed, so detailed climate controls and unreported methods remain unknown. Do not infer them or transfer a target [S7].
- **Operating guidance (S2–S4, S8).** Extension sources distinguish air exchange from internal circulation, describe seasonal heat/energy tradeoffs, and support checking sensor placement and fan condition. These are inspection and measurement cues, not a schedule for this house. Tomato humidity examples and winter HAF practice are not herb thresholds. Greenhouse measurement guidance supports recording sensor identity, position, calibration, interval, and independent checks [S8].

These comparisons make implementation history essential. A study in another crop or climate can suggest what to measure; it cannot determine the appropriate control behavior for the unknown teaching greenhouse.

## Operating-history reconstruction

Before interpreting a schedule change, retrieve and compare dated old/new irrigation and air-system settings, who changed them, why, and whether the equipment delivered the programmed operation.

1. **Irrigation:** document timer programs, event duration/frequency, water source, line/zone/emitter changes, pump and valve function, plant moves, hand watering, drainage, and media history. Compare programmed operation with measured representative delivery. Record water volume by zone where possible.
2. **Air system:** identify whether each changed control is internal circulation, exhaust ventilation, natural venting, or a combination. Recover controller/event and service/wiring records. Inspect fan direction, belts, blades, shutters, screens, dust/blockage, vent positions, and measured airflow. A scheduled “on” event does not establish actual air movement [S3, S4].
3. **Sensors and weather:** record sensor make/model, exact position relative to crop/door/fan/vent/intake, calibration and independent-check dates, sampling interval, missing data, control strategy, and outside weather/light. Greenhouse spatial and temporal variation makes location and calibration part of the evidence [S8].
4. **Crop and practice:** record species/cultivar, sowing/transplant date, age/size, containers, substrate/lot, spacing, bench moves, other management changes, and seasonal conditions. Keep study differences in crop, substrate, climate, controls, and duration visible whenever evidence is transferred.

This is a proposed records review and equipment inspection. No local record was supplied or recovered for this proposal.

## Proposed baseline and later limited comparison

### Baseline

A practical proposal is 7–14 days spanning several representative dawns and irrigation cycles. This is a planning range, not a literature-validated minimum. Extend or reschedule if weather, crop stage, or the teaching cycle is unrepresentative. Preserve current routine during observation only if there is no separate plant-welfare reason to stop it.

- Draw a bench map and label plant/position, species/cultivar, crop age/size, pot/media, watering zone, and door proximity.
- Use a written, repeatable rubric for lower-leaf color/symptom extent, affected-plant count, leaf age/position, and growth. Photograph the same plants from a consistent view at fixed times.
- Record droplets by surface location, time of first appearance/disappearance, and relation to irrigation. Repeat observations before irrigation and after dawn.
- Measure separate outcomes: (a) visible symptoms, (b) plant growth such as repeatable height/canopy or leaf-count measures, (c) water applied and drained, and (d) any condition requiring diagnosis. Symptom reduction alone cannot identify a cause.
- If feasible, add calibrated substrate moisture/EC, air temperature/RH, outside weather, canopy or leaf-surface temperature, and direct leaf-wetness observations. Calculate dew point/VPD only when instrument quality supports it. Record calibration, location, interval, raw logs, missingness, and deviations.
- If the bench is too large for every plant, define a stratified sample across door/center/far positions and crop cohorts in advance. Treat the plant as the unit for plant-level comparisons, not each leaf or sensor reading. Graph repeated measures by time, position, cohort, and watering zone; align them with actual irrigation and fan/vent events.

### Conditional pilot

After baseline review, confirm the instructor's primary outcome and plant-welfare stop criteria. If verified delivery/root-zone variation is the signal, a later authorized pilot could compare one reversible, preapproved irrigation/delivery factor against current practice in matched randomized blocks while holding ventilation constant. Do not introduce deficit watering or a literature-derived setpoint. If microclimate or air movement is the signal, compare one reversible, locally safe circulation/ventilation factor in independently controlled comparable zones while holding irrigation constant. If the control is house-wide, use a documented exploratory time series matched as far as practical for outside conditions and crop stage; do not call it randomized causal evidence. If no signal is clear, continue observation or seek specialist advice rather than forcing an intervention.

Set stopping rules in advance with the instructor for worsening symptoms, wilting, decline, or other welfare concerns. No treatment, pesticide, fertilizer dose, or control target is proposed. Determine replication from actual bench size and baseline variability rather than inventing a sample count. A pilot estimates feasibility and variability; it does not prove yield improvement, disease prevention, or food safety.

## Specialist escalation and owner decisions

Seek a local greenhouse horticulture or plant-diagnostic specialist if symptoms spread or intensify, plants wilt/decline/die, lesions or root/stem signs appear, verified delivery and environmental records do not explain the pattern, or a diagnosis is needed before a teaching-protocol decision. Escalate earlier if plant welfare requires it. If sampling is authorized, follow the receiving clinic's current instructions. NC State's greenhouse sample page, for example, requests representative symptomatic material and distribution photos and specifies dry foliage with slightly moist media; this is local guidance, not a universal lab protocol [S9].

The instructor should decide before a pilot:
1. Is the primary decision plant welfare, water use, or teaching reliability? Name one primary outcome and secondary outcomes.
2. Which crop conditions and equipment must remain unchanged for teaching, and what reversible change is allowed?
3. What conditions require an immediate welfare stop or specialist call?
4. Who may access operating records, place sensors, and authorize sample submission?
5. What measurement burden, schedule disruption, and energy tradeoff are acceptable?
