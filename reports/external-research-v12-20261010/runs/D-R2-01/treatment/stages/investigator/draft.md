## Proposal in brief

Treat the reports as a set of observations to explain, not as evidence for one cause. Keep pale lower leaves, plant growth, water use, and any condition needing diagnosis as separate endpoints. Morning droplets could be dew/condensation, guttation, overhead irrigation or splash, or structural drips. Pale lower leaves may be age/cohort-related, environmental, root-zone, nutritional/salt, pest, or disease related. These are hypotheses only. Neither droplets nor symptom reduction identifies a cause.

The highest-value first step is a mapped, observation-first baseline that reconstructs the timer and fan history and measures the conditions plants actually experienced. Then use one reversible, limited comparison only if the baseline identifies a question, an independent comparison is possible, and the instructor approves. Do not change the teaching protocol on the anecdote. If symptoms progress or diagnosis would change a consequential decision, consult a plant diagnostic clinic or greenhouse specialist.

The instructor has not defined the decision objective. Plant welfare, water use, and teaching reliability may require different endpoints and stop rules. No preferred treatment or cause is selected.

## Operating and implementation history needed

Before any pilot, ask the instructor/staff to recover, label as documented or recollected, and date:

- Old and new irrigation settings: start time, duration, days, zones, controller clock, seasonal program, manual overrides, delivery method, water source, emitter/sprinkler maintenance, pressure changes, clogging, and line repairs.
- Old and new fan/vent/heating settings: equipment make/model and function, schedule, controller links/sensors, sensor placement, manual operation, maintenance or outages, vent/door behavior, and any changes in crop/teaching practice. Without the make/model, firmware, and manual, no device-specific default, fault history, or release/fix behavior can be verified.
- Crop changes: species/variety, seed/transplant date or age, pot size, substrate/batch, grouping, spacing, position changes, pruning/harvest, and staff/operator access or training.
- Environmental context: dates and timing of visible reports, seasonal/weather notes, glazing/shading, heat/cooling, sensor type/calibration/location, and missing records.

Comparable implementation studies show why this history matters, not what this greenhouse should do. In a two-year commercial floriculture comparison, grower-controlled sensor irrigation of poinsettia and geranium did not reduce water use; repeated threshold increases reflected historical timer-based expectations, while labor reallocation was valued [S09]. In a 2014–2015 commercial nursery trial, sensor irrigation reduced water overall but a woody crop experienced over 50% losses in one season; canopy architecture, averaged cross-species thresholds, unusually hot/dry conditions, salt buildup and management access were discussed as contributors [S10]. The crop, substrate, outdoor/greenhouse conditions, controls, season, and operator differ. A 2003 fan/pad pepper study and a 2017 evaporative-cooling sensor-network study also show that gradients depend on cooling mode and position [S19–S20]. None supplies a setting or expected effect for the unknown teaching greenhouse.

## Baseline, observations, and limited reversible pilot

### Baseline proposal

First decide with the instructor whether continuing the current safe routine is acceptable for a baseline. If there is an immediate welfare concern, seek specialist guidance rather than waiting to complete a baseline. A provisional 10–14-day baseline, or enough repeated morning cycles to sample ordinary weather and watering cycles, would make a trend more interpretable; the exact duration is an owner decision based on crop status, season, resources and time.

1. Draw a bench map with numbered positions, door, vents, fans, heat/cooling, control sensor, irrigation lines/zones and any nearby environmental boundary. Assign each pot an identifier.
2. For each plant, record species/variety if known, age/planting batch, pot/substrate, canopy size or stage, position, movement history and any harvest/pruning. Photograph the same tagged lower leaves and new growth with consistent framing and date.
3. At a consistent pre-irrigation morning time, record whether droplets are present, their leaf location/pattern, approximate drying time, leaf surface/underside appearance, and current irrigation/fan/vent/heat state. Repeat after irrigation and later as practical. A wetness sensor may measure duration but not identify the water source; validate its placement and output before relying on it. Do not turn a wetness reading into a diagnosis.
4. Log canopy air temperature and RH at more than one mapped position, including near the door and away from it, plus an outside reference if available. Record the sensor's location, shielding, calibration check and timestamp. If feasible, record leaf surface temperature and compare with dew point; also note weather and solar/light conditions. Do not invent missing readings.
5. Separately record the irrigation volume applied by zone and total water use, delivery uniformity/output, and representative pot mass after drainage and later. If measuring substrate moisture, record the sensor model and calibration against the actual crop/media. Record observed growth (e.g., consistent height/canopy/new growth measure), leaf symptom score, and any signs needing diagnosis as separate outcomes. Missing, uncertain or recollected history must be labeled.

Define simple repeatable symptom categories before scoring; for example, absent, mild, moderate or severe pale area on tagged lower leaves, plus number of affected tagged leaves and whether new growth changes. This is a proposed observation scale, not an existing prevalence estimate. Do not collapse it into one “plant health” score or use symptom improvement alone as evidence of cause.

### Limited pilot proposal

After baseline review, choose one question. Only proceed with instructor approval, a safe crop-protection plan, and a comparison that isolates the factor.

- **Irrigation question:** If separate irrigation zones or individually measured pots are possible, pair/block by bench position and cohort (species/age/pot/media), then randomly allocate matched units to the documented safe reference versus one modest, reversible change selected after consultation. Keep ventilation and other management stable. Verify application uniformity and root-ball wetting before interpreting symptoms. Record the exact actual water volume and all overrides. Without independent water delivery, use observational comparisons only.
- **Ventilation question:** Use separately controlled comparable compartments if present. Map and monitor both before making a reversible schedule comparison, and keep irrigation unchanged. If the entire greenhouse is a single ventilation zone, plants inside it are subsamples, not independent ventilation replicates. In that case, prefer observation or an approved time-series/reversal comparison across matched weather and crop-stage periods; report the result as limited because weather/time are confounded.
- **One-factor rule:** Do not simultaneously change irrigation and fan/vent schedules. A documented old routine can serve as a control only if its suitability and safety for the present crop and season have been checked.
- **Predefine endpoints and stopping:** Track leaf symptoms, plant growth, water use, and diagnosis-related signs independently. The instructor and specialist should set plant-welfare limits and a stop/escalation path before the pilot. Stop or refer for marked deterioration, wilting/collapse, rapid spread, root/crown concern, or other predefined concern; do not hold a stressed crop in a treatment for data completeness.
- **Interpretation:** Use the map and cohort blocks to separate bench position and crop age from treatment. Report missing observations, weather, exact control operation, any protocol deviations, and whether changes in a single zone permit causal attribution. A single uncontrolled before/after comparison can suggest a pattern but cannot confirm cause.

### Escalation

Request specialist diagnosis if the pattern progresses, plants collapse or wilt despite an apparently adequate root zone, roots/crowns show concerning changes, suspicious signs appear, the pattern spreads, or the instructor needs confirmation before a consequential crop protocol decision. Supply representative affected and comparison plants as requested by the local Extension diagnostic service; include roots/media if requested, and attach dated images, crop identity/age, map, substrate, irrigation/fan history, environmental records, and any observations. No pesticide, fertilizer dose, pathogen diagnosis, food-safety statement, disease-prevention claim, or yield claim is made here.
