# Research proposal: uneven leaf symptoms in a teaching greenhouse

Run: D-R2-01-treatment  
Case: ER12-D-R2-01-FRESH  
Stage: independent complete reviser  
Status: desk research and proposal only; no plant diagnosis, crop intervention, or crop validation

## Decision summary

The reported pale lower leaves, occasional morning droplets, door-position impression, and recent timer and circulation-fan schedule changes are observations, not a diagnosis or causal pattern. The symptoms and droplets may have different causes. Keep visible symptoms, plant growth, water use, and any condition needing specialist diagnosis as separate outcomes; symptom reduction alone would not identify a cause.

The lowest-disturbance first step is a mapped, repeated observation period while preserving the current safe operating state, provided the instructor or specialist considers waiting acceptable for plant welfare. Reconstruct the irrigation and fan histories and measure the conditions and delivery plants actually experience. Then choose one limited, reversible comparison only if the baseline identifies a useful question, a safe comparison is available, and the instructor approves it. Do not change the crop protocol based on this anecdote.

No treatment or cause is selected. The instructor still needs to decide whether the immediate priority is plant welfare, water use, or teaching reliability; these choices lead to different endpoints and stop rules.

## Case facts, unknowns, and plausible explanations

The case supplies no diagnostic samples, greenhouse logs, timer settings, fan type, bench map, sensor readings, treatment results, or measured symptom prevalence. Species and age mix, substrate, irrigation delivery, climate-control behavior, sensor placement, season, and plant movement are unknown. The door pattern is informal and could reflect position, cohort, light, handling, irrigation distribution, or an environmental gradient; it is not established as a real difference or cause.

| Observation or hypothesis | Mechanism and useful prediction to investigate | What the current evidence does and does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Morning droplets | Condensation can form when leaf surface temperature is below the surrounding air dew point. Guttation can exude liquid at leaf margins or tips when transpiration is low and root pressure moves water, under conditions such as humid/cool air with a warm moist root zone. Overhead irrigation, splash, or water from greenhouse structure are other possibilities. | Droplet position, timing, persistence, irrigation state, leaf and air temperatures, and photographs can help compare explanations, but appearance alone does not identify the source. Guttation and dew may resemble each other [S01–S03]. |
| Pale lower leaves | Normal age or cohort turnover, uneven root-zone water, temperature/light/airflow, substrate or soluble-salt/nutritional conditions, pests, or a disease may produce overlapping above-ground signs. | These are a differential for observation, not a diagnosis. Environmental and nutritional disorders can resemble infectious disease in herbs; no pathogen or deficiency is established [S12–S16]. |
| Door-proximity impression | A mapped position could coincide with a draft, temperature/light gradient, water-delivery difference, plant cohort, or none of these. | The informal impression should be tested with a bench map and repeated observations, not treated as a cause. Greenhouse studies found spatial patterns in their specific cooling configurations, which supports mapping but cannot predict this bench [S19–S20]. |
| Timer and fan schedule changes | Either change, an interaction, a seasonal/weather trend, plant movement, or a coincident crop-age change could explain a later observation. | The changes occurred before symptoms were noticed, but temporal order does not establish causation. Exact dates, settings, overrides, and other changes are needed. A later comparison should change one factor at a time. |

A basic leaf-wetness-duration (LWD) signal records surface wetness duration; that signal alone does not identify whether water came from dew, irrigation, or another source [CR02, S17]. A separate field study evaluated a learned classifier using wetness-sensor patterns together with temperature, relative humidity, duration, and other features to classify dew, rainfall, or irrigation [CR03]. That is a study-specific capability, not a property of a bare wetness logger, not validated for these herbs, and not a means shown here to identify guttation. S17 tested a particular tomato sensor and S18 calibrated a sensor against condensate on a flat surface; neither validates a sensor for this crop or identifies the source of the reported droplets [S17–S18].

## Operating history and evidence transfer

Before any pilot, ask staff to recover and label each item as documented or recollected, with dates where possible:

- Old and new irrigation start times, durations, days, zones, controller clock, seasonal programs, manual overrides, delivery method, water source, emitter/sprinkler maintenance, pressure changes, clogs, repairs, and total-water records.
- Old and new fan, vent, and heat schedules; equipment make/model and function; linked control sensors and their placement; manual operation; maintenance/outages; door and vent behavior; and changes in staff or teaching practice.
- Crop identity/variety, planting or transplant date, age, pot size, substrate/batch, irrigation grouping, spacing, movement, pruning/harvest, and operator access or training.
- Dates and timing of symptom reports, season/weather, glazing/shading, heating/cooling, sensor type and calibration/location, and missing records.

Comparable implementations explain why this history matters, but they do not predict this greenhouse:

- In a two-year commercial floriculture study conducted in 2014–2015 in two Georgia greenhouse bays, grower-controlled sensor irrigation on poinsettia and geranium grown in peat-perlite media did not reduce irrigation water use. The sensor-based system was compared with the operator's historical timer-based practice; staff repeatedly increased sensor thresholds, which the authors related to production preferences and limited understanding of how sensor control responded as plant water use changed. Managers valued labor reallocation. The crop, peat-perlite substrate, commercial Georgia greenhouse, season, and controls differ from this case's unknown herbs, medium, climate, and operation [S09].
- A commercial woody-nursery trial reported about 50% less irrigation overall, while a severe Rhododendron loss occurred in 2015. The accessible primary abstract associates the loss with averaged cross-species moisture thresholds, canopy structure obstructing water capture, and persistent drought in that block. The paper's discussion of EC/salt should not be restated as an established cause of the loss; the carried source record reports the EC values were below levels the authors considered typically crop-damaging. The fresh direct PDF request during reviser review returned 403, so the exact salt discussion is not independently re-read here. Woody crops, bark media, overhead capture, nursery controls, and a hot/dry season differ materially from this unknown herb bench [S10].
- The pepper fan-only versus fan-and-pad study measured vertical and other temperature/humidity gradients in its warm-climate greenhouse; the gradient patterns differed by cooling mode and ventilation rate. The Brazilian evaporative-cooling study mapped horizontal and vertical variability under fixed shade and different fan/pad/window configurations. These primary studies support mapping the actual fan/vent layout, not a prediction of this facility's gradient or the effect of changing its fan [S19–S20].
- UMass extension describes potential subirrigation water/fertilizer savings and reports growers observing more uniform growth and less foliar disease; runoff/leaching reduction assumes a truly closed system. It also warns of salt accumulation, the need for pH/EC and cleanliness practices, and that different pot sizes may require different zones. These are general greenhouse observations, not trial results for this bench [S11]. Separate inoculated ornamental studies found disease reduced under partial saturation in three of five experiments [CR04], and pathogen recovery/transmission from infested nutrient solution in two tested recirculating systems, with differing disease expression and inoculum-route results [CR05]. Together they show conditional outcomes, not a universal disease benefit or hazard and not a claim about these herbs.

The S09, S10, S19, and S20 studies differ in crop, substrate, climate, equipment, controls, season, and operator practices. Their results justify collecting implementation history; they do not supply a timer, sensor threshold, fan duration, watering interval, disease prediction, or expected effect for the teaching greenhouse.

## Proposed baseline and observation plan

First define with the instructor whether the primary decision concerns plant welfare, water use, or teaching reliability. Confirm that keeping the current routine during observation is safe; otherwise obtain specialist advice rather than waiting. A provisional 10–14-day baseline, or enough repeated morning cycles to span available watering and ordinary weather variation, is a design option rather than a biological threshold. The instructor should select duration based on crop status, season, time, and resources.

1. Draw a bench map with stable pot identifiers and the door, vents, fans, heating/cooling, control sensor, irrigation lines/zones, and relevant environmental boundaries.
2. Record species/variety if known, planting batch or age, pot/substrate, canopy stage, movement history, pruning/harvest, and position for each plant. Photograph the same tagged lower leaves and new growth with date and repeatable framing.
3. At a consistent pre-irrigation morning time, record whether droplets are present, where on the leaf, which plants, and how long they persist. Repeat after irrigation and later as practical. Note the irrigation, fan, vent, and heat state and weather. Droplet counts and drying times are new observations to collect, not known case values.
4. Measure air temperature and relative humidity at multiple mapped canopy locations, including near the door and away from it, and outside if available. Record sensor model, position, shielding, calibration/reference check, timestamp, and missing intervals. If feasible, measure leaf-surface temperature and compare with dew point. A wetness sensor can add duration data only after its placement and output are checked; a bare duration signal is not source identification.
5. Record applied water by zone or total meter, delivery uniformity/output, representative pot mass after drainage and later, and substrate-moisture readings only with model, placement, and crop/media-specific cross-check. Define a simple repeatable symptom scale before scoring, for example absent/mild/moderate/severe pallor on tagged lower leaves, affected-leaf counts, and new-growth changes. Separately record growth, water use, and signs that warrant diagnosis. Do not collapse these into one plant-health score or treat improvement alone as causal evidence.
6. Label uncertain, recollected, or missing history as such. Do not fill unmeasured humidity, moisture, temperature, prevalence, or treatment response from memory.

## Limited reversible pilot proposal

Only after reviewing the baseline, choose one question and get instructor approval, a safe plant-protection plan, and a feasible comparison. Keep endpoints and stop criteria separate.

- **Irrigation question:** If independent zones or individually measured pots are available, match/block by bench position and cohort (species, age, pot, and substrate); randomize matched units to the documented safe reference and one modest reversible change chosen after consultation. Keep ventilation and other management stable. Verify application uniformity and root-ball wetting; record actual volume and overrides. If water delivery cannot be independently compared, stay observational.
- **Ventilation question:** Use comparable compartments or zones only if the ventilation treatment can be assigned independently to them. The independently assigned ventilation zone is the experimental unit; plants within it are subsamples, not additional treatment replicates [CR01]. If the greenhouse is one ventilation zone, use observational monitoring or an approved reversible time-series comparison while keeping irrigation fixed; weather and time remain confounders, so report limited inference.
- **One-factor rule:** Do not change irrigation and fan/vent schedules at the same time. A previous routine can be a reference only after its safety and suitability under present crop and season are established.
- **Stop and escalation:** Agree on plant-welfare limits and stop conditions with the instructor/specialist in advance. Stop or refer for marked deterioration, wilting/collapse, rapid spread, root/crown concern, or other predefined concern. Do not keep stressed plants in a comparison to finish data collection.
- **Interpretation:** Use the map, cohort blocks, repeated timing, and documented control states to assess position, age, weather, and time trends. Report missing observations, protocol deviations, and whether treatment units were genuinely independent. A single uncontrolled before/after sequence can suggest a pattern but cannot confirm cause.
