## SCW-01 — S10: woody nursery sensor-irrigation study

- **Identity:** S10, Wheeler et al., “Implementation of Soil Moisture Sensor Based Automated Irrigation in Woody Ornamental Production,” Journal of Environmental Horticulture 38(1):1–7 (March 2020), DOI [10.24266/0738-2898-38.1.1](https://doi.org/10.24266/0738-2898-38.1.1). Official PDF: https://jeh.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/jenh/38/1/article-p1.pdf. Locator: abstract and discussion, printed pp. 1, 4–6.
- **Access:** Prior direct PDF text was retrieved in this stage; its exact timestamp is not present in the compacted access record. A fresh direct PDF request in the 07:18 UTC batch returned HTTP 403. S10's carried source-map access time is 2026-10-10T06:59:50Z.
- **Evidence:** The study reports lower aggregate irrigation and severe 2015 losses in one Rhododendron treatment. It discusses hot/dry drought, canopy interception, cross-species averaging, and implementation access. It also discusses elevated EC/salt; however, both treatment readings remained below 2.0 mS/cm and the authors say those levels are not usually sufficient to damage crops. They considered drought the main explanation for the mortality.
- **Condition/applicability:** Commercial woody nursery, particular crop/media, sensor/control setup, and unusually hot/dry season. EC discussion is not evidence that salt caused the reported losses; this is not an herb prediction.

## SCW-02 — S09: commercial floriculture sensor-irrigation comparison

- **Identity:** S09, “Implementation of Sensor-based Automated Irrigation in Commercial Floriculture Production: A Case Study,” HortTechnology 28(6):719–727 (December 2018), DOI [10.21273/HORTTECH04114-18](https://doi.org/10.21273/HORTTECH04114-18). Article text used: author-hosted full-text PDF https://www.publicgardens.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/19437714-horttechnology-implementation-sensor-based-automated-irrigation-commercial-floriculture.pdf; article discussion, PDF pp. 1, 4–6.
- **Access:** Full article was reviewed earlier in this stage via the author-hosted copy; exact fetch second is unavailable in the compacted record. Carried source-map access time 2026-10-10T06:59:50Z. DOI remains the version identity; the host is recorded as the access copy.
- **Evidence:** Across two years and commercial trials on poinsettia and geranium, sensor control did not reduce water use; growers repeatedly raised thresholds in line with timer-based expectations, and labor reallocation was valued.
- **Condition/applicability:** These implementation outcomes concern the named crops, commercial operation, and grower-controlled sensor setup; transfer to unknown herbs is unresolved.

## SCW-03 — S11: greenhouse subirrigation guidance

- **Identity:** S11, UMass Extension, “Subirrigation for Greenhouse Crops,” https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/greenhouse-floriculture/fact-sheets/subirrigation-for-greenhouse-crops. Retrieved page ©2025; no publication/update date shown. Locator: “Advantages” and “Challenges,” rendered lines 20–38.
- **Access:** Official page reopened 2026-10-10 07:18:12–07:18:18 UTC (batch window; individual second not exposed).
- **Evidence:** The page describes potential water/fertilizer savings when solution is recirculated; growers report more uniform growth and less foliar disease. It says lower leaching/runoff requires a truly closed system, salt accumulation calls for EC checks, cleanliness matters, and differing container sizes can require separate zones.
- **Condition/applicability:** General greenhouse-crop guidance with ornamental examples, not a measured result for this teaching bench. The draft's qualifier “may” is appropriate; local crop/system transfer remains untested.

## SCW-04 — S19 and S20: greenhouse gradient studies

- **S19 identity:** Teitel, Barak, and Zhao, “Temperature and Humidity Gradients in Fan Ventilated Greenhouses under Two Cooling Modes,” Acta Horticulturae 614, article 614_70 (2003), DOI [10.17660/ActaHortic.2003.614.70](https://doi.org/10.17660/ActaHortic.2003.614.70). Official ISHS abstract, fan-only versus fan-and-pad pepper greenhouse, two ventilation rates, horizontal/vertical gradients. Direct primary page/PDF result was available earlier in this stage; exact access second not present in compacted record.
- **S20 identity:** Zorzeto and Leal, “Wireless sensor network to map the meteorological variability in a greenhouse with evaporative cooling,” Acta Horticulturae 1154, article 1154_28 (2017), DOI [10.17660/ActaHortic.2017.1154.28](https://doi.org/10.17660/ActaHortic.2017.1154.28). Official ISHS abstract: https://www.actahort.org/books/1154/1154_28.htm, lines 16–24; reopened in the 07:18 UTC batch.

- **Condition/applicability:** These are configuration- and site-specific studies. They support mapping actual conditions, not predicting this greenhouse's gradients or effect sizes.

## SCW-05 — CR01: treatment units and pseudoreplication

- **Identity:** CR01, Hurlbert, “Pseudoreplication and the Design of Ecological Field Experiments,” Ecological Monographs 54(2):187–211 (1984), DOI [10.2307/1942661](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942661). Publisher record/abstract and experimental-design definition; stage access window 2026-10-10 07:18:12–07:18:18 UTC.
- **Evidence:** Hurlbert distinguishes unreplicated treatment units from samples within those units; observations within a treated unit do not become independent treatment replicates merely by increasing plant count.
- **Condition/applicability:** The unit is whatever is independently assigned the ventilation treatment. A greenhouse-wide single zone yields one unit; independently assignable zones can be units, with plants nested as subsamples. Site topology and actual assignment remain unknown.

- **S18 identity:** Han and Guo, “Development of a method for condensation rate measurement on flat surfaces,” Information Processing in Agriculture 5(4) (December 2018):490–497, DOI [10.1016/j.inpa.2018.06.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.inpa.2018.06.003). Publisher abstract reports chamber calibration with known condensate on a flat sensor surface; carried access 2026-10-10T06:59:50Z; fresh direct request returned 403.
- **CR02 identity:** UF/IFAS AE538, “How to Avoid Common Problems with Leaf Wetness Sensor Installation and Maintenance,” https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/AE538. Official page reopened 2026-10-10 07:18:12–07:18:18 UTC, rendered lines 17–33. It defines wetness duration and notes rainfall, dew, and overhead irrigation as possible sources; common sensors respond to surface resistance/impedance or dielectric changes.
- **CR03 identity:** Saini et al., “Identifying the Source of Water on Plant Using the Leaf Wetness Sensor and via Deep Learning-Based Ensemble Method,” IEEE Sensors Journal 24(5):7009–7017 (March 2024), DOI [10.1109/JSEN.2023.3343574](https://doi.org/10.1109/JSEN.2023.3343574), https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10381613. Official IEEE abstract/search record only; direct article text was not retrieved.
- **Evidence:** Basic wetness output records a surface-wetness signal/duration and does not label the source. A separate study describes source classification using LWS pattern data plus environmental features and a trained model; that is a conditional research result, not a capability of a basic logger by itself.
- **Condition/applicability:** Sensor model, mounting, calibration, species/surface and classifier inputs matter. None validates source identification on unknown herbs or distinguishes guttation in this facility.

## Ordinary-review additions

### CR04 — disease outcomes depend on inoculum and subirrigation conditions

- **Identity:** Elmer, Gent, and McAvoy, “Partial saturation under ebb and flow irrigation suppresses Pythium root rot of ornamentals,” Crop Protection (March 2012):29–33, DOI [10.1016/j.cropro.2011.10.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cropro.2011.10.010), ScienceDirect article record. Abstract-level evidence, retrieved 2026-10-10 07:18 UTC.
- **Evidence and condition:** Five experiments with artificially inoculated poinsettia, geranium, or chrysanthemum found significantly reduced disease in three experiments under partial saturation. This supports condition-specific disease outcomes, not a general disease-prevention claim or herb recommendation.

### CR05 — pathogen transmission in contaminated recirculating solution

- **Identity:** Oh and Son, “Phytophthora nicotianae transmission and growth of potted kalanchoe in two recirculating subirrigation systems,” Scientia Horticulturae 119(1) (2008-12-10):75–78, DOI [10.1016/j.scienta.2008.07.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scienta.2008.07.026), ScienceDirect publisher abstract at https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304423808002793. Abstract retrieved 2026-10-10 07:18 UTC; direct publisher page request returned 403.
- **Evidence and condition:** Inoculated nutrient solution resulted in pathogen recovery from roots in both tested recirculating systems, but symptom expression differed; inoculated medium did not transmit to adjacent plants in one wick system. This supports qualifying possible movement to contaminated solution and system, not a universal disease-movement or disease-prevention statement.
