## Working frame

The observation is underdetermined. Pale lower leaves and occasional morning droplets are separate observations, and neither establishes a shared cause. Droplets could be dew or condensation on cool leaves, guttation from leaf margins or tips under low transpiration and root pressure, irrigation splash or overhead delivery, or water dripping from structure. Droplet position and persistence, irrigation timing, leaf and air temperatures, canopy humidity, and photographs may help distinguish hypotheses, but appearance alone cannot. Guttation may occur under high humidity, cool air, and warm moist root zones; condensation occurs when a surface falls below the surrounding air's dew point [S01–S03]. A wetness sensor measures wetness, not its source, and requires sensor-specific validation [S17–S18].

Pale lower foliage could reflect normal age-related turnover, plant-specific water/root-zone stress, temperature/light or airflow differences, soluble-salt/nutritional issues, pests, or disease. Herb extension sources warn that environmental and nutritional disorders can resemble infectious disease [S12, S15]. This list is a differential to investigate, not a diagnosis. No pathogen, deficiency, food-safety state, or production loss is established.

Door proximity is an observation worth mapping, not an explanation. The door may coincide with a temperature or draft gradient, altered light, handling, or water delivery, but none is known. Plant species, age, canopy, pot size, substrate, and placement can alter water demand and overhead capture [S05, S10]. The timer and fan schedules changed together, so a before/after comparison cannot attribute an effect to either change. The exact old/new settings, change dates, reason, override/access history, fan function (horizontal circulation versus exhaust/ventilation), and any temperature-control link are high-priority records.

## Competing non-chemical investigation paths

1. **Observation-first reference.** Preserve the current safe operating state while documenting a mapped baseline, unless the instructor or a specialist judges plant welfare requires immediate action. Record each plant's species/variety if known, age or planting batch, pot/substrate, coordinates, symptom score, and dated photographs. Each day note dawn conditions and whether droplets are present before and after irrigation, their leaf location, how long they persist, and which leaves/plants are affected. Record new lower-leaf symptoms separately from whole-plant growth/condition, water use, and any signs warranting diagnosis. This is the lowest-disturbance comparator and protects against treating an anecdote as a result.

2. **Irrigation/substrate inquiry.** Recover timer start times, duration, zone, and actual delivery type; measure total applied water, pressure, and output. For overhead sprinklers, a mapped catch-can grid can reveal position-dependent application and changed/clogged components; for emitters, measure flow/pressure at representative near/far positions [S06–S07]. Inspect representative pots after irrigation to see whether the root ball is wetted, and weigh matched containers after drainage and later in the day to estimate water loss. If using a moisture probe, verify it against direct pot-weight/substrate observations in the actual mix and locate it in the active root zone [S04–S05, S08]. Do not adjust the whole bench based on an unverified sensor or droplet pattern.

3. **Ventilation/microclimate inquiry.** Identify whether the changed fan recirculates air, exhausts greenhouse air, runs with vents, or is tied to a thermostat/humidistat; these functions differ. Map temperature and relative humidity at canopy height near the door, middle, and far side, plus outside; log the control sensor location and operation state. Add leaf-surface temperature/dew-point or a validated leaf-wetness indicator if available. HAF mixing can reduce local temperature differences; ventilation/exhaust replaces moist air, but outside air, heat demand, season, and crop constraints determine whether exchange helps. Fan operation alone does not prove humidity was removed [S02–S04, S15]. One room-level sensor may miss a spatial gradient [S04].

4. **Alternative delivery for later comparison.** Subirrigation can keep foliage dry and may improve uniformity or reduce runoff, but it is a system change, not a diagnostic shortcut. Recirculation, pot-size compatibility, sanitation, pH/EC monitoring, salt accumulation, and pathogen movement need attention; transfer from ornamental bedding crops to unknown herbs and media is limited [S09–S12]. Do not install or switch systems in this assignment.

## Transfer and implementation history

Timer schedules, sensor-based root-zone control, and fan programs depend on equipment function and who controls it. A commercial floriculture case found no water reduction under grower-controlled sensor irrigation because moisture thresholds were repeatedly raised in line with timer-based habits; the crops were geranium and poinsettia, so no savings were established for herbs [S09]. Another commercial nursery case reduced irrigation substantially overall, yet had severe losses in one woody crop when cross-species averages, canopy water capture, drought conditions, and implementation responsibilities diverged [S10]. Those studies are not predictions for this bench. Together they show why crop grouping, setpoint history, sensor placement, canopy architecture, operator training/access, climate, and salt/leaching practices must be recorded before transferring a claimed benefit. Irrigation uniformity can also change as pressures, nozzles, clogging, and components age [S06].

The relevant evidence supports measurement and separation of hypotheses, not a universal fan duration, watering interval, moisture threshold, or treatment.

## Proposed baseline and reversible pilot

First recover the schedule-change history and map the bench, door, irrigation lines, fan/vents, sensors, and plants. Use stable plant identifiers. For approximately 10–14 days, or long enough to observe multiple morning cycles under available weather, take observations at a consistent pre-irrigation morning time and again after irrigation/drying; log any other event immediately. Record air temperature and relative humidity at several mapped canopy locations and outside, irrigation/fan/vent/heating states, delivery volume or meter totals, wetness/droplet presence and duration, plant position/species/age/substrate, and a separate photo-based score for lower-leaf symptoms and new foliage. Count or score growth, water use, and plant condition as different outcomes. Record sensor model, placement, calibration/reference check, timestamps, and missing intervals. Do not invent values or fill missing history from memory without labeling it.

After baseline review and instructor approval, select one low-risk question and vary one factor at a time. For irrigation, use matched plants of the same species/age/pot/substrate in independently controllable zones, block by bench position, randomize within blocks, and keep ventilation unchanged. Verify delivery before interpreting plant response. For fan/vent effects, use independent comparable zones if they exist; otherwise keep the work observational or use a logged reversible time-series change with matched weather and no simultaneous irrigation change. A single greenhouse climate zone is one experimental unit for a fan intervention: many pots inside it do not create independent ventilation replicates. Predefine stop conditions for visible worsening, wilting, rapid spread, or crop-welfare concerns with a specialist; restore prior safe settings if the pilot threatens plants. Measure symptom trajectory, growth, water use, and wetness separately. Do not claim causality from one uncontrolled before/after sequence.

If symptoms advance, plants collapse, roots/crowns appear abnormal, signs suggest a transmissible problem, multiple plants become affected, or a consequential crop decision depends on the cause, consult the local Extension plant diagnostic clinic or greenhouse specialist. Provide representative affected and comparison plants as requested by that service, roots/media if requested, photographs, crop identities/ages, map, exact schedule history, substrate and irrigation details, and environmental records. Until then, diagnosis and treatment remain open.
